Introduction Breast cancer is the most common cancer among women in the United States (U.S.) [ 1 ]. It is estimated that there will be over 370,000 new cases of female breast cancer diagnosed in 2025 [ 2 ], of which 15–20% will be ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), a non-invasive form of breast cancer [ 3 ]. DCIS is characterized by abnormal proliferation of the epithelial cells confined to the ducts in the breast without invasion to surrounding tissue. DCIS is believed to be the precursor to invasive breast cancer; however not all DCIS will progress to invasive breast cancer. It has been estimated that between 25–60% of DCIS cases will progress to invasive breast cancer in the absence of treatment [ 4 ]. Risk factors for invasive breast cancer are well-established, however risk factors for DCIS are less well characterized and have been based primarily in cohort studies comprised of White women [ 5 – 17 ]. These studies reported that risk factors shared for both invasive breast cancer and DCIS include family history of breast cancer, obesity among postmenopausal women, older age at first live birth, older age at menopause, and menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) use, and that those found for DCIS only, include mammography screening and current smoking [ 5 , 7 , 9 , 12 – 20 ]. We report here on the shared and distinct risk factors for DCIS and invasive breast cancer identified among diverse racial and ethnic populations of the Multiethnic Cohort (MEC) study. Methods Study population This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the University of Hawaii and the University of Southern California. The MEC study is a prospective cohort designed to identify risk factors for cancer and other chronic diseases in a multiethnic population [ 21 ]. From 1993 through 1996, a baseline questionnaire was mailed to adults aged 45–75 years residing in Hawaii and California, predominately Los Angeles County. After the exclusion of women with prior history of breast cancer (DCIS and/or invasive) identified from the baseline questionnaire and/or through cancer registries, implausible dietary questionnaire data, and racial and ethnic groups outside the five targeted (n = 6,891), over 106,500 women were included in the present study to examine risk factors for DCIS and invasive breast cancer. The current analysis included 19.8% African American, 26.8% Japanese American, 22.4% Latino, 7.3% Native Hawaiian, and 23.7% White women. DCIS and invasive breast cancer ascertainment Incident breast cancer cases in the MEC were identified by linkage to the statewide Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results (SEER) cancer registries of Hawaii and California. DCIS cases were based on the first breast cancer diagnosis, International Classification of Diseases for Oncology third edition (ICD-O-3) site C500-C509, in situ behavior and histology codes (8500, 8501, 8503, 8504, 8507, 8509, and 8230). Incident invasive breast cancer cases were based on the first breast cancer diagnosis, ICD-O-3 site codes C500-C509, invasive behaviors, excluding histology codes: 9050–9055, 9140, and 9590–9992. Prior to 2018, SEER designated DCIS Grade on a four-level scale: well-differentiated, moderately differentiated, poorly differentiated, and undifferentiated (Grade I-IV). The majority ( 95%) of DCIS cases included in this study were diagnosed prior to 2018, therefore we have applied this scale for our analyses. The DCIS grading was not designated by a centralized review process. Clinical and pathologic data were retrospective and obtained from the SEER cancer registries in Hawaii and California, acknowledging that there may be inter-observer variability in grade determination across the DCIS cases. Deaths were identified via linkages with the National Death Index and death certificate files for Hawaii and California. Cancer and death ascertainment was complete through December 31, 2019. Exposure ascertainment Participants provided information on sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, medical history, and reproductive factors in the baseline questionnaire. Established breast cancer risk factors considered in this analysis included family history of breast cancer (no, yes), body mass index (BMI kg/m 2 ; 25 underweight/normal, 25–29.9 overweight, ≥ 30 obese), alcohol use in prior year (no, yes), age at menarche (≤ 12, 13–14, 14 yrs), age at first live birth (nulliparous, ≤ 20, 21–30, 30 yrs), number of children (0, 1, 2–3, ≥ 4), moderate/vigorous physical activity during the past year ( 30, ≥ 30 min per day), menopausal status (premenopausal, postmenopausal), age at menopause (pre-menopause, natural 45, natural 45–49, natural 50–54, natural ≥ 55 yrs, surgical menopause), and MHT use (never estrogen use, with or without past or current progesterone use, past estrogen use with or without past progesterone use, current estrogen use alone, current estrogen use with past or current progesterone use). Other factors considered included nativity (U.S. born, foreign born), marital status (married, never married/divorced/widowed), education (≤ high school, some college, college graduate, graduate or professional school), BMI at age 21 yrs ( 25 underweight/normal, ≥ 25 overweight/obese), smoking status (never, former, current), oral contraceptive use (ever, never), mammography (ever, never), and years since last mammography (no mammogram, 1, 1–2, ≥ 3 yrs). The baseline neighborhood socioeconomic status (nSES) index was developed from principal component analysis of 1990 U.S. census data that incorporated poverty, education, household income, rent, house value, employment, and occupation; nSES was parameterized as quintiles 1–5, 5 being the highest, based on Hawaii and Los Angeles County-specific distributions [ 22 ]. Baseline addresses of MEC participants were geocoded and linked to a census block group measure of nSES. In addition, information on diabetes was obtained from all MEC questionnaires 1–5 (QX1-QX5) and Medicare data, as previously described [ 23 ]. Statistical analysis Cox proportional hazards models of DCIS were used to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) with age as the time metric. Follow-up started at date of cohort entry and ended at the date of diagnosis of DCIS or invasive breast cancer, death, or December 31, 2019, whichever came first. Invasive breast cancer cases were censored at diagnosis age in the models of DCIS. Model 1 adjusted for race and ethnicity (African American, Japanese American, Latino, Native Hawaiian, White) as strata variables and age as a covariate. Model 2 further adjusted for factors previously found to be associated with invasive breast cancer risk in the MEC, published literature, and/or in the univariate analysis: family history of breast cancer, nativity, nSES, BMI, smoking status, age at first live birth, number of children, mammography history, menopausal status, MHT use, and Type 2 diabetes (T2D), which was modeled as a time varying exposure. Models accounted for clustering by block group by means of a robust variance estimator [ 24 ]. Missing values for categorical variables (0.4%-8.8%) were included as a separate category. Linear trends were evaluated by the Wald test of a trend variable assigned consecutive numbers (e.g., 1, 2, 3) representing the ordered categories of the exposure. All exposures met the proportional hazards assumption based on the test of interaction of time and the exposure after including race and ethnicity as a strata variable. Tests for heterogeneity of risk factor associations across race and ethnicity were based on the Wald test for cross-product interaction terms with the trend variables, where applicable. We repeated the models with the outcomes of low/intermediate (I-II) and high-grade (III-IV) DCIS. Cases with the DCIS not being modelled were censored at diagnosis age; that is, high-grade DCIS cases are censored in the model of low-grade DCIS. Breastfeeding history, which was obtained for over 43,000 women at the 10-year follow-up questionnaire, was modeled with follow-up time initiated at the date of the survey. To account for the potential sampling bias from using a subset of the MEC at baseline, the model included a strata variable for the propensity score for inclusion into the model, based on birth year, race and ethnicity, number of children, smoking status and BMI. Models were analyzed similarly for invasive breast cancer, with follow-up time starting at age at cohort entry and ending at date of diagnosis of invasive breast cancer, death, or study closure (end of December 2019). We conducted a competing risk analysis using cause-specific hazard models to evaluate time to diagnosis for DCIS and invasive breast cancer. To compare parameter estimates of DCIS and invasive breast cancer, we utilized the augmented data approach described by Lunn and McNeil [ 25 ], allowing for the simultaneous estimation of effects for both DCIS and invasive breast cancer. Statistical significance was defined as p 0.05. Data analyses were conducted using SAS, version 9.4 (Cary, NC). Results Among the 106,525 women that were included in this study, the majority had no family history of breast cancer, were U.S. born, had underweight/normal BMI at baseline and at age 21, were never-smokers, engaged in 30 or more minutes per day of moderate/vigorous exercise, did not have T2D, had menarche at age ≤ 12 yr, were parous, had first live birth at age 21–30 yr, had 2–3 children, had a mammogram less than 1 year prior to baseline, were postmenopausal, and had never used MHT (Table 1 ). When evaluating baseline characteristics among the five racial and ethnic groups included in the study, Native Hawaiian women were younger at cohort entry and had the largest proportion with a family history of breast cancer and with early menarche (age ≤ 12 yr). Latino women had the lowest proportion of a family history of breast cancer and highest proportion of non-U.S. born. Japanese American and White women were more likely to live in high SES neighborhoods (quintiles 4 and 5), and be current users of MHT. Japanese American women tended to be older at their first live birth ( 30 yrs) and White women were more likely to be nulliparous. African American women had the highest proportions of surgical menopause. Obesity and current smoking status were more common among African American and Native Hawaiian women. Latino and African American women were less likely to report 30 min of moderate or vigorous exercise per day. The prevalence of T2D was higher among African American, Native Hawaiian, and Latino women. Native Hawaiian and Latino women had lower proportions of mammogram screenings and the highest proportion of parous women. MHT, menopausal hormone therapy; E, estrogen; P, progesterone A total of 1,485 incident DCIS cases were identified during a mean follow-up of 20.1 ± 7.5 yrs. The mean age of DCIS diagnosis was 70.5 yrs and the overall age-adjusted incidence rate per 100,000 was 62.6 (adjusted to the U.S. 2000 standard, truncated at 45 yrs). There were racial and ethnic differences in incidence rates of DCIS, with the highest incidence observed among Japanese American (89.4) women, followed by Native Hawaiian (75.7), African American (63.3), White (49.3), and Latino (37.4) women. The majority of DCIS tumors were of well-differentiated and moderately-differentiated grade (43%). There were 7,695 incident invasive breast cancer cases identified during the same follow-up period. The mean age of invasive breast cancer was 70.8 yrs and the overall age-adjusted (truncated at 45 yrs) incident rate per 100,000 was 306.4, with highest rates among Native Hawaiian (429.3), followed by African American (363.8), Japanese American (349.9), White (296.9) and Latino (207.9) women (Table 2 ). The associations with DCIS were similar across race and ethnic groups for the majority of risk factors (Table 4 ). However, an increased risk of DCIS with current smoking was observed only among Native Hawaiian women (p-het = 0.05), but not for the other race and ethnic groups. Grade I-II and Grade III-IV DCIS tumors were significantly associated with a history of T2D (HR 1.40, 95% CI 1.11–1.76; HR 1.47, 95% CI 1.13–1.90, respectively). Older age at first live birth ( 30 yrs) was associated with risk of both Grade I-II (HR 1.62, 95% CI 1.18–2.21) and Grade III-IV (HR 1.48, 95% CI 1.04–2.11) DCIS tumors. Similarly, having a mammography was associated with increased risk for Grade I-II DCIS (HR 2.21, 95% CI 1.50–3.25) but not Grade III-IV DCIS (HR 1.26, 95% CI 0.89–1.79). Risk factors were also evaluated for invasive breast cancer, providing a comparison of factors that contribute to risk of DCIS and/or invasive breast cancer (Table 3 ). In the fully adjusted model, Native Hawaiian and Japanese American women were at increased risk of both DCIS and invasive breast cancer when compared to White women. African American women were at increased risk of DCIS, but not invasive breast cancer, and Latino women had a similar risk of DCIS and were at decreased risk of invasive breast cancer compared to White women. There were multiple shared risk factors that were positively associated with DCIS and invasive breast cancer, including family history of breast cancer, obesity, T2D, nulliparity, older age ( 30 yrs) at first birth, and current use of MHT. There were also shared inverse associations with DCIS and invasive breast cancer, including non-U.S. born and increasing number of children. Some risk factors differed for DCIS and invasive breast cancer either in the magnitude and/or direction of association. Although history of mammography was a significant risk factor for both DCIS (HR 1.82) and invasive breast cancer (HR 1.15), the HR estimate was stronger for DCIS (p-het 0.0001). Years since last mammogram ( 1 yr vs. ≥ 3 yrs) was associated with a decreased risk of identifying DCIS (HR 0.83) compared to invasive breast cancer (HR 1.00; p-het = 0.0002). Current use of estrogen alone was associated with an increased risk of DCIS (HR 1.18), but not invasive breast cancer (HR 1.00; p-het = 0.02). Current smoking status was associated with an increased risk for invasive breast cancer (HR 1.12), but not for DCIS (HR 0.88; p-het = 0.01). There was a marginally significant difference for the association with physical activity of ≥ 30 min/day (p-het 0.04), however the associations observed for DCIS (HR 1.07) and invasive breast cancer (HR 0.96) were not significant. Discussion This study investigated the role of established breast cancer risk factors and incident DCIS breast cancer in the MEC, adding new information on African American, Japanese American, Latino and Native Hawaiian women, understudied population groups that have been rarely included in previous DCIS studies. Factors such as family history of breast cancer, obesity, T2D, nulliparity, history of mammography, older age at first birth, and current use of MHT (estrogen alone and/or with progesterone) were associated with increased risk of DCIS, while risk of DCIS was reduced among non-U.S. born women and those with ≥ 2 children. We observed heterogeneity in the association between smoking with DCIS risk across race and ethnicity, with an increased risk limited to Native Hawaiian women (p = 0.05). However, the association was marginally significant and should be interpreted cautiously, warranting further investigation. We also found similarities and differences between the associations of breast cancer risk factors with DCIS and invasive breast cancer . Rates of DCIS and invasive breast cancer by race and ethnicity Ethnic differences in both DCIS and invasive breast cancer incidence may be attributed to differences in screening practices as well as underlying risk factors. We observed the age-adjusted and truncated incidence rate of DCIS was highest among Japanese American, followed by Native Hawaiian, African American, White, and Latino postmenopausal women. Prior studies, capturing DCIS rates between 1988–1999 and 1999–2004, have reported the highest incidence rates of DCIS rates among White women when compared to African American, Asian/Pacific Islander (API), American Indian/Alaska Native, and Hispanic women [ 26 , 27 ]. However, in a separate report covering 1990–2014, incidence rates of DCIS in the U.S., API women had the highest in rates, followed by African American, Hispanic and White women [ 28 ], reflecting similar incidence rankings that were observed in the current study. For invasive breast cancer, Native Hawaiian women were observed to have the highest incidence followed by African American, Japanese American, White, and Latino women. The higher risk of DCIS among African American (HR 1.49), Native Hawaiian (HR 1.50) and Japanese American (HR 1.66) women compared to White women that we observed in the MEC study, differed from prior published results. To our knowledge, three previous cohort studies (National Institutes of Health-American Association of Retired Persons Diet and Health Study (NIH-AARP) [ 14 ], M.D. Anderson Breast Cancer Management System ( BCMS ) [ 18 ] and United Kingdom (UK) Biobank [ 5 ] found largely null associations for risk of DCIS among non-White women compared to White women. In the NIH-AARP [ 14 ] and UK Biobank [ 5 ] study, risk of DCIS was marginally higher in non-White women compared to White women (HR 1.07, HR 1.06, respectively). The BCMS study reported no differences in DCIS among African American, Hispanic and API women compared to White women [ 18 ]. Limited number of non-White case numbers and also the aggregation of the different race and ethnic groups may have contributed to these null findings. In contrast to these prior cohort studies, the current study consisting of 1,199 non-White DCIS cases (296 African American, 117 Native Hawaiian, 532 Japanese American, and 254 Latino women), offers a large racial and ethnic representation of non-White populations, allowing estimates of DCIS risk across distinct groups. These findings underscore the importance of disaggregating racial and ethnic groups, unmasking the higher burden of DCIS experienced by underrepresented minority populations in the U.S. Risk factors Our findings of risk factors are largely consistent with published cohort studies of DCIS, which show increased risk among women with a family history of breast cancer [ 5 , 7 , 14 , 16 , 18 ], older age (≥ 30 yrs) at first live birth [ 5 , 7 , 13 , 14 , 16 , 17 ], current use of MHT [ 14 – 16 ], and obesity [ 5 ], and reduced risk associated with high parity ( 2 children) [ 5 , 9 , 13 , 16 , 17 ]. Our null findings for education, smoking status, physical activity, alcohol use, age at menarche, age at menopause, and oral contraceptive use align with prior cohort studies [ 5 , 7 – 10 , 12 , 14 , 16 , 29 ], supporting the consistency across studies. Similar to our null findings for physical activity, the WHI study also reported non-significant results for DCIS [ 11 ]. In contrast, other cohorts have suggested a protective effect. For example, the UK Biobank found a significantly decreased risk for the highest versus lowest tertile of physical activity (HR 0.84), and the Women’s CARE Study reported a 35% lower risk [ 5 , 30 ]. However, we note that both studies included combined populations of pre- and post-menopausal women, whereas our analysis (and the WHI) focused on post-menopausal women. In addition, mammographic screening frequency is a critical potential confounder, as most DCIS is detected during mammographic screening. Recent literature has demonstrated an association between higher physical activity levels and increased utilization of mammographic screening [ 31 ]. In contrast to our null findings for older age at menarche, results from the UK Biobank reported on a positive association for older age at menarche (12–13 yrs vs. ≤ 11 yrs) [ 5 ]. These divergent results, may in part be explained by differences in variable categorization, study population, and/or sample size. We observed common risk factors that were shared between DCIS and invasive breast cancer, including increased risk for having a family history of breast cancer, being U.S. born, overweight/obese, T2D, parity, older age at first live birth ( 30 yrs), mammography screening, and MHT use (current estrogen with progesterone). However, a subset of risk factors also showed differences. For example, in the present study, current smoking was a risk factor for invasive breast cancer (HR 1.12) but not DCIS (HR 0.88; p-het = 0.003), which is consistent with findings from the WHI RCT [ 10 ]. Overweight and obesity was associated with an increased risk for both DCIS and invasive breast cancer, however both overweight and obesity were more strongly associated with invasive breast cancer than DCIS (p-het = 0.01). There also differences in the strength of association with the number of children the women had for risk of DCIS and invasive breast cancer (p-het = 0.04). Having 2 children was significantly associated with reduced risk of DCIS but not for invasive breast cancer, and having 4 children was significantly associated with both. Use of MHT (current estrogen alone) was observed to be significantly associated with increased risk of DCIS (HR 1.18), but not for invasive breast cancer. Our results align with a previous meta-analysis which reported a statistically significant higher risk of DCIS with estrogen-only therapy (RR = 1.25, 95% CI = 1.01–1.55) [ 32 ]. However, we acknowledge the complexity of the existing literature, as other studies have not found a significant association between estrogen use and DCIS risk [ 33 , 34 ]. Conversely, our findings for MHT (current estrogen with progesterone) was significantly associated with both DCIS (HR 1.19) and invasive breast cancer (HR 1.35), align more consistently with previous reports [ 32 – 35 ]. We observed significant differences in associations with physical activity (p-het 0.02), with an increased risk for DCIS (HR 1.07) and decreased risk for invasive breast cancer (HR 0.96). Findings from the Cancer Prevention Study-II Nutrition Cohort (CPS-II) were directionally similar but not statistically significant when physical activity, measured in metabolic equivalents (METs), was compared with no activity. Specifically, for 0–8.75 METs, the HRs were 0.85 (95% CI: 0.52–1.25) for low-grade DCIS and 1.09 (95% CI: 0.91–1.31) for low-grade invasive breast cancer (p-het 0.25) [ 29 ]. Inverse associations between physical activity and breast cancer risk have also been reported in two meta-analysis of 31 and 38 prospective studies including more than 63,000 and 116,000 breast cancer cases, respectively, with corresponding pooled relative risks of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.85–0.91) [ 36 ] and 0.88 (95% CI: 0.85–0.90) [ 37 ]. We found that the risk of DCIS smoking was significantly elevated among Native Hawaiian women, indicating that the effect may be present for some subgroups. To our knowledge, U.S. birthplace, BMI at age 21, marital status, and nSES as risk factors for DCIS have not been previously studied in cohort studies. However, our findings largely agree with the past results for invasive breast cancer for these factors. The lower risk of DCIS and invasive breast cancer that we observed mirrors that of well documented migration studies, where U.S. born Latinos have higher invasive breast cancer incidence compared to non-U.S. born [ 38 ]. U.S. born Asian women also have higher invasive breast cancer rates than non-U.S. born Asians [ 39 ]. The null finding for higher BMI at age 21 yrs with DCIS contrasts with its association with decreased invasive breast cancer risk in the MEC (HR 0.77) and published literature on BMI at age 18 yrs [ 40 – 42 ] from a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational cohort studies [ 43 ]. We found an increased risk with nSES for both DCIS and invasive breast cancer, although the association was weaker for DCIS. Our findings for nSES agree with a systematic review and meta-analysis that found living in urban and higher SES areas was associated with higher breast cancer incidence [ 44 ]. Our study found no statistically significant association between breastfeeding (no children breastfed vs. ≥ 1 children breastfed for at least a month) with the risk of DCIS among parous women, consistent with null finding from other cohort studies [ 8 , 9 , 13 ]. These results add to the body of evidence suggesting that breastfeeding may not influence DCIS risk, despite its protective effect for invasive breast cancer [ 45 ]. Overall, the associations with DCIS were largely similar across race and ethnic groups, with the exception of smoking status. An increased risk for DCIS with current smoking status (vs. never smoking) among Native Hawaiian women (HR 1.78) was not observed for the other race and ethnic groups in the study. Risk factor differences by Grade I-II vs. Grade III-IV DCIS DCIS is histologically heterogeneous. DCIS tumor grade is defined based on the degree of cellular differentiation, nuclear features (nuclear size and shape), mitotic rate, and the presence of necrosis. Although the natural history of DCIS is not fully understood, small low-grade lesions have been associated with lower risk of progressing to invasive carcinoma [ 46 ]. This understanding highlights the importance of stratifying analyses by grade to capture potential heterogeneity in risk factor associations. In our study, overweight was associated with a higher risk of Grade I-II DCIS (HR 1.33) but not Grade III-IV DCIS (HR 0.89) (p-het 0.02). In contrast, the CPS-II reported no significant differences in BMI by grade, with HRs of 0.99 for Grade I-II and 1.06 for Grade III-IV DCIS (p-het 0.45) [ 29 ]. Differences across studies may reflect variation in tumor grading and/or sample size of cases. Our study included 637 Grade I-II and 488 Grade III-IV DCIS cases. Puvanesarajah et al. used similar grade designations but with fewer cases (430 low grade and 372 high grade) [ 29 ], whereas Kabat et al. defined high grade DCIS using ICD code 8501 (n = 107) and classified all others as low/medium grade (n = 343). Strengths and limitations A major strength of the current study was the large cohort that includes a diverse set of racial and ethnic populations with differences in risk of DCIS. Other strengths include the inclusion of a wide range of detailed demographic and lifestyle exposures as well as the inclusion of diverse understudied non-White populations included in the MEC. Other notable strengths include the study’s extended follow-up time, detailed data on many breast cancer risk factors, and the prospective study design. Additionally, the use of statewide SEER cancer registries, coupled with low out-migration in the MEC, ensured nearly complete identification of DCIS and invasive breast cancer cases. We acknowledge some limitations should be considered. We were unable to account for information of other breast cancer risk factors such as benign breast disease and mammographic breast density. The majority of our population were postmenopausal women; therefore, our findings would not be generalizable to premenopausal women. Lastly, we did not have information of timing of mammography screening (interval vs screen-detected). Conclusion Overall, our findings, in all racial and ethnic groups in our multiethnic population, are generally consistent with other cohort studies mainly of White women, providing further epidemiologic evidence supporting possible shared and distinct risk factor associations with DCIS and invasive breast cancer. The associations with risk differences between DCIS and invasive breast cancer merit further examination. The identification of unique DCIS risk factors may help to elucidate the pathogenesis of early breast cancer including distinction of individuals at higher potential to progress to invasive breast cancer from those whose disease remain indolent. The refinement of risk profiles, combined with current clinical and pathologic measures, can contribute to improved management of DCIS including distinguishing patients for whom active surveillance and de-escalation of treatment is appropriate from those requiring more aggressive treatment, such as surgical and other treatments.
