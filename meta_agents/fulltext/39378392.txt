1. Introduction The number of people worldwide living with Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (AD/ADRD) increased from ~36 million around 2012 to 57 million in 2019 and this figure is projected to double by 2050. (Collaborators GBDDF. Estimation of the global prevalence of dementia in, 2019) This rapid increase poses significant challenges to healthcare systems and economics globally. Sociodemographic, lifestyle, and cardiometabolic conditions are well-established risk factors for AD/ADRD. ( Livingston et al., 2020 ; Park et al., 2024 ) However, the role of ambient air pollution in the etiology of AD/ADRD remains equivocal. ( Tsai et al., Oct 2019 ; Fu and Yung, 2020 ) A 2023 meta -analysis of 16 studies found that exposure to fine particulate matter (PM 2.5 ) was associated with increased dementia risk (hazard ratio (HR) per 2 μg/m 3 =1.04, 95% CI 0.99–1.09), with substantially stronger associations in a subset of nine studies using active case ascertainment to diagnose dementia (HR=1.42, 95% CI 1.00–2.02). Risk increased in association with exposure to nitrogen dioxide (NO 2 ) and nitrogen oxide (NOx) but no association was observed with ozone (O 3 ) exposure. ( Wilker et al., 2023 ) An updated 2025 meta -analysis strengthened the overall evidence, showing a significant association between dementia incidence and exposure to PM 2.5 , NO 2 , and also PM 10 . ( Best Rogowski et al., Jul 2025 ) However, studies included in these meta -analyses were conducted among predominantly White populations in the US ( Ilango et al., Jun 2023 ; Shaffer et al., Aug 2021 ; Sullivan et al., Aug 2021 ; Shi et al., 2023 ; Yu et al., Mar 2023 ; Zhang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022 ) [two studies included Latino, ( Yu et al., Mar 2023 ; Zhang et al., 2023) three studies included African American ( Shi et al., 2023 ; Zhang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022 ), Canada, ( Chen et al., Nov 2017 ; Smargiassi et al., 2020 ; Yuchi et al., 2020 ; Ilango et al., 2020 ) United Kingdom, (Wood, 2024; Zhang et al., 2022 ; Carey et al., 2018 ) Europe, ( Mortamais et al., Mar 2021 ; Grande et al., 2020 ; Andersson et al., 2023 ; de Crom et al., 2023 ) and Australia, ( Bagheri et al., 2021 ; Trevenen et al., Dec 2022 ) with only two studies from Asia. ( Ran et al., 2021 ; Yan et al., 2022 ) Thus, while these meta -analyses investigated potential differences by geography, no information could be presented by race and ethnicity and had limited generalizability. The incidence of ADRD in the US is 1.5 to 2 times higher among African American and Latino populations than US White populations, which may be related, in part, to their higher prevalence of established risk factors including cardiometabolic conditions (e.g., heart disease, stroke, hypertension, diabetes), low education, low neighborhood socioeconomic status (nSES), not married, current smoking, low physical activity, obesity, poor diet quality and poor sleep. ( Park et al., 2024 ) In addition, minoritized population groups are more likely to live in neighborhoods of low SES, near sources of air pollutants due to decades of racial and ethnic segregation and may have greater susceptibility to the health effects of air pollution. ( Hajat et al., Dec 2015 ; Woo et al., Mar 2019 ) The geographical, social, and demographic imbalance of published studies highlight a knowledge gap of the role of air pollution on ADRD incidence across racially and ethnically diverse populations. ( Livingston et al., 2020 ) We address these unanswered questions by examining the role of ambient air pollution and incidence of ADRD using data from California participants in the Multiethnic Cohort (MEC) Study (over 70% African Americans and Latino participants) enrolled in Medicare Fee-for-Service (FFS). We report here the role of long-term (2001–2016) exposure to time-varying particulate matter (PM 2.5 , PM 10 , airport-related ultrafine particles (aUFP), 100 nm in diameter) and gaseous pollutants (NO 2 , a common traffic-related pollutant, NOx, carbon monoxide, ozone (O 3 )) in relation to incidence of ADRD with adjustment for sociodemographic, lifestyle, and cardiometabolic risk factors. These specific air pollutants were selected based on their prevalence in urban environments and their potential to cause neurological harm. ( Calderon-Garciduenas et al., Feb 2008 ; Oberdorster et al., Jun 2004 ; Hussain et al., Nov 2023 ) Both PM 2.5 and UFP can penetrate deeply into the respiratory system, cross the blood–brain barrier, and trigger adverse neurological effects. This 16-year exposure period aligns with the extended latency of ADRD development, allowing for a robust assessment of long-term air pollution effects. 2. Materials and Methods 2.1. Study Participants The MEC was established in 1993–1996 with a population-based sample of more than 215,000 men and women, living in Hawaii or California (largely Los Angeles County) who completed a 26-page questionnaire at enrollment that assessed sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., race and ethnicity, education, marital status), lifestyle factors (e.g., body weight, height, smoking, alcohol, physical activity, diet), personal medical history and other factors. ( Kolonel et al., 2000 ) The cohort has been linked to the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) claims data for 1999–2016 ( Setiawan et al., 2015 ) to identify chronic disease outcomes including ADRD based on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes included in the claims, in addition to linkage to Hawaii and California death files and the National Death Index for vital status. However participants enrolled in managed care (i.e., Medicare Part C) were excluded because their medical services were not directly billed to Medicare. ( Setiawan et al., 2015 ) As in previous studies of late onset ADRD in the MEC, ( Park et al., 2024 ; Lim U, Wang S, Park SY, et al. Risk of Alzheimer’s disease and related dementia by sex and race/ethnicity: The Multiethnic Cohort Study. Alzheimers Dement. Dec 9, 2021 ) this analysis included Medicare FFS beneficiaries with available claims data (n=106,080) among the five racial and ethnic groups (African American, Japanese American, Latino, Native Hawaiian, and White). Because the availability of EPA air pollution monitor data are sparse in Hawaii, we have developed measures of criteria air pollutants only for the California component of the MEC and thus FFS Hawaii MEC participants (n=57,512) were excluded. Of the 48,568 FFS California MEC participants, 44,954 remained in the current analysis ( Fig. 1 ), after excluding 3,164 participants (598 had 1 month of follow-up after Medicare surveillance start in 1999 or at a later date, 2,064 exited MEC cohort before 2001, 876 had incomplete address information, and 78 self-identified as Native Hawaiian). Supplement Table 1 showed that the excluded participants were more likely to be African American and less likely to be Japanese American participants, and also had a higher prevalence of established ADRD risk factors than those remaining in the analyses. 2.2. Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents The IRBs at the UH (CHS 9575) and the USC (HS-17-00714) approved the MEC study protocol and agreed that implicit consent was granted by participants who returned a completed baseline questionnaire. We adhere to the Data Use Agreement with CMS on our Limited Data Set use for research. 2.3. ADRD Ascertainment As in previous publications on late-onset ADRD in the MEC, ( Park et al., 2024 ; Lim U, Wang S, Park SY, et al. Risk of Alzheimer’s disease and related dementia by sex and race/ethnicity: The Multiethnic Cohort Study. Alzheimers Dement. Dec 9, 2021 ; Park et al., 2022 ) cases were identified during the Medicare linkage period based on the ICD code using CMS chronic conditions algorithm. We defined ADRD as AD or related dementia based on the definition of Medicare ( Taylor et al., Sep 2002 ) and methods described by Goodman and colleagues, ( Goodman et al., Jan 2017 ) and obtained the following ADRD subtypes: AD-only, AD of mixed etiology, other dementia including degenerative dementia not otherwise specified (NOS), vascular dementia (VD)-only, Lewy body dementia (LBD)-only, and frontotemporal dementia (FTD). We present here results separately for AD (included AD-only and AD of mixed etiology), other dementia (included dementia NOS, VD, LBD, and FTD) and ADRD (AD and other dementia combined). 2.4. Address History, Geocoding, and Neighborhood Socioeconomic Status (nSES) The MEC maintains up-to-date addresses on all participants by periodic mailings of newsletters, follow-up questionnaires, and linkages to administrative data and registries. These geocoded addresses were used to assess nSES, using a composite measure of seven census-based indicators (education, median household income, percent living 200% below poverty level, percent blue-collar workers, percent ≥16 years in workforce without job, median rent, and median house value). ( Yost et al., Oct 2001 ; Yang et al., 2014 ) The nSES index was assigned to participants’ census block group in California for all addresses from the time of Medicare enrollment to the end of study follow-up (i.e., ADRD diagnosis, death, or December 31, 2016); low and high nSES were defined as quintiles 1–3 and quintiles 4–5, respectively. 2.5. Air Pollution Exposure Assessment Time-dependent cumulative average air pollution exposure estimates were developed for 44,954 FFS California MEC participants, using information on month and year living at each of the residential addresses to generate the cumulative average air pollution value based on each month at the residence. Proportional values are assigned for partial months, such as in the start year/month, end year/month and the year/months when the individual moved. If the start date for the address is before or on the 15 th day of the month, the calendar month for the date was used to compute year/month value, and if the start date is after the 15 th day of the month, the preceding calendar month was used to compute year/month value. Similarly, if the end date for the address is before or on the 15 th day of the month, the previous calendar month was used to compute year/month value, and if the date is after the 15 th day of the month, the calendar month was used. As in our previous studies, ( Wu et al., 2025 ; Bookstein et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2021 ; Cheng et al., 2022 ) estimates of satellite-based PM 2.5 concentrations (2001–2016) were obtained from a fine-resolution spatiotemporal model that provided validated and publicly available monthly PM 2.5 outputs at a 1-km resolution over North America by statistically fusing chemical transport model (GEOS-Chem) predictions and satellite observations of aerosol optical depth with ground-based observations. ( Meng et al., 2019 ) Monthly ultrafine particle concentrations (UFP) from Los Angeles International Airport (LAX) (referred to hereafter as airport-related UFP, or aUFP) emissions were estimated at a 1-km resolution using a recommended American Meteorological Society/Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Regulatory Dispersion Model (AERMOD), which accounted for variations in airport flight activity and meteorology. ( Hudda et al., 2014 ) Concentrations of PM 10 and gaseous traffic-related pollutants (NO 2 , NOx, CO) and O 3 were assessed using monitoring data routinely collected by the U.S. EPA at participants’ addresses, applying empirical Bayesian kriging interpolation. California maintains one of the most extensive air monitoring networks in the world, with approximately 250 regulatory monitoring sites statewide (California Air Resources Board, 2023), including 35 permanent stations for criteria air pollutants in the Los Angeles Basin where the majority of MEC participants reside (South Coast AQMD, 2023). Among the 44,954 participants who remained in our analysis for PM and gaseous pollutants, 91% had complete data and 9% had some imputed data (5% had 10% imputed, 1% had 10- 20% imputed, and 3% had 20- 50% imputed); the exposure levels of the last known addresses were used for the subsequent period missing an address and requiring imputation. ( Cheng et al., 2020 ) HR estimates including the 9% participants with imputed air pollution measures were similar to the HR estimates that excluded them (data not shown) and all subsequent analyses included participants with imputed data. For aUFP only, we restricted our analysis to 68% of total subjects (n=30,703) with any residential addresses within the exposure grid zone surrounding LAX, given that UFP measurements were only available for this geographic area. Baseline demographic and lifestyle characteristics of participants without UFP data (n=14,251) were similar to the 44,954 participants except a higher percent of White and a lower percent of Japanese American participants were excluded (Column C, Table S1 ). Tables S2a and Table S2b present the correlation matrices of these air pollutants by race and ethnicity. Results for PM 2.5 , aUFP and NO 2 are shown in the main tables and results for PM 10 , NOx, CO and O 3 in the supplementary tables , in part, because of the lower penetration into and beyond the respiratory system of PM 10 compared with PM 2.5 , the high correlations between NO 2 and NOx (R=0.95) and between CO and NO 2 /NOx (R= 0.82 to 0.87), and because O 3 as modeled here is a secondary pollutant. 2.6. Statistical Analysis. We estimated incidence of AD, other dementia, and the combined ADRD in relation to air pollution using Cox proportional hazard models with monthly time-varying air pollutant exposures. The exposure period began 2 years after January 1, 1999 when the first Medicare surveillance data providing the ADRD diagnosis were available for participants who were Medicare beneficiaries as of January 1999 or 2 years after Medicare enrollment for those who enrolled after 1999 to avoid immortal person time. Age in calendar months was used as the time variable and defined a series of risks based on month/year at identification of each AD, other dementia (index case). Each risk set consisted of all MEC California participants, who remained alive and uncensored at the age of diagnosis of cases of AD and other dementia. For each member of each risk set (including the index case), the independent variable for exposure was the average air pollutant exposure for the period starting from the time of Medicare surveillance start (month/year) up to the age of AD and other dementia diagnosis of the index case in each risk set based on their residential history. Analyses were performed for the combined outcome of AD and other dementia, the outcome of AD where other dementia event was censored at diagnosis and the outcome of other dementia where AD was censored at diagnosis. We first conducted a minimally adjusted model which included as strata variables age at Medicare surveillance start (single-year age categories), and Medicare usage (in-patient, out-patient) and adjusted for race and ethnicity and sex (Model A). We then conducted a fully adjusted model (Model B), including 12 sociodemographic, lifestyle, and cardiometabolic risk factors assessed at cohort entry in 1993–1996 (education, marital status, history of hypertension, stroke, diabetes, heart disease, smoking status, physical activity, sleep duration, body mass index (BMI), and diet quality) and nSES at Medicare entry and at censoring. The associated population attributable fraction for ADRD in relation to these 12 factors combined was 24.0% in men and 22.8% in women in the MEC. ( Park et al., 2024 ) For comparison of our results to the recent meta-analysis results, ( Wilker et al., 2023 ) we estimated HRs and 95% CI for a fixed unit increase of PM (per 2 μg/m 3 for PM 10 and PM 2.5 ), NO 2 (10 μg/m 3 or 5.32 ppb), NOx (10 μg/m 3 or 5.23 ppb), CO (100 μg/m 3 or 86.96 ppb), and O 3 (5 μg/m 3 or 2.55 ppb) that are comparable to the interquartile range (IQR) of the average exposures over follow-up in the MEC (2.73 μg/m 3 for PM 2.5 , 3.98 μg/m 3 for PM 10 and 5.29, 14.25, 134.4, and 2.99 in ppb units, respectively for NO 2 , NOx, CO, and O 3 ). For aUFP, we used the IQR of 4400 particle counts/cm 3 as we have done in previous studies. ( Bookstein et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2021 ) Deviations from proportionality were checked using an analysis of Schoenfeld residuals and we found no violation of this assumption. Results obtained using Fine-Gray model to account for competing risks were similar to those using Lunn-McNeil augmentation approach; ( Lunn and McNeil, Jun 1995 ) we showed results obtained using the latter approach where AD or other dementia parameter estimates were fit by a cause-specific model in a separate stratum. Parameter estimates for AD and other dementia were compared using the Wald test. We also conducted stratified analyses by sex, race and ethnicity. As air pollution is a complex mixture of correlated pollutants, we conducted co-pollutant and multipollutant models to examine the concurrent contribution of PM 2.5, NO 2 and aUFP exposures to ADRD incidence in all subjects and by race/ethnicity. Sensitivity analyses were performed to check the robustness of the results from the main models. We investigated whether there are residual differences because participants entered Medicare at different ages/calendar years by further adjusting for Medicare entry year. In addition, we evaluated whether the air pollution-ADRD associations may be modified by the 12 ADRD risk factors. We also explored the role of genotype information (APOE) that was available in a subset of participants. To test for heterogeneity in HRs by race and ethnicity and other subgroups used in stratified analyses, we evaluated an interaction term in the model for each pollutant with subgroup membership using a Wald statistic. All statistical tests were based on two-tailed probability and a significance level was set at alpha 0.05. Participants were followed from January 2001 or 2 years after Medicare entry to the diagnosis of ADRD, or death, or end of follow-up (December 31, 2016). Death was identified through linkages to the National Death Index and state death certificate files. Mean follow-up time was 9.5±4.3 years. All analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 statistical software (SAS Institute, Cary NC). 3. Results 3.1. Study population and characteristics This analysis included 44,954 California MEC Medicare-FFS participants, of whom 28.2% self-reported as African American, 13.6% Japanese American, 43.6% Latino American, and 14.6% White American. The mean summary score of the 12 sociodemographic (education, marital status, nSES), lifestyle (smoking, physical activity, sleep, BMI, and diet quality) and cardiometabolic factors (hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, and stroke) was highest among African American (4.0 ±1.9), followed by Latino (3.8±1.7), White (2.6±1.7) and was lowest among Japanese American (2.4±1.5) participants ( Table 1 ). Air pollutant levels differed by race and ethnicity, especially for aUFP. Relative to other racial and ethnic groups, levels of aUFP were almost doubled in African American participants and levels of PM 2.5 and NO 2 were 4% to 9% higher among Latino participants (all P’s 0.001). These patterns by race and ethnicity were observed in male and female MEC participants ( Table S3 ). 3.2. Single pollutant results In minimally adjusted models, incidence of ADRD in men and women combined was positively associated with exposure to PM 2.5 (HR 1.04, 95% CI 1.02–1.06), aUFP (HR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.05), and NO 2 (HR 1.09, 95% CI 1.06–1.12) ( Table 2 ). The respective HRs for AD were 1.07 (95% CI 1.04–1.09), 1.03 (95% CI 1.00–1.06), and 1.18 (95% CI 1.13–1.23) and the corresponding HRs for other dementia were 1.03 (95% CI 1.01–1.05), 1.03 (95% CI 1.01–1.06), and 1.06 (95% CI 1.02–1.10). In fully adjusted models for other dementia, all the 95% CI included the null, whereas the HRs for AD and ADRD associated with PM 2.5 and NO 2 remained statistically significant. HR estimates associated with these pollutants showed largely comparable associations by subtypes of dementia ( Table S4 ). Exposure to PM 10, NOx, and CO was significantly positively associated with increased AD and ADRD incidence but there was no association with O 3 exposure ( Table S5 ). In men and women combined, the magnitude of the HR estimates associated with PM 2.5 , PM 10 , NO 2 , NOx, and CO were significantly higher for AD than for other dementia in both minimally and fully adjusted models (P heterogeneity ranged from 0.01 for CO to 0.0001 for NO 2 ). HR estimates were comparable in females and males. All results we discuss below on ADRD are based on men and women combined. Table 3 shows the race/ethnic specific HR estimates for AD, other dementia, and ADRD. We found no evidence of significant heterogeneity in associations by race and ethnicity, but some suggestive differences in the magnitude of the effect estimates emerged. In minimally adjusted models, African American participants showed elevated HRs for ADRD in association with exposure to PM 2.5 (HR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.06), aUFP (HR 1.04, 95% CI 1.01–1.07), and NO 2 (HR 1.09, 95% CI 1.04–1.14). Although these pollutants conferred elevated risks of ADRD among Japanese American, Latino and White participants, statistically significant HRs were observed only with NO 2 among Latino (HR 1.10, 95% CI 1.04–1.16) and White (HR 1.08, 95% CI 1.01–1.16) participants. However, the fully adjusted HRs for ADRD with aUFP and NO 2 remained statistically significant only in African American. HRs were higher for AD than for other dementia across race/ethnicity; the P heterogeneity were ≤0.05 among African American participants. HR estimates associated with exposures to PM 10, NOx, and CO were statistically significant for AD and ADRD among African American participants but not among Japanese American, Latino, and White participants ( Table S6 ) in both minimally and fully adjusted models (all P heterogeneity 0.05). There was no association with O 3 exposure. 3.3. Co-pollutant and multi-pollutant results Data on PM 2.5 , NO 2 and aUFP were available on 30,703 MEC participants. This aUFP subgroup included slightly more African American (28.4%) and Japanese American (15.3%) and fewer White (12.8%) participants and also showed higher cumulative average levels of PM 2.5 (16.5 ± 2.1 ug/m 3 ) and NO 2 (43.4 ± 7.7 ug/m 3 ) ( Table 1 ) than the group of participants (n=14,251) without aUFP data (PM 2.5 13.3 ± 3.4 ug/m 3 , NO 2 35.6 ± 8.4 ug/m 3 ) ( Table S1 ). In this subgroup, the HRs for ADRD associated with PM 2.5 (1.11, 95% CI 1.06–1.16) and NO 2 (1.19, 95% CI 1.12–1.26) were higher than the respective HRs for all participants but the 95% CIs were overlapping ( Table 2 ). The ADRD-NO 2 association remained statistically significant when adjusting for PM 2.5 and aUFP in co- and multi-pollutant models. Similarly, the HR estimate for aUFP remained statistically significant when adjusting for PM 2.5 and NO 2 in co-pollutant models and multi-pollutant models. However, the HR associated with PM 2.5 weakened and the 95% CI included the null when adjusting for NO 2 but remained statistically significant when adjusting for aUFP in co-pollutant models. Both aUFP and exposures were statistically significantly associated with ADRD incidence in African American and Latino participants in multipollutant models including all three pollutants ( Table 4 ). These effect estimate patterns for co-pollutant and multipollutant models were largely observed for AD and other dementia ( Table S7 ). 3.4. Sensitivity analysis HR results associated with PM 2.5 , NO 2 and aUFP for AD, other dementia and ADRD were largely unchanged with the addition of Medicare entry year as a linear term, suggesting little residual differences related to Medicare entry at different ages and calendar years ( Table S8 ). We found little evidence for modification of PM 2.5 , NO 2 and aUFP and ADRD incidence effect measures by each of the sociodemographic, lifestyle and cardiometabolic factors ( Table 5 ). Although there was a suggestion of higher HR for the PM 2-- ADRD association among participants who did not have the cardiometabolic conditions, this was not observed for NO 2 or aUFP. In the about one-third of study participants with genotype information, there was no modifying effects by APOE e4 carrier status ( Table S9 ). The evidence for mediation by cardiometabolic condition or lifestyle factors combined was also weak ( Tables S10 ). 4. Discussion In this large prospective study of California MEC participants (73% African American and Latino adults) enrolled in the FFS component of Medicare, incidence of ADRD was found to increase with traffic- and airport-related air pollutants, including particulate matter (PM 2.5 , PM 10, aUFP) and gaseous pollutants (NO 2 , NOx, and CO) in single pollutant models. The correlation between PM 2.5 and NO 2 was high (0.89), and the effect estimate for PM 2.5 moved towards the null when both were included in a co-pollutant model. ADRD incidence increased statistically significantly with PM 2.5 and aUFP in co-pollutant models, and both NO 2 and aUFP were associated with ADRD incidence in a multi-pollutant model adjusting for PM 2.5 . Risk patterns were similar across race and ethnicity, but African American participants showed more consistently elevated HRs for ADRD with all pollutants (except ozone). These results add to the literature, representing one of the largest prospective analyses of long-term air pollution and incidence of ADRD in African American and Latino populations using individual-level geocoded address-based exposure assessment rather than zip-code level data. Dementia incidence increased significantly 8% and 3% with exposures to PM 2.5 and NO 2 in a 2025 meta-analysis and HRs were comparable for AD and vascular dementia although the latter result, based on fewer studies, was not statistically significant. ( Best Rogowski et al., Jul 2025 ) As this and other recent meta-analyses concurred that the PM 2.5 -dementia association was stronger in studies that used active ascertainment of dementia diagnosis, ( Wilker et al., 2023 ; Best Rogowski et al., Jul 2025 ; Huang et al., 2025 ) the smaller HR estimate associated with PM 2.5 among MEC participants is likely an underestimate. Two recent studies not included in the 2025 meta-analysis are unlikely to change the overall result. ( Best Rogowski et al., Jul 2025 ) In the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study, PM 2.5 and NO 2 exposures were not associated with incident dementia (719 African American, 1,792 White) after adjustment for education and other factors. ( Song et al., 2024 ) In contrast, in a large Danish nationwide administrative cohort, dementia incidence increased strongly by 14% and 25%, respectively, in relation to PM 2.5 or NO 2 exposures. ( Andersen et al., Aug 2025 ) The consistent associations between air pollution and ADRD incidence among African American participants in the MEC add to the sparse literature in this racial group within a cohort of a robust sample size and carefully accounted for established risk factors. These associations may reflect that African American participants in the MEC had the highest prevalence of 8 of the 12 ADRD risk factors (low nSES, not married, history of hypertension, heart attack and stroke, current smoking, short/long sleep and obese) and almost 2-times higher levels of exposure to aUFP compared to other race/ethnic groups ( Table 1 ). The higher prevalence of established risk factors and exposure to other unmeasured toxicants, may explain their longstanding higher incidence of dementia. ( Eiser, Oct 2024 ; Backhouse et al., 2024 ) Published results on African American participants are mixed. In the Women’s Health Initiative Memory Study (WHIMS), PM 2.5 exposure was associated with a HR of 2.6 for AD for African American compared to HR of 1.14 in White women but results were based on 19 and 111 AD cases, respectively. ( Younan et al., 2021 ) A large Medicare data study also found stronger associations of PM 2.5 and NO 2 and ADRD incidence in African American than White adults, ( Shi et al., 2021 ) but this study used zip code-level exposure assignment, and lacked geocoded address locations and individual-level SES and other covariates. However, the Environmental Predictors of Cognitive Health and Aging (EPOCH) cohort reported no significant racial/ethnic differences in associations between incident dementia and total PM 2.5 mass. (Zhang et al., 2023) Our finding on air pollution and ADRD incidence among Latino adults, based on nearly 3000 events, adds substantially to the existing data in Latinos. ( Yu et al., Mar 2023 ; Paul et al., 2020 ) Our findings linking airport-related UFP and incidence of ADRD risk are supportive of a recent nationwide Medicare-based study which investigated the role of UFP using zip-code level data from a single year. ( Zhu et al., 2025 ) However, our results differed from a Seattle-based study reporting no association between dementia incidence and exposure to UFP and NO 2 ( Zhu et al., 2025 ) but a positive association with PM 2.5 exposure. ( Shaffer et al., Aug 2021 ) The mean UFP (10,537 particle/cm 3 ) levels in this Seattle study were higher than UFP levels among African American subjects in the MEC (9,881 particle/cm 3 ), but their levels of NO 2 (9.9 ppb) and PM 2.5 (10.1 μg/cm 3 ) were lower than the respective levels (15.2 ppb and 15.6 μg/cm 3 ) in Los Angeles County. Another Seattle study found that air pollution levels differed by income, race and ethnicity; this was particularly marked for UFP levels which were also highest among African American residents. ( Zhu et al., 2025 ; Bramble et al., Jul 2023 ) UFPs may impact brain health through unique mechanisms. Their small sizes, high concentrations in ambient environments, large surface area per mass relative to their size, and long retention in the lung enable them to penetrate extra-pulmonary organs and cross the blood brain barrier. There is increasing evidence that UFPs can impact the brain directly via the olfactory bulb, impairing mitochondrial functions and eliciting their toxic effects. ( Mussalo et al., Sep 2024 ) Hussian et al hypothesized that inhaled PM, particularly UFP, may impair glymphatic clearance, and that glymphatic transport may facilitate the spread of UFPs and their adverse effects to different brain regions. ( Hussain et al., Nov 2023 ) Airport-related UFP has been associated with risk of malignant brain tumors in a Canadian study ( Weichenthal et al., Mar 2020 ) and among MEC participants in Los Angeles County; these latter results were also stronger among African American subjects. ( Wu et al., 2021 ) In a cross-sectional study, long-term exposure to UFP was associated with a 3.6% increase in high sensitivity C-reactive protein, which increased to 6.3% when PM 2.5 was adjusted for in two-pollutant models. ( Pilz et al., Apr 2018 ) Thus, high exposures to UFP may increase risk of ADRD via both brain specific effects such as reducing the efficiency of glymphatic clearance of waste metabolites and other systemic inflammatory pathways. Air pollution is a heterogenous mixture with highly correlated components and common sources, making it challenging to identify the effects of individual pollutants or components on ADRD incidence. We investigated the effects of PM 2.5 , NO 2 , and aUFP in co-pollutant and multipollutant models among 30,703 MEC participants with data on all three pollutants ( Table 4 ). Our findings of statistically significant associations for both PM 2.5 and aUFP in co-pollutant models are supportive of findings in the nationwide Medicare cohort, ( Zhu et al., 2025 ) and add new information on mutually adjusted effects of NO 2 and aUFP. The weakened PM 2.5 effect estimate when adjusting for NO 2 among MEC participants differed from what has been reported in previous studies. In Southern California, both PM 2.5 and NO 2 represent traffic-related air pollutants, and the weakened effect estimate for PM 2.5 may reflect that our measure of PM 2.5 -derived from a chemical transport model captures contributions from traffic less well than the NO 2 measure. Nevertheless, we presume that the traffic exhaust components of PM 2.5 and NO 2 are important for the development of dementia. The relative importance of different particle sources contributing to the PM 2.5 mixture and different pollutant constituents in different geographic areas may have contributed to the divergent co-pollutant results for PM 2.5 and NO 2 in published studies. ( Yan et al., 2022 ; Shi et al., 2023 ; Andersen et al., Aug 2025 ) We did not observe consistent evidence of a modifying effect of the air pollution-ADRD association by any of the 12 established sociodemographic, lifestyle, and cardiometabolic risk factors. Although we observed stronger associations s of PM 2.5 for ADRD incidence among those without a history of cardiometabolic risk factors than those with a history, this was not observed in relation to NO 2 and aUFP exposures. Our information on risk factors was based on self-reports at cohort entry, about 10 years before Medicare entry. It is very likely that the number of heart attacks increase with increasing age and there is misclassificaton for this covariate.. Published results on potential modifying effect of comorbidities on air pollution-ADRD associations are mixed and difficult to interpret because the specific chronic conditions that were investigated differed. In some studies, modifying effects were found with one pollutant but not another pollutant, or the results related to ADRD, or only AD, or only other dementia. Since information on medications or treatment of these comorbidities were generally not available, differences in findings on modifying effects of comorbidities may be related to severity of these conditions and other unmeasured factors. ( Chen et al., Nov 2017 ; Ran et al., 2021 ; Younan et al., 2021 ; Zhang et al., 2023; Zhu et al., 2025 ) We did not find any differences in risk association by APOE e4 carrier status, consistent with other reports. ( Wang et al., 2022 ; Mortamais et al., Mar 2021 ; Semmens et al., Feb 2023 ; Parra et al., Jun 2022 ) Air pollution exposure has been estimated to be responsible for 16% of mortality worldwide, adversely affecting brain health via oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, microglial activation and vascular damage. In animal models, exposure to fine particulate matter has been associated with brain structural atrophy in a manner hypothesized to increase the risk of ADRD. ( Haghani et al., 2020 ; Woodward et al., May 2017 ) Using individual-level estimates of ambient concentrations of PM 2.5 and NO 2 to assess improvement in air quality over the span of 10 years in the WHIMS cohort, Wang and colleagues reported that reductions in PM 2.5 and NO 2 levels were associated with significantly 20% reduced risk of dementia. ( Wang et al., 2022 ) These results highlight the importance of air pollution reduction to lower ADRD incidence on a population level. Strengths of this study include the prospective design, large numbers of male and female participants, a long-term follow-up, racial, ethnic, and socioeconomic diversity, and consideration of 12 ADRD risk factors as well as APOE genotype, as adjustment variables and in stratified analyses exploring effect modification. Our study also benefited from having updated geocoded residential history since cohort entry and thus time-dependent, high-resolution air pollution measures with satisfactory model performance ( Table S11 ). Yet, there are some study limitations. We did not have information on air pollution during travel, work, or time indoors and thus exposure misclassification cannot be ruled out. High aUFP exposure is likely to coincide with elevated aircraft noise, and may introduce confounding bias although this is not likely to be a serious concern based on another study we conducted in Southern California. ( Wing et al., Apr 2020 ) African American participants are the subgroup with the highest aUFP exposures across follow-up and the highest concentration of residents living within the modeled UFP exposure grid, ( Bookstein et al., 2024 ; Wu et al., 2021 ) which may have contributed to the stronger associations. The ADRD case definition was based solely on Medicare claims data, which may not have fully captured incident cases. Our analysis was restricted to Medicare FFS beneficiaries, representing approximately ~60% of the original MEC participants, and while our results may not be generalizable to the entire MEC cohort, our study subgroup was large and had ample racial/ethnic and socioeconomic diversity. Further, the under-detection of cases might occur more often for those with mild symptoms than for severe dementia. The stronger association between air pollution and dementia incidence, for studies that used active case ascertainment instead of electronic health records suggests a higher rate of misclassification of dementia status with the latter. ( Wilker et al., 2023 ) However, this would mean that our HR estimates are likely underestimations of the true effect sizes for these air pollutants. In addition, our results only apply to those with FFS Medicare claims and we lacked information on individuals with earlier onset ADRD or those without Medicare coverage. Finally, despite the large sample size, statistical power might still be limited for some of the subgroups, and information on APOE genotype was available on only a subset of MEC participants. In summary, long-term air pollution exposure is associated with ADRD incidence, specifically AD and possibly dementia in this multiethnic cohort of older adults. As air pollution is a complex mixture of highly correlated particulate matter, gaseous pollutants and air toxics from numerous sources, it is challenging to isolate the effects for each individual pollutant and we interpret our findings to reflect an impact of traffic- and airport-related air pollution. These findings were largely consistent across race and ethnicity, but subgroup analyses suggest that ambient air pollutants may have stronger estimated effects among African American and Latino adults.
