Introduction Tumors of the vulva (VC) can originate from different cell types. Vulvar squamous cell carcinoma (VSCC) is the most common type, accounting for 90% of cases. VSCCs develop from intraepithelial neoplasia of the vulva, which can arise with or without infection with HPV types 16 and 18 ( 1 , 2 ). VC initially metastasizes lymphogenically, particularly to the inguinal lymph nodes. As the disease progresses, pelvic lymph nodes may also be affected. Hematogenous metastases are rare. The most important prognostic marker is therefore the status of the inguinal lymph nodes ( 3 ). The relative 5-year survival rate in Germany or the USA is currently around 70% ( 4 , 5 ). G-protein-coupled estrogen receptor 1 (GPER1) plays a critical role in progression, migration, and therapy resistance of various tumor entities ( 6 ). However, the overall picture remains inconsistent. GPER1 also appears to be involved in tumor suppression. Current research provides a range of answers to the question of whether GPER1 is tumor-promoting or tumor-suppressive. In VSCC, GPER1 seems to play a tumor-suppressive role ( 7 , 8 ). In addition, activation of GPER1 leads to reduced expression of plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) ( 8 ). In another entity of squamous cell carcinoma, cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC), activation of GPER1 also leads to increased expression of PAI-1 ( 9 ). In CSCC, it has been shown in addition that inhibition of GPER1 using its antagonist G36 increases PAI-1 expression ( 9 ). Tumor invasion and metastasis are critical determinants of outcome and are facilitated by proteolytic remodeling of the extracellular matrix (ECM). The urokinase plasminogen activator (uPA) system, a key protease cascade, is crucially involved in this process. PAI-1, the primary inhibitor of uPA, paradoxically exhibits a strong positive correlation with poor prognosis in multiple cancer types, including breast, ovarian, and cervical cancer. This “PAI-1 paradox” is attributed to its complex roles beyond protease inhibition, including modulation of cell adhesion, migration, and survival signaling via interactions with vitronectin and endocytosis receptors ( 10 , 11 ). In VSCC, data on PAI-1 expression and function are sparse. Given its established pro-tumorigenic role in other anogenital cancers, we hypothesized that PAI-1 represents a viable therapeutic target in VSCC. This study investigates the expression of PAI-1 in VSCC cell lines and evaluates the anti-tumor effects of its specific small-molecule inhibitor, tiplaxtinin (PAI-039, TPX) ( 12 ), on cell viability, proliferation, migration, invasion and apoptosis. Materials and Methods Cell culture. The human VSCC cell lines A431 and Cal-39 were obtained from the American Type Cell Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA). Although A431 is often referred to in the literature as a “skin cancer cell line” or “epidermoid carcinoma cell line,” the ATCC and numerous scientific articles specify its exact origin as the epidermis of the vulva ( 13 - 15 ). The cells were cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM; L0416-500, Biowest, Nuaillé, France) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; S181B-500, Biochrom, Berlin, Germany) and 1% Penicillin/Streptomycin (P/S; L0022-100, Biowest). To retain the identity of the cell lines, purchased cells were expanded and aliquots were frozen in liquid nitrogen. A new frozen stock was used every half year and mycoplasma testing of cultured cell lines was performed routinely using the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) Mycoplasma Test Kit I/C (D101-02, Vazyme, Düsseldorf, Germany). All cells were cultured in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO 2 at 37°C. Treatment. The cells were treated with PAI-1 inhibitor tiplaxtinin (PAI-039, TPX) (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) dissolved in 2% v/v ethanol (Th. Geyer, Höxter, Germany), MEM with 2% v/v ethanol (ethanol control), or plain MEM (control). Initially, the chosen concentrations were 25 µM, 50 µM, 75 µM, and 100 µM. Once the E 50 value had been determined to be between approximately 50 µM and 75 µM, depending on the assay, all further experiments were performed at these concentrations. Western blot . Cells were lysed in cell lytic M buffer (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with 0.1% phosphatase-inhibitor (C2978, Sigma) and 0.1% protease-inhibitor (P5726 and P8340, Sigma). The protein concentration was measures using the Bradford method. Isolated proteins (40 µg) were fractioned using 12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel and electro-transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (IPVH00010, Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). Primary antibodies were used against PAI-1 at 1:1,000 dilution (27535, Cell Signaling, Danvers, MA, USA) and GAPDH at 1:1,000 dilution (5174S, Cell Signaling). The membrane was washed and incubated for at least 1 h at room temperature in horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody at a 1:10,000 dilution (711-035-152, Dianova, Eching, Germany). The non-specific binding sites of the PVDF membrane were blocked by incubation in a milk powder solution [TBST-T and 5% (W/V) dried milk powder]. Blocking was carried out for 1 h at room temperature on a shaking plate. TBST was used as diluent. The membrane was washed 3 times for 10 min with TBST before and after blocking. Antibody-bond protein bands were assayed using a chemiluminescent luminol enhancer solution (XLS 30100, Cyanagen, Bologna, Italy) and the C-DiGit Blot Scanner with Image Studio software v. 4.0 (Li-Cor, Lincoln, NE, USA). Viability. For cell viability analysis via the Resazurin assay, 10,000 (A431) or 25,000 (CAL-39) cells per ml were seeded in 96-well plates (Sarstedt, Nümbrecht, Germany) using Dulbecco’s Minimum Essential Medium without phenol red (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Treatments with TPX concentrations of 25 µM, 50 µM, 75 µM, and 100 µM were initiated 24 h later. After a 72 h incubation, 20 μl of Resazurin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to each well. Ten h post-incubation, the reduction of Resazurin to Resorufin was measured at wavelengths of 570 nm and 630 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Bad Friedrichshall, Germany). The data were analyzed with GEN5 1.08 software (BioTek Instruments). Proliferation. To assess proliferation, 20,000 (A431) or 40,000 (CAL-39) cells per well were seeded in a 24-well plate (Sarstedt). Following a 24 h attachment period, cells were treated with TPX concentrations of 25 µM, 50 µM, 75 µM, and 100 µM and incubated for 72 h. After washing, the cells were fixed with methanol and then stained with crystal violet (Sigma Aldrich). After washing and drying, the plates were scanned using Epson Scan 2 software (Epson, Suwa, Japan), and analyzed for grown area using ImageJ 1.52a software (Wayne Rasband, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Migration. For gap closure assay, 1,000,000 (A431) or 1,500,000 (CAL-39) cells per ml were plated in a 24-well plate (Sarstedt) using a 2-well culture-insert (ibidi, Gräfelfing, Germany) to create a gap. After 24 h of incubation, the cells were washed with Dulbecco’s Phosphate Buffered Saline (DPBS; Pan Biotech, Aidenbach, Germany) and treated with 50µM or75 µM TPX. Images of the gap closure process were captured immediately after treatment (t0) and then after 10 h and 15 h till the gaps were closed using uEye Cockpit 2.0 software (IDS Imaging Development Systems, Obersulm, Germany). The closure rate was analyzed using ImageJ 1.52a software (Wayne Rasband). Invasion. For transwell co-culture invasion assay, Matrigel (Corning, Kennebunk, ME, USA) was mixed with DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in equal parts (150 µl each) and distributed on the bottom of inserts in a 24-well plate, incubated for 1 h. Cells (A432: 25,000 cells/ml; CAL-39: 50,000 cells/ml) were resuspended in medium and added to the inserts, which were incubated for 24 h. To attract carcinoma cells, MG63 cells (ATCC) (35,000 cells/ml) were seeded in a second 24-well plate. After 24 h, inserts were transferred into the wells with attractor cells and treated with TPX concentration of 75µM. After 5 days of co-culture, the inserts were stained using PBS (PAN-Biotech), dH₂O, methanol (Th. Geyer), hematoxylin (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and tap water. The stained inserts were transferred to microscope slides, covered with Aquatex (Merck), and sealed with a coverslip. After drying, the slides were scanned and analyzed with Cell Sens Dimension software (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and ImageJ software (Wayne Rasband). Apoptosis. A431 and Cal-39 cells (200,000 cells/ml) were seeded into a 6-well plate (Sarstedt) and treated after 24 h with BSA (Sigma Aldrich) in MEM, ethanol solution, or TPX (75 µM). On day 5, the cells were prepared for FACS analysis by transferring the medium into Eppendorf tubes (Greiner Holdings, Kremsmünster, Austria), centrifuging, and adding trypsin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to detach the cells. After counting, the samples were centrifuged again, and Binding Buffer, Annexin V (Southern Biotech, Birmingham, AL, USA), and propidium iodide (PI) (Sigma Aldrich) were added. The samples were incubated in the dark, filtered, and transferred into polystyrene tubes for analysis on the LSRFortessa X-20 flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). The data was processed using FlowJo software v10.10 (BD Bioscience, Bedford, MA, USA). Statistical analysis. All experiments were conducted with at least three biological replicates and three technical replicates. The data are presented as mean±SEM. Statistical analyses, including one-way ANOVA, two-way ANOVA, and unpaired t -tests, were carried out using GraphPad Prism (v. 8.0.1, GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Following one-way ANOVA, Dunnett’s or Tukey’s multiple comparisons tests were applied. When two groups were compared, unpaired, two-tailed parametric t-tests were performed. Statistical significance was determined for p -values 0.05. Results PAI-1 is expressed in A431 and Cal-39 VSCC cells. A similar amount of PAI-1 was detected in all samples ( Figure 1 ). Discussion This study provides in vitro evidence that pharmacological inhibition of PAI-1 with the specific small-molecule inhibitor PAI-039, TPX suppresses multiple hallmarks of aggressiveness in VSCC cells. Our data demonstrate that functional inhibition of PAI-1 in VSCC cells significantly reduces cell proliferation and potently impairs migratory and invasive capacities. These findings nominate PAI-1 as a contributory factor to VSCC aggressiveness and highlight its potential as a novel therapeutic target in this disease. Pronounced PAI-1 signals were detectable in both A431 and Cal-39 VSCC cells by western blotting. This finding is consistent with the well-documented overexpression of PAI-1 in a variety of solid tumors, where it is often associated with a poor prognosis for the patient ( 16 , 17 ). This correlation, often termed the “PAI-1 paradox” given its primary role as a protease inhibitor, underscores its complex, context-dependent functions in the tumor microenvironment ( 10 ). In VSCC, literature on PAI-1 is scarce; however, its elevated expression in related HPV-associated anogenital cancers, such as cervical carcinoma, suggests a conserved pathogenic role ( 18 ). The anti-proliferative effects of TPX observed in our study are consistent with a growing body of evidence from other cancer types. For example, similar results have been observed in cervical cancer cells ( 19 ). PAI-1 is not merely a passive inhibitor of proteolysis but an active signaling molecule. Its binding to the low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) can initiate internalization and modulate intracellular pathways, including the Akt and ERK1/2 survival signals ( 20 , 21 ). In breast cancer cells, PAI-1 inhibition has been shown to suppress these pro-survival pathways, leading to growth arrest and apoptosis ( 22 ). Our data, showing a slight, non-significant activation of apoptosis after TPX treatment, suggest that a similar mechanism may be at work in VSCC. By antagonizing PAI-1, TPX likely disrupts these LRP1-mediated survival signals, thereby shifting the cellular equilibrium towards programmed cell death. Perhaps the most striking finding of our study is the potent inhibition of VSCC cell migration and invasion by TPX. This can be explained through several complementary mechanisms independent of matrix metalloproteinase activity. A primary function of PAI-1 in cell motility is its role as a molecular orchestrator of cell adhesion and detachment. PAI-1 binds with high affinity to vitronectin (VN), a component of the provisional extracellular matrix, and to integrins via its somatomedin B domain ( 23 , 24 ). At high concentrations, PAI-1 competitively inhibits the binding of uPAR and integrins to VN, thereby destabilizing focal adhesions and promoting cell detachment—a critical first step for migration ( 25 - 27 ). Pharmacological inhibition of PAI-1 by TPX would be expected to “freeze” cells in a more adherent state, impairing their ability to detach and migrate, as vividly demonstrated in our gap closure assay. Furthermore, PAI-1 influences invasion through its central role in the spatial and temporal regulation of pericellular proteolysis. By inhibiting uPA, PAI-1 controls the generation of plasmin, a broad-spectrum protease that directly degrades matrix components and activates latent growth factors and other proteases ( 28 ). The TPX-mediated blockade of this uPA/plasmin axis would severely limit the proteolytic capacity necessary for cells to invade through a dense basement membrane or stromal matrix. This provides a plausible and literature-supported explanation for the dramatic reduction in Matrigel invasion we observed ( 29 ). The invasive phenotype of cancers like VSCC is a multi-step process, and our results position PAI-1 inhibition as an effective strategy to interrupt both the detachment (migration) and matrix degradation (invasion) phases. The clinical relevance of targeting PAI-1 in VSCC, while yet to be established in vivo , is supported by its prognostic significance in other malignancies. In the context of VSCC, which often presents in elderly patients with comorbidities, a targeted therapy with a potentially favorable toxicity profile, as suggested by some in vivo studies with TPX ( 12 ), could be particularly valuable. It could be envisioned as an adjunct to standard therapies for locally advanced or recurrent disease. Given that lymph node metastasis is the strongest predictor of poor outcome in VSCC, the ability of TPX to impair migration and invasion in vitro suggests a potential to interfere with the metastatic spread to regional lymph nodes. Indeed, recent analyses confirm that refined lymph node assessment strategies remain central to risk stratification and therapeutic decision-making in VSCC ( 30 , 31 ). Should our findings translate in vivo , PAI-1 inhibition could emerge as a valuable adjunct to current therapy, potentially reducing recurrence rates in high-risk patients. Study limitations and future directions. This in vitro proof-of-concept study has limitations that define clear paths for future research. First, the in vitro nature of our work and the relatively high concentrations of TPX required for efficacy necessitate validation in in vivo models. Establishing xenograft models of VSCC would be ideal to assess tumor growth inhibition and potential toxicity ( 32 ). Second, the precise molecular cascade downstream of PAI-1 inhibition in VSCC cells warrants detailed investigation. Proteomic and phosphoproteomic analyses following TPX treatment could unbiasedly identify the key affected pathways, such as integrin-mediated signaling (FAK/Src) or survival cascades (PI3K/Akt) ( 33 ). Third, the therapeutic potential of combining PAI-1 inhibition with standard cytotoxic agents ( e.g. , cisplatin) or radiotherapy should be explored, as PAI-1 is known to be induced by cellular stress and chemotherapy, potentially contributing to treatment resistance ( 34 , 35 ). Conclusion Our data establish PAI-1 as a functionally relevant protein in VSCC aggressiveness. TPX effectively counteracts pro-tumorigenic functions of PAI-1, reducing viability and proliferation, and potently inhibiting migration and invasion. These results nominate PAI-1 as a promising novel therapeutic target for VSCC and provide a strong rationale for further preclinical development of PAI-1 inhibitors in this disease context.
