Introduction Inflammatory breast cancer (IBC) is the most aggressive form of breast cancer, characterized by high rates of local and distant failure 1 , 2 . It accounts for approximately 2–4% 3 , 4 of all breast cancer cases with a high mortality rate 5 , 6 . Due to the high rate of early distant metastasis, about 30% of IBC patients are diagnosed with metastatic disease 7 , 8 . Historically, local treatment was often deferred in this group due to the suspected widespread dissemination of micrometastatic disease 9 – 11 and the related presumption that local therapies would not impart survival benefit. As systemic therapy options have improved, clinical trials across multiple primary disease sites have shown promise that delivering local therapies in the setting of metastatic disease may improve survival and/or extend the viability of the current line of systemic therapy 12 – 18 . Unfortunately, despite the encouraging results in other cancer types, trials in the non-inflammatory metastatic breast cancer population evaluating mastectomy 19 – 28 and consolidative metastatic therapy 29 – 31 have largely failed to show a progression-free survival or overall survival benefit. Nevertheless, these prospective results may not be applicable to the inflammatory metastatic breast cancer setting, as patients with inflammatory metastatic breast cancer were either not included or underrepresented in these trials. In contrast to non-inflammatory BC, IBC patients experience high rates of local progression of up to 57% in the absence of local therapy 32 – 34 . This clinical behavior corresponds to a distinct gene expression profile and suggests a potential utility in aggressive local therapy to the primary site in the metastatic setting 35 – 37 . Prior retrospective studies have shown that the delivery of mastectomy with or without post-mastectomy radiation therapy (PMRT) is associated with improved survival 33 , 34 , 38 – 43 . While these studies failed to show an OS benefit of radiation therapy in multivariate analyses, these projects were not designed to assess the benefit of adjuvant PMRT as part of trimodality therapy, defined in this manuscript as neoadjuvant chemotherapy and surgery followed by PMRT. Since local control is unlikely to affect survival events in the setting of uncontrolled systemic disease, assessing the benefit of local therapies in populations that did not receive upfront systemic therapy may result in underestimated survival benefits of local therapies such as PMRT. To better understand which components of local therapy are associated with a survival benefit when delivered after chemotherapy in metastatic IBC, data from patients with metastatic IBC in the National Cancer Data Base (NCDB) was reviewed. Outcomes with trimodality therapy were compared to those following chemotherapy alone, chemotherapy plus radiation, and chemotherapy plus surgery. It was hypothesized that: 1. Patients who received any form of local therapy would have improved survival relative to those receiving chemotherapy alone, and 2. Patients who received trimodality therapy will have improved survival compared to those receiving chemotherapy followed by surgery or radiation alone. Results Patient characteristics Between 2010 and 2021, a total of 3219 metastatic IBC patients meeting the inclusion criteria were extracted from the NCDB. Of these, 2469 (76.7%) received chemotherapy alone, 261 (8.1%) received chemotherapy plus surgery, 86 (2.7%) received chemotherapy plus radiation, and 403 (12.5%) received trimodality therapy (Fig. 1 ). Associations between treatment groups and overall survival The median follow-up was 23.6 months (range 0–168.2 months). Over this period, a total of 2210 (68.7%) OS events were observed: 1742 (70.6%) in the chemotherapy alone group, 165 (63.2%) in the chemotherapy plus surgery group, 70 (81.4%) in the chemotherapy plus radiation group, and 233 (57.8%) in the trimodality therapy group. The unadjusted 2-year OS rates were 51.8% for the chemotherapy alone group, 57.8% for the chemotherapy plus surgery group, 36.5% for the chemotherapy plus radiation group, and 65.1% for the trimodality therapy group (Supplementary Fig. 1 ). However, the benefit of trimodality therapy disappeared on adjustment. Receipt of chemotherapy alone (adjusted hazard ratio (aHR): 1.41, 95% CI: 1.16–1.71) and receipt of chemotherapy plus radiation (aHR 1.80, 95% CI: 1.32–2.47) were associated with increased all-cause mortality compared to patients who received chemotherapy plus surgery. The addition of adjuvant radiation therapy after chemotherapy and surgery was not associated with a survival benefit (aHR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.78–1.24) Table 2 . Moreover, the addition of radiation therapy after chemotherapy without surgery was not associated with a survival benefit (aHR: 1.25, 95% CI: 0.97–1.61) compared to patients who received chemotherapy alone (Supplementary Table 2 ). Over a period of 12 years, we observed that the use of systemic therapy alone has been increasing steadily year by year (Supplementary Fig. 2 ). Two-year overall survival by each year of diagnosis was plotted (Supplementary Fig. 3 ) for each treatment type to visualize trends in treatment group outcomes over time. No clear trends were observed between 2010 and 2021. Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess if PMRT was associated with improved survival dependent on surgical margin, lymph node status, receptor subtypes, age, and response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy (Supplementary Tables 3 – 19 , Supplementary Fig. 4 ). We found that, in patients with positive surgical margins ( n = 78), the addition of PMRT to surgery plus chemotherapy was associated with improved survival compared with surgery plus chemotherapy alone adjusting for age (aHR 0.57; 95% CI: 0.34–0.97) (Supplementary Table 3 ). In contrast, PMRT when added to chemotherapy and surgery, was not associated with improved survival compared to the chemotherapy plus surgery group among patients with positive lymph nodes (aHR 0.98; 95% CI: 0.77–1.25), ER+/PR+/HER2− receptor status (aHR 0.96; 95% CI: 0.63–1.45), ER+/PR (+ or −)/HER2+ receptor status (aHR 0.63; 95% CI: 0.38–1.06), ER−/PR−/HER2+ receptor status (aHR 1.12; 95% CI: 0.66–1.91), triple-negative receptor status (aHR 0.88; 95% CI: 0.60–1.29), or those younger than 50 years of age (aHR 1.08; 95% CI: 0.70–1.67). In addition, chemotherapy plus any local therapy (either surgery or radiation or both) was not associated with improved survival compared to chemotherapy alone (aHR 0.91; 95% CI: 0.81–1.02). Although a larger cohort (2004–2021) was also analyzed to improve statistical power, we were unable to detect the OS benefit of PMRT in any additional subgroups (Supplementary Tables 20 – 30 , Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6 ). Out of the total cohort, 481 patients who received neoadjuvant systemic treatment followed by surgery with documented pathologic response were identified (Supplementary Table 12 ). The pathologic response rates following neoadjuvant therapy were as follows: complete response (CR) in 114 patients (23.7%), partial response (PR) in 201 patients (41.8%), response without specified CR or PR in 114 patients (23.7%), and no response in 52 patients (10.8%). In the ‘any response’ subgroup (429 patients), 155 (36.1%) patients received chemotherapy plus surgery, and 274 (63.9%) received trimodality therapy. The chemotherapy plus surgery group was older (median ages: 60 versus 53 years) and had a higher proportion of patients with two or more metastatic organ systems (18.7% versus 9.9%) compared to the trimodality therapy group. No OS benefit from trimodality therapy compared to chemotherapy and surgery was observed in this subgroup (aHR 0.93; 95% CI: 0.70–1.24). In the ‘complete response’ subgroup (114 patients), 33 (28.9%) patients received chemotherapy plus surgery, and 81 (71.1%) patients received trimodality therapy. The chemotherapy plus surgery group was older than the trimodality therapy group (median ages: 62 versus 51 years). No significant OS benefit from trimodality therapy was observed (aHR 0.81; 95% CI: 0.43–1.51). In the ‘no-response’ subgroup (52 patients), 27 (51.9%) patients received chemotherapy plus surgery, and 25 (48.1%) received trimodality therapy. There was a higher proportion of patients with ER+/PR (+ or −)/HER2- status in the trimodality therapy group versus the chemotherapy plus surgery group (56.0% versus 29.6%). There was a significant decrease of 50% in all-cause mortality in the trimodality therapy group compared to the chemotherapy plus surgery group after adjusting for age and number of metastatic organ systems (aHR 0.50; 95% CI: 0.25–0.98). However, after adjusting for receptor subtype, this association was no longer significant (aHR 0.75; 95% CI: 0.39–1.44) (Supplementary Tables 13 – 19 ). Discussion To our knowledge, this represents the first study designed to assess the marginal benefit of radiation therapy, surgery, and surgery with adjuvant radiation therapy when added to chemotherapy in the metastatic IBC setting. Although we observed that survival was higher when either chemotherapy plus surgery or trimodality therapy was used compared to chemotherapy alone, we did not observe an improvement in survival among patients who received chemotherapy followed by radiation therapy compared to chemotherapy alone. Additionally, no survival benefit was noted with trimodality therapy compared to chemotherapy plus surgery after adjusting for age, Charlson–Deyo score, receptor subtype, and number of metastatic organ systems. These results contrast the crude survival data showing a 7.3% improvement in OS with trimodality therapy and suggest that improvement may be due to a selection bias in which physicians select healthier patients with less widely metastatic disease for trimodality therapy. Additionally, the equivalent adjusted survival outcomes between trimodality therapy and chemotherapy plus surgery suggest that most of the survival benefit from local therapy in the MIBC setting comes from the surgical rather than radiation component of care. Nonetheless, in a subgroup analysis, the addition of PMRT to surgery and chemotherapy was associated with improved survival among patients with positive surgical margins. Our findings align with those of several retrospective studies evaluating the benefit of local therapies in MIBC summarized 33 , 38 , 39 , 44 , 45 in Table 3 . For example, Drapalik et al. 33 investigated the role of surgery in 1049 patients with de novo metastatic IBC. They found that the 2-year OS was significantly better in patients who underwent surgery (71% vs. 38%) while radiation therapy had no effect on OS (HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.72–1.36) in their multivariable analysis. Similar results were reported by Chen et al. 38 , who studied 1293 patients with de novo metastatic IBC comparing MRM versus no-MRM. The 5-year disease-free survival (DFS) was higher in the MRM group (31.4% vs. 17.7%, p = 0.001). Although radiation treatment was more common in the MRM group, no significant OS benefit from radiation was observed on multivariate analysis (HR 1.07, 95% CI 0.88–1.30). Of note, while these studies often included radiation therapy in multivariate analyses for OS, they did not design the analyses to detect the benefit of adjuvant radiation following neoadjuvant chemotherapy and surgery as done in this manuscript. Additionally, no prior retrospective reviews have classified the benefit of PMRT in this setting by response to neoadjuvant therapy or surgical margin status. To date, there are no prospective phase 3 trials specifically evaluating the benefits of local treatment in de novo MIBC patients. The available prospective trials, mostly involving metastatic non-IBC breast cancer patients, have yielded conflicting results and are summarized in Table 4 . For example, the Tata Memorial Hospital trial 25 randomized 350 de novo metastatic breast cancer patients—173 to locoregional treatment (a standard surgical procedure that was either mastectomy or breast conserving surgery followed by PMRT as per standard institutional practice) and 177 to no locoregional treatment—and found no significant difference in 2-year OS between the groups. EA2108 20 trial randomized 256 patients with metastatic breast cancer who had no disease progression after systemic treatment to either continuation of systemic therapy or early locoregional therapy and found no improvement in 3-year overall survival. Similar findings were reported in the ABCSG-28 Posytive Trial 19 , 28 , which showed no OS or quality-of-life benefits from upfront surgery. On the other hand, MF07-01 27 randomized 278 treatment-naïve stage IV breast cancer patients to surgery with or without radiation followed by systemic therapy versus systemic therapy alone. The surgery group demonstrated a 29% reduction in mortality compared to the systemic therapy alone group (HR 0.71; 95% CI 0.59–0.86; p = 0.0003). Given distinctive cancer biology and clinical behavior resulting in high rates of local recurrence in IBC, the applicability of the above prospective trials, which often exclude or do not specify the percentage of IBC patients included, is limited. Notably, the addition of PMRT to surgery and chemotherapy was associated with improved survival among patients with positive surgical margins in our study. While exploratory, this observation highlights margin status as a clinically relevant factor that may help identify patients with metastatic IBC most likely to benefit from PMRT. This observation is consistent with emerging evidence from the recently published JCOG1017 PRIM-BC 24 trial, which randomized metastatic breast cancer to receive either systemic treatment (ST) alone or primary tumor resection and ST. Although no overall survival benefit was observed in this study for primary tumor resection and the ST arm. The investigators reported significantly worse outcomes among patients with positive surgical margins compared with those with negative margins (median overall survival 60.7 vs. 94.5 months; hazard ratio 1.97, 95% CI 1.16–3.35). Together, these findings underscore the prognostic importance of margin status in the metastatic setting and suggest that optimizing locoregional control—potentially through the selective use of PMRT—may be particularly relevant for patients with residual disease following surgery. Among our cohort, the triple-negative subtype was identified as the worst prognostic factor for OS. In addition, older age, worsened performance status, and having two or more metastatic organ systems were associated with poorer survival in our study. The association of ER-negative disease with worse outcomes aligns with findings of other investigations. Takiar et al. 39 observed significantly worsened overall survival in the ER-negative cohort versus the ER-positive cohort (5-year OS 37 versus 71 months, p -value = 0.02), and Chen et al. 38 found that HR−/Her2− was associated with worse survival (HR 2.54, p -value 0.00) compared to HR+/Her2−. In our investigation, ER+/PR (+ or −)/HER2+ and ER−/PR−/HER2+ status appeared to be protective factors, likely reflecting advances in HER2-targeted therapies. This finding aligned with those of Drapalik et al. 33 , which found HER2+ to be a protective factor (HR 0.392, p -value 0.001). Strengths of this study include the large sample size and the use of the NCDB, which allows analysis of rare diseases and interventions that are otherwise difficult to study. While previous large database analyses suggested benefits of bimodal or trimodal therapy in metastatic IBC, this is, to our knowledge, the first study to specifically evaluate the role of adjuvant chest wall radiation after neoadjuvant chemotherapy and mastectomy in metastatic inflammatory disease, and to identify a subset of patients with metastatic IBC who may be most likely to benefit from PMRT. Limitations include inherent biases associated with retrospective analyses and missing information on immunotherapy usage. Although the addition of surgery to chemotherapy was associated with improved survival in this study, these retrospective data are descriptive in nature and not sufficient to suggest changes in management. Another limitation of this study is that the NCDB does not provide any information regarding local control, recurrence patterns, or symptomatic benefit, which, if improved by surgery and PMRT, may be a justification to offer trimodality therapy given the potential morbidity of an IBC local recurrence. However, the potential morbidity of surgery and radiation—particularly lymphedema—must be carefully weighed when counseling patients. Our NCDB analysis suggested that adding surgery but not adjuvant radiation therapy is associated with improved survival among patients with metastatic inflammatory breast cancer. Margin status emerged as a clinically relevant factor that may help identify patients most likely to benefit from PMRT following chemotherapy and surgery and warrants further investigation. Methods Study population This retrospective cohort study used data from NCDB, a joint program of the American College of Surgeons and the American Cancer Society that captures approximately 70% of new cancer diagnoses from more than 1500 Commission on Cancer–accredited facilities in the United States 46 , 47 . This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Because the NCDB contains only de-identified data, institutional review board approval and informed consent were not required. Women aged 18 years or older with metastatic inflammatory breast cancer—defined as clinical stage T4d, any N, and M1—diagnosed between 2010 and 2021 were identified. Since the number of metastatic organ systems was not recorded until 2010, the main analytical cohort included patients diagnosed between 2010 and 2021. However, a full cohort, which included all patients diagnosed from 2004 to 2021, was also analyzed, and those results are included in the supplementary materials. Treatment cohorts Patients were categorized into four groups: (1) chemotherapy alone; (2) chemotherapy plus surgery (modified radical mastectomy (MRM), mastectomy with ≥10 lymph nodes removed, or radical mastectomy); (3) chemotherapy plus radiation (40.05–70 Gy in 15–33 fractions to the breast, chest wall, or regional nodes); and (4) trimodality therapy (chemotherapy, surgery, and postoperative radiation). Treatment was modeled as a time-varying covariate to reduce immortal time bias. For example, in patients who received both chemotherapy and radiation therapy or patients who received trimodality therapy, we classified the period from chemotherapy administration to the onset of radiation therapy as chemotherapy only, while the interval from the onset of radiation therapy to the study end was classified as chemotherapy and radiation category or as trimodality therapy category, respectively. A secondary exposure dichotomized patients as chemotherapy only versus chemotherapy plus any local therapy (surgery and/or radiation) and was similarly modeled as time-varying. Outcome The primary outcome was overall survival (OS), defined as the duration, in days, from the chemotherapy administration to the date of death from any cause or the date of last known follow-up, whichever occurred first 47 . Patients who were alive at the end of the study were censored. Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics Sociodemographic variables included age, race, ethnicity, insurance status, geographic region, median income, educational attainment, and urban versus rural location. Clinical variables included the Charlson–Deyo comorbidity score, nodal status, tumor grade, molecular subtype, treatment facility type, and number of metastatic organ systems. Molecular subtype followed SEER classification 48 : HR+/HER2–, HR+/HER2+, HR–/HER2+, and triple-negative (HR–/HER2–). Surgical margins indicated the final status of the surgical margins after resection of the primary tumor and were coded as “surgical margin positive” if they had a microscopic margin (R1) or macroscopic margin (R2) positive, or R1/R2 not specific. Reconstruction was coded based on specific values for the surgical procedure performed on the primary site (breast) at any facility and included tissue, implant, as well as combined reconstructions. Statistical analysis Group differences were compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables and χ² or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables. Cox models with robust variance estimated hazard ratios (HRs) for OS and time-varying treatment type (primary exposure) with adjustment for age, comorbidity, subtype, and number of metastatic organ systems. Overlap Propensity Score Weighting (OPSW) was used to account for the impact of confounding, which results from differences in baseline characteristics that can affect receipt of treatment type (secondary exposure) 49 . OPSW allows for an exact balance of means for every covariate included in the multivariable logistic regression models used to estimate propensity score 50 . Treatment group (chemotherapy with any local therapy vs chemotherapy only) was the outcome, and age, Charlson–Deyo score, molecular subtype, and number of metastatic organ systems were covariates. OPSW Cox model with robust variance was used to assess the relationship between this time-varying treatment group and OS. Subset analyses evaluated the comparisons between patients who received chemotherapy plus surgery versus trimodality therapy based on the response to neoadjuvant therapy, surgical margin status, and reconstruction. Patients were coded as ‘any response’ to neoadjuvant therapy if they had a response, but not noted if complete or partial. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc).
