Introduction Thyroid cancer is one of the fastest-growing malignancies worldwide, with its incidence rising particularly rapidly among adolescents and young adults (AYAs), making it the most common cancer in this age group. 1 When compared with other major AYA cancers, such as testicular, breast, ovarian, brain central nervous system, colorectal cancer, and non-Hodgkin lymphoma, which also show upward trends, thyroid cancer demonstrates the fastest increase. 2 A diagnosis during these formative years poses unique challenges, including significant psychological distress and a higher risk of severe long-term effects like infertility, cardiovascular disease, and secondary cancers compared with older patients with cancer. 3 This is compounded by overdiagnosis, which can lead to unnecessary treatments and impose a lifelong psychological and physical burden on young patients and the healthcare system. 4 This escalating trend is further complicated by significant global disparities in socio-demographic development. These Socio-demographic Index (SDI) disparities may mediate the thyroid cancer epidemic through differential environmental exposures and healthcare practices. In high-SDI regions, populations experience higher exposure to medical radiation, while the widespread adoption of diagnostic technologies has fueled concerns about overdiagnosis and overtreatment. 5 , 6 Conversely, in low and low-middle SDI countries, populations are affected by iodine deficiency, which elevates the risk of thyroid cancer, as well as significant challenges related to misdiagnosis and delayed treatment stemming from limited healthcare resources, ultimately leading to considerably poorer clinical outcomes. 7 , 8 This contrast highlights the urgent need for tailored global health strategies that address the specific needs of AYAs. While previous studies have analyzed the global burden of thyroid cancer in broader populations, 9 , 10 the specific epidemiological patterns within the AYA cohort, particularly in relation to socioeconomic development, remain insufficiently explored. This gap is critical from a global health perspective. High- and high-middle SDI countries have begun to address AYAs challenges by developing tailored guidelines and survivorship care models. In contrast, many low and low-middle SDI regions often lack this focus, grouping AYAs with the general population. This research examined this disparity by analyzing global trends in thyroid cancer among AYAs from 1990 to 2021, with a primary focus on how the burden correlates with the SDI. By identifying key inflection points, such as the rapid acceleration of the disease burden around an SDI of 0.55, this study provides evidence to inform more targeted diagnostic and treatment priorities across different development settings, especially for vulnerable populations in developing regions where awareness and tailored policies are most lacking. Methods Data sources This study is a secondary analysis of publicly available, aggregated data obtained from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 project, which is led by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) at the University of Washington. The GBD 2021 study provides comprehensive and comparable estimates of burden for 369 diseases and injuries across 21 regions and 204 countries and territories globally from 1990 to 2021. 11 To ensure data quality, GBD integrated information from multiple sources, including cancer registries, vital registration systems, hospital records, and scientific literature. For countries or regions with incomplete or missing data, GBD employs advanced statistical modeling approaches, such as Cause of Death Ensemble Modeling (CODEm), spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression, and Bayesian meta-regression (DisMod-MR 2.1), to borrow strength from neighboring countries, similar populations, or related covariates. These methods allowed estimation of disease burden even in data-sparse settings, thereby ensuring internal consistency, comparability across countries, and robustness of the results. All estimates are presented with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) for each metric, which are calculated from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 1000 draws from the posterior distribution to reflect statistical uncertainty. 12 Study design and variables A repeated cross-sectional study design was used to analyze trends in the global burden of thyroid cancer. The study population was defined as AYAs aged 15–39 years, in accordance with the definition established by the National Cancer Institute and the Young Adult Oncology Progress Review Group. 13 Previous studies on cancer in AYAs have also used this definition. This range captures the unique biological, psychosocial, and healthcare access challenges faced by this group, distinct from both pediatric and older adult populations. 3 We extracted the number, rate (per 100,000 population), and 95% UI of annual incidence, prevalence, deaths, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for thyroid cancer in AYAs aged 15–39 years from the Global Health Data Exchange website ( http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool ) for analysis. The dataset comprises information on genders, ages (5-year intervals from 15 to 19 years up to 35–39 years), years (1990–2021), regions, and countries. 14 The primary independent variable for stratification was the Socio-demographic Index (SDI), a composite measure evaluating a geography's socio-demographic development status. Following the methodology updated since GBD 2017 and applied in GBD 2021, the SDI is computed as the geometric mean of three rescaled covariate indices. These include lag-distributed income per capita, which captures long-term economic capacity; average years of schooling for the population aged 15 years and older, serving as a proxy for educational attainment and health literacy; and the total fertility rate for females under 25 years of age, an indicator reflecting the reproductive status and social empowerment of young women. To ensure comparability, each component was rescaled on a 0–1 scale based on the theoretical minimum and maximum values observed across all GBD locations over the estimation period. An SDI value of 0 represents the theoretical minimum level of development relevant to health (lowest income, lowest education, highest fertility), while a value of 1 represents the theoretical maximum. In GBD 2021, the 204 countries and territories globally were divided into five groups based on their SDI values: Low (0–0.4658), Low-middle (0.4658–0.6188), Middle (0.6188–0.712), High-middle (0.712–0.8103), and High (0.8103–1). 15 Data processing To ensure the comparability of disease burden indicators across different years, genders, countries, and regions, we calculate the age standardized rate (ASR), including age standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age standardized prevalence rate (ASPR), age standardized mortality rate (ASMR), age standardized DALYs rate (ASDR), using the GBD world population standard. 16 The specific formula for age-standardization is as follows: ASR = ∑ i = 1 A a i w i ∑ i = 1 A w i × 100 , 000 where a i represents the age-specific rate for the i th age group, w i represents the number of individuals (or weight) in the same i th age group in the chosen reference standard population, and A represents the number of age groups. Statistical analysis The time trends in disease burden were assessed with joinpoint regression analyses by calculating average annual percentage change (AAPC) statistics and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). This method was used to identify significant changes in trend direction over time. We fitted a log-linear model allowing up to five joinpoints. All models were plotted using the “grid search method” and the “uncorrelated error model”. To assess the potential impact of autocorrelation in the data, the analyses were repeated multiple times, each with different autocorrelation parameter values (0.1, 0.2, and 0.3). Outcomes were similar to those obtained with the uncorrelated error model, which was then selected for the final analyses, as correcting for autocorrelation can reduce the power to detect joinpoints ( Figures S1–2 ). The statistical significance of each joinpoint was assessed using a Monte Carlo permutation test (4499 permutations, α = 0.05) with built-in multiple testing correction to control the overall type I error rate. 17 If AAPC 0, the ASR was considered to have an increasing trend; if AAPC 0, the ASR was considered to have a decreasing trend. We applied the restricted cubic splines (RCS) with the lowest Akaike information criterion (AIC) to explore the nonlinear relationship between SDI and AAPC of ASRs. The RCS model avoids the loss of information due to categorisation and reduces subjectivity as compared to the discrete treatment of continuous variables. 18 To verify the temporal stability of the association between SDI and disease burden of AAPC, we performed a stratified analysis of AAPC by dividing the study period into three decades (1990–2000, 2001–2010, and 2011–2021) and examining the correlation between the AAPC of each period and the SDI of the corresponding end year. This study was reported in line with the strengthening the reporting of cohort, cross-sectional, and case–control studies in surgery 2025 criteria. 19 GBD 2021 complies with the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER) statement. 12 All statistical analyses were conducted using the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.0.2) and R (version 4.2.3). A two-sided P -value 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Results Global trends and disparities by SDI and country From 1990–2021, the global burden of thyroid cancer in adolescents and young adults increased substantially. New cases increased from approximately 19,268 to 48,203, and the ASIR rose from 0.93 (95% UI: 0.83–1.06) to 1.59 (95% UI: 1.34 to 1.92) per 100,000 population with an AAPC of 1.73% (95% CI: 1.60–1.87). Similarly, the ASPR increased from 8.28 per 100,000 (95% UI: 7.43–9.45) to 14.35 (95% UI: 12.13–17.4) over the same period. In stark contrast, the ASMR and ASDR remained low and demonstrated only a modest upward trend globally, with AAPCs of 0.20% and 0.38%, respectively ( Fig. 1 , Table 1 ). At the national level, the highest ASIR and ASPR were recorded in Saudi Arabia (ASIR = 5.49, ASPR = 50.04 per 100,000), Libya (ASIR = 4.78, ASPR = 43.68), and Viet Nam (ASIR = 4.25, ASPR = 38.81) in 2021. These broad global patterns are consistent with previous studies. 20 , 21 Association between development and trend acceleration In the pooled analysis spanning from 1990 to 2021, SDI was associated with the AAPCs of ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR with an inverted U-shaped pattern (All P -overall and P -nonlinear 0.001). This pattern indicates that the rate of increase in the thyroid cancer burden among AYA is not uniform across the development spectrum. Specifically, the highest AAPCs were observed at 1.88% for ASIR with an SDI of 0.58, and 2.06% for ASPR at an SDI of 0.55. In contrast, the peaks for ASMR and ASDR were smaller, at 0.13% and 0.25%, occurring at SDI values of 0.52 and 0.54, respectively ( Fig. 2 ). These peaks are clustered around an SDI value of approximately 0.55, identifying the upper end of the low-middle SDI range as a critical transition point where the disease burden escalates most rapidly. Temporal shift in peak acceleration across SDI levels Stratified analysis by decade (1990–2000, 2001–2010, and 2011–2021) revealed a significant 'High-to-Low-middle SDI Shift' in risk patterns ( Fig. 3 ). In the early phase (1990–2000), the fastest increases in ASIR and ASPR were concentrated in high SDI settings, where the maximum AAPC reached 3.05% for ASIR and 3.07% for ASPR at an SDI of 0.89. During the same period, the peaks for ASMR and ASDR were located at lower SDI values, with a maximum AAPC of 0.56% for ASMR at an SDI of 0.39 and 0.69% for ASDR at an SDI of 0.4. This pattern may be consistent with the influence of intensive medical surveillance and overdiagnosis. In the intermediate phase (2001–2010), the overall relationship weakened and no dominant SDI gradient was observed at the global level ( P 0.05). This plateau likely resulted from divergent regional trends, as the slowing incidence rates in high-SDI nations occurred concurrently with the initial acceleration of disease burden in developing regions. In the recent phase (2011–2021), the development related pattern became significant again ( P -overall 0.001) and the location of maximum acceleration shifted to the low middle SDI range. The maximum AAPC reached 2.33% for ASIR and 2.43% for ASPR at an SDI of 0.54, and increased to 0.5% for ASMR at an SDI of 0.53 and 0.67% for ASDR at an SDI of 0.54. This recent peak aligns closely with the threshold identified in our overall pooled analysis (SDI ≈ 0.55), indicating that the primary region of rapid disease growth has effectively shifted from developed countries to nations experiencing rapid socioeconomic development. Discussion The global burden of AYA thyroid cancer increased significantly, with significant disparities across regions and socioeconomic development levels. While recent literature has explored the overall thyroid cancer burden in AYAs, 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 the primary focus of our study was to assess global health inequities in AYA thyroid cancer burden across regions with varying levels of socioeconomic development, as measured by the SDI. Based on the GBD 2021 database, the present study showed that there were significant differences in the disease burden of thyroid cancer in AYAs across different regions and countries. From 1990–2021, the global ASIR and ASPR showed a significant upward trend, while the ASMR and ASDR remained at low levels with stable trends. Furthermore, a distinct peak was observed in the AAPC of all disease burden indicators at an SDI level of approximately 0.55. These findings provide a basis for understanding the epidemiological status of thyroid cancer in AYAs and call for tailored policy interventions to deal with this issue. The dramatic rise in the global incidence of AYA thyroid cancer, contrasted with stable and low mortality rates, may be consistent with overdiagnosis as an important contributor, particularly in high-SDI nations. This phenomenon is strongly associated with the widespread adoption of advanced diagnostic imaging technologies since the 1980s, which has resulted in a higher incidental detection rate of small, indolent papillary microcarcinomas that are unlikely to pose a significant health risk. Evidence from countries like the United States 26 and South Korea 27 shows a temporal correlation between rising thyroid ultrasound use, mass screening programs, and soaring incidence rates. This trend carries significant consequences for AYAs, who may face unnecessary treatments, lifelong hormone replacement, and substantial psychological distress. 28 However, overdiagnosis does not fully explain the rising trends. Additional factors such as radiation exposure, obesity, and generational risk shifts may also contribute to changing trends in thyroid cancer among AYAs. Recent population-based studies examining tumor size trends have identified an increase in the incidence of both small and large tumors among young individuals, suggesting that increased overdiagnosis is not the sole explanation for the rising trends. 4 Since 1990, most countries have observed an increase in obesity rates among children and adolescents, with particularly significant rises noted in many middle-income regions. 29 Furthermore, exposure to diagnostic radiation has significantly increased among AYAs. National data from Japan indicate a 30–70% increase in annual CT procedures over the past two decades. Additionally, North American cohorts reporting continued growth in adults aged 18–39, highlighting greater cumulative radiation exposure in this age group. 5 , 30 Therefore, the observed increase in thyroid cancer among AYAs incidence is likely a complex interplay of both detection bias from overdiagnosis and a real increase driven by environmental and lifestyle factors. A novel finding of our study is the inverted U-shaped association between SDI and the AAPC of thyroid cancer in AYAs, peaking around an SDI value of 0.55 (a level corresponding to low-middle SDI regions). This pattern aligns with findings reported in prior authoritative studies. 31 , 32 This peak likely represents a critical transition period where several socioeconomic and health system dynamics converge to accelerate the disease burden. Firstly, this SDI phase often corresponds to rapid industrialization and urbanization. During this period, populations undergo a "nutrition transition" toward obesogenic diets, while environmental pollution often peaks before regulatory measures are fully implemented—a pattern consistent with the Environmental Kuznets Curve theory. 33 This creates an environment where true risk for cancers like thyroid cancer accelerates rapidly. Secondly, these countries are experiencing a gradual shift in the disease spectrum toward non-communicable diseases (NCDs). 34 , 35 However, in countries with low-middle SDI, levels of the effective coverage index (an indicator of universal health coverage) for NCDs were far lower than those for communicable, maternal, neonatal, and nutritional diseases. 36 This creates a perilous gap: while the proliferation of diagnostic technologies may fuel an initial surge in case detection, the infrastructure for cancer treatment, public health education, and evidence-based screening guidelines often lags behind. This mismatch can lead to both rising incidence and, critically, rising mortality and DALYs from inadequately managed cases. Thirdly, improvements in cause-of-death registration and surveillance systems during this stage may also steepen the estimated trend. Therefore, greater attention should be paid to thyroid cancer in AYAs. These divergent trends necessitate a differentiated public health response. In high-SDI regions, the priority should be to mitigate overdiagnosis by avoiding unnecessary detection and treatment, promoting active surveillance for low-risk papillary microcarcinomas, and enhancing education for clinicians and the public on the harms of over-screening. Furthermore, this strategy should include reconsidering the 'cancer' label for patients with low-risk disease to prevent unnecessary treatment and the associated psychological burden. For low-middle SDI regions around 0.55, prevention and control strategies should focus on strengthening diagnostic accuracy, treatment quality, and referral networks so that increased detection can translate into better outcomes. 37 , 38 Therefore, parallel efforts are needed to strengthen cancer care infrastructure, including diagnostic accuracy, treatment quality, and referral networks. Furthermore, addressing modifiable risk factors through cost-effective primary prevention measures, such as universal salt iodization and obesity-prevention programs, is essential. 39 , 40 Leveraging innovative and affordable models of care, including digital health tools and task-sharing, can help extend access to follow-up and survivorship support in these resource-limited settings. 41 , 42 Despite providing a comprehensive analysis of the global burden of thyroid cancer in AYAs, this study has certain limitations. First, data quality and under-reporting remain particularly problematic in low-SDI regions due to challenges such as vital-registration lags, sparse cancer registries, and misdiagnosis, all of which likely lead to underestimation of true incidence and mortality. 12 Secondly, the data on thyroid cancer in the GBD 2021 database lack detailed information on tumor pathology, stage, screening exposure, or treatment pathways. Consequently, mechanisms such as overdiagnosis and care-related limitations could not be directly analyzed. Third, consistent with all aggregate-level analyses utilizing GBD data, our study is susceptible to the ecological fallacy. The observed correlations between SDI and thyroid cancer burden reflect population-level patterns that may not signify causal relationships for individuals. For instance, the link between rising SDI and increasing incidence is indicative of macro-level determinants like healthcare infrastructure, diagnostic imaging accessibility, and registry completeness instead of direct biological risks to individuals, such as radiation history or genetic susceptibility. Therefore, these results should be viewed as a guide for setting regional policy priorities rather than a replacement for individual-level etiological investigation. Future research should focus on integrating individual-level data to examine the impact of environmental exposures, genetic factors, and lifestyle behaviors on thyroid cancer in AYAs. Conclusions This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the global burden of thyroid cancer among AYAs, highlighting global health inequities driven by socioeconomic development. We identified a critical transition point around an SDI value of 0.55, beyond which the disease burden escalates most rapidly. High-SDI regions exhibit a burden pattern indicative of potential overdiagnosis, whereas lower-SDI regions experiencing higher mortality likely reflect gaps in timely diagnosis and care capacity. Addressing this disparity calls for public health initiatives that are tailored to regional contexts, with an emphasis on diagnostic appropriateness, risk stratification, and avoidance of unnecessary detection in high-SDI regions, as well as strengthening diagnostic and treatment capacity in resource-limited settings. The development of evidence-based, age-specific screening guidelines for adolescents and young adults is essential to guide these efforts.
