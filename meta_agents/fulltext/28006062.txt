Introduction Breast cancer is the most common malignancy among women and remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide 1 , 2 . Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) is widely used to reduce tumor burden before surgery, allowing some patients who achieve pathologic complete response (pCR) to undergo breast-conserving surgery or potentially avoid surgery altogether 3 , 4 . However, due to the high heterogeneity of breast cancer, ~70% of patients do not achieve pCR after NAC 4 – 6 . Therefore, early and accurate prediction of pCR is essential, particularly for non-responders, to avoid unnecessary treatment-related toxicity and optimize clinical decision-making. MRI offers a noninvasive and sensitive tool for monitoring tumor changes during NAC, capturing detailed information on tumor extent and morphology 7 , 8 . Semantic features derived from MRI, such as tumor volume, maximum diameter, and sphericity, have shown potential for predicting pCR 9 – 11 . However, these conventional morphological features often rely on manual measurement, are subject to interobserver variability, and may fail to capture complex spatial patterns of intratumoral architecture. Therefore, an objective MRI-based method is urgently needed to quantitatively assess tumor characteristics, which may provide novel and valuable biomarkers for predicting pCR to NAC. Fractal analysis is a mathematical approach based on scale invariance and has been increasingly applied to medical imaging for quantifying structural complexity and spatial distribution patterns 12 – 14 . Two key parameters derived from fractal analysis are fractal dimension (FD) and fractal abundance (FA) 15 – 17 . FD reflects the morphological complexity of a tumor, while FA represents the degree of spatial occupation across scales, indicating tissue density and expansion characteristics. A recent study has shown that FD is negatively correlated with sphericity and positively correlated with tumor volume, suggesting its potential in characterizing tumor complexity and predicting pCR to NAC 11 . However, its underlying biological significance remains unclear. In addition, current evidence suggests that dynamic longitudinal monitoring of imaging biomarkers is critical for early prediction of treatment response 18 , 19 . Longitudinal fractal features may therefore be valuable for predicting pCR to NAC. The aim of this study was to analyze early longitudinal changes in fractal features and to evaluate their clinical utility in predicting pCR to NAC (subtask Ⅰ). Additionally, RNA sequencing data were integrated to explore the biological basis of early changes in fractal features (subtask Ⅱ). The final sample comprised 320, 78, 129, and 384 patients in the training, internal validation, and two external validation cohorts, respectively. One patient from the I-SPY2 trial was excluded due to missing RNA sequencing data, resulting in a genomics cohort of 383 patients (Fig. 1 ). Variance-components analysis and interobserver agreement for FD and FA Variance-components analysis demonstrated good reproducibility for both FD and FA, with patient-level variance exceeding measurement-level variance (FD: 0.056 vs 0.002; FA: 0.165 vs 0.011). The coefficients of variation between readings were 3.52% for FD and 5.08% for FA, markedly lower than the between-patient variation (FD: 278.15%; FA: 185.22%) (Supplementary Table 1 ). Interobserver agreement was also good, with intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) of 0.869 for FD and 0.857 for FA (Supplementary Table 2 ). Longitudinal analysis of FD and FA Differences in FD and FA were observed between pre-NAC and early-NAC stages across all four cohorts (all P 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 1 ). Additionally, both delta FD and delta FA showed differences between the pCR and non-pCR groups in each cohort (all P 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 2 ). In the training cohort, linear mixed-effects models showed reductions in both FD and FA from pre-NAC to early-NAC (FD: β = –0.657, P 0.001; FA: β = –0.153, P 0.001). FD and FA levels were lower in the pCR group than in the non-pCR group (FD: β = –0.973, P 0.001; FA: β = –0.170, P 0.001). The interaction between time and response was not observed for either FD or FA (FD: P = 0.933; FA: P = 0.428), indicating similar temporal trends across groups. Other covariates, including age, menopausal status, hormone receptor status, and HER2 status, were not associated with FD or FA (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4 ). Performance of FD and FA in predicting pCR to NAC Seven models were developed for predicting pCR to NAC. These included four single-timepoint models: (i) FD (T0), (ii) FA (T0), (iii) FD (T1), and (iv) FA (T1); two delta models: (v) delta FD and (vi) delta FA; and one combined delta model: (vii) delta FD plus delta FA. Across all cohorts, models based on early-NAC features outperformed those based on pre-NAC features, with FD (T1) yielding the highest performance among the single-timepoint models (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUC] range, 0.677–0.698). The delta models showed stable performance across cohorts, with AUCs ranging from 0.697 to 0.724 for delta FD and from 0.684 to 0.704 for delta FA. The combined delta model achieved the best predictive performance in both internal and external validation cohorts (AUC range, 0.732–0.752) (Table 2 and Fig. 3 ). Calibration curve analysis demonstrated good model fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow test, all P 0.05), and decision curve analysis showed higher net clinical benefit across most threshold probabilities (Supplementary Fig. 3 and 4 ). Subgroup and sensitivity analyses Subgroup analyses showed that the combined delta model demonstrated favorable performance for predicting pCR to NAC across all three validation cohorts. The AUCs ranged from 0.689 to 0.700 for the HR − /HER2− subtype, 0.720 to 0.778 for HR − /HER2 + , 0.707 to 0.736 for HR + /HER2 − , and 0.765 to 0.808 for HR + /HER2+ (Supplementary Fig. 6 ). Sensitivity analyses in the overall cohort (training and validation cohorts combined, n = 911) further confirmed that both delta FD and delta FA were independent predictors of pCR across molecular subtypes (Supplementary Table 5 ). Discussion This study evaluated the potential of longitudinal MRI-based fractal analysis for predicting pCR to NAC in breast cancer. The predictive model integrating longitudinal fractal features (delta FD and delta FA) demonstrated good performance across three independent validation cohorts (AUC range, 0.732–0.752). A nomogram combining delta FD, delta FA, and clinicopathologic variables (HR status and HER2 status) outperformed the clinical model in all validation cohorts (nomogram AUC range, 0.781–0.807; clinical model AUC range, 0.685–0.711; Delong test, all P 0.05). Moreover, delta FD and delta FA were associated with infiltration levels of activated NK cells and CD4 naive T cells, respectively, along with corresponding signaling pathways. Fractal analysis is a mathematical method for measuring structural complexity. It has shown potential in various diseases, including outcome prediction in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and treatment response assessment in rectal and breast cancer 11 , 12 , 20 . However, most studies have focused on two-dimensional analysis or lacked investigation into biological relevance. In this study, we used a three-dimensional approach to quantify tumor heterogeneity in breast cancer. FD reflected the geometric complexity of tumor boundaries, while FA captured the density and spatial distribution of internal structures 11 , 15 , 21 . We evaluated their predictive value for pCR in three validation cohorts and explored their potential biological associations using RNA sequencing data. Previous studies have used clinical factors such as Ki-67, HR status, and HER2 status to predict pCR to NAC, but these models showed limited performance with AUCs ranging from 0.52 to 0.71 22 – 24 . Radiomics and deep learning approaches have shown higher potential, with some studies reporting AUCs of 0.79 to 0.85 25 , 26 . However, these methods often rely on high-dimensional feature selection and complex algorithms, which limit interpretability and clinical applicability. In this study, we applied three-dimensional fractal analysis to extract FD and FA in an unsupervised manner. This means that the features were directly derived from the intrinsic spatial and intensity patterns of the images without using labeled data or model training. This approach is individualized and does not require large training datasets, making it less prone to overfitting compared with radiomics or deep learning. In three validation cohorts, single-timepoint fractal features achieved AUCs ranging from 0.660 to 0.691, demonstrating moderate diagnostic performance. Single-timepoint imaging may not fully capture the temporal changes in biomarkers associated with breast cancer treatment. Dynamic monitoring is therefore essential for early prediction of pCR. Consistent with expectations, models based on delta FD and delta FA achieved better performance than single-timepoint features in all three external validation cohorts (AUC range, 0.684–0.716 vs 0.660–0.691). Combining delta FD and delta FA further improved the AUC to 0.732–0.752. These findings align with previous studies suggesting that longitudinal analysis enhances model accuracy 18 , 19 , 27 . When delta FD and delta FA were integrated with HR and HER2 status, the nomogram achieved AUCs of 0.781–0.807, outperforming the clinical model. This suggests that delta FD and delta FA may provide additional value in predicting pCR to NAC and informing individualized treatment decisions. We used RNA sequencing data and GSEA to identify immune-related pathways significantly enriched in the low-risk group defined by delta FD and delta FA. These pathways included natural killer cell mediated cytotoxicity , intestinal immune network for IgA production , and NOD like receptor signaling , all of which have been associated with enhanced immune cell infiltration and improved treatment response in previous studies 28 – 30 . The low-risk group also showed increased infiltration of activated NK cells and naive CD4 + T cells, supporting its higher likelihood of achieving pCR. These findings highlight the potential of delta FD and delta FA as noninvasive imaging biomarkers that reflect the tumor immune microenvironment. This study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective and exploratory analysis, there may be selection bias in the study population. Although the results suggest that longitudinal fractal features show potential for predicting pCR to NAC in breast cancer, prospective studies are needed to further validate their clinical utility and to establish a more standardized definition of the early-NAC stage. Second, the joint analysis of MRI scans obtained at different NAC cycles as a single early-NAC time point may introduce temporal heterogeneity, potentially biasing the results. Moreover, fractal analysis was based on a single enhanced phase of DCE-MRI, which may not fully capture tumor characteristics. In addition, recent studies have demonstrated the predictive value of radiomics-based intratumoral heterogeneity assessment for pCR in breast cancer. Combining fractal features with heterogeneity-based radiomic features may further improve predictive performance. Furthermore, due to the absence of clinical T and N stage data in the I-SPY2 cohort, model performance could not be evaluated within specific T or N stage subgroups. Finally, due to the lack of follow-up data, we were unable to assess the prognostic value of longitudinal fractal features for long-term outcomes. In conclusion, this study developed and validated a longitudinal MRI-based imaging biomarker using fractal features to predict pCR to NAC in breast cancer. The nomogram integrating delta FD, delta FA, and clinicopathologic variables demonstrated strong predictive performance in external validation cohorts. Moreover, delta FD and delta FA were associated with activated NK cells, naïve CD4 T cells, and relevant immune-related pathways, suggesting their potential to reflect the tumor immune microenvironment. Further prospective studies in large-scale cohorts are needed to confirm the clinical utility of this biomarker. Methods This retrospective multicenter study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the institutional review boards of all participating institutions. Written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. The study adhered to the Standards for Reporting of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) 2015 guidelines 31 . Training and validation cohorts for predicting pCR to NAC (subtask Ⅰ) The study included female patients with biopsy-confirmed invasive breast cancer who received NAC between April 2017 and June 2024 at institutions 1 and 2 (institutions 1: Chongqing University Cancer Hospital; institutions 2: The Southwest Hospital of Army Medical University). Institution 3 was derived from the I-SPY2 trial conducted from May 2010 to November 2016 in The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA; clinical trial number: NCT01042379 ) 9 . In this study, MRI scans were obtained at two time points: pre-NAC and early-NAC. Patient data from two medical centers included MRI scans acquired before treatment (T0, pre-NAC) and after the first or second cycle of NAC (T1, early-NAC). The I-SPY2 dataset provided MRI scans acquired before treatment (T0, pre-NAC) and after three NAC cycles (T1, early-NAC) 9 , and was used as an independent external validation cohort to assess the robustness and generalizability of predictive model. The inclusion criteria were: (a) histologically confirmed invasive breast cancer; and (b) availability of clinical and pathological data. (c) availability of MRI images both pre-NAC and early-NAC. The exclusion criteria were: (a) incomplete NAC treatment; (b) poor image quality; and (c) unavailable surgery or no assessment of pCR. To evaluate the predictive performance of longitudinal MRI-based fractal analysis for pCR, patients from Institution 1 were randomly assigned to a training cohort and an internal validation cohort at a 7:3 ratio using stratified sampling. Patients from Institution 2 and Institution 3 served as external validation cohort 1 and external validation cohort 2, respectively. Genomics cohort for exploring the biological basis (subtask Ⅱ) The genomics cohort was derived from the I-SPY2 trial and used to explore the biological underpinnings of fractal analysis on longitudinal MRI. Patients underwent pretreatment whole-transcriptome profiling using the Agilent 44 K microarray platform. The platform-corrected and normalized gene expression data are publicly available in the Gene Expression Omnibus ( GSE194040 ) 32 . Patients lacking RNA sequencing data were excluded. NAC regimens and pathological assessment Patients at Institution 1 and Institution 2 received NAC according to National Comprehensive Cancer Network guidelines 33 . Regimens included taxane-based, alkylating agent–based, or anthracycline-based chemotherapy, administered alone or in combination. Detailed NAC regimens are presented in Supplementary Table 7 . Core needle biopsies were performed before NAC in patients at Institution 1 and Institution 2. Estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) positivity was defined as ≥ 1% nuclear staining by immunohistochemistry (IHC) 34 . Hormone receptor (HR) status was considered positive if either ER or PR was positive. HER2 status was determined based on IHC results: scores of 0 or 1+ were classified as HER2-negative, and scores of 3+ as HER2-positive. For IHC 2+ cases, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was used to assess HER2 gene amplification. Ki-67 expression was considered low ( 20%) and high (≥ 20%) 35 . HR and HER2 status for I-SPY2 patients were obtained from TCIA. All patients were classified into four molecular subtypes: (i) HR − /HER2 − , (ii) HR − /HER2 + , (iii) HR + /HER2 − , and (iv) HR + /HER2 + . The pCR was defined as the absence of residual invasive tumor in both the breast and axillary lymph nodes, with or without ductal carcinoma in situ (ypT0/Tis ypN0). MRI acquisition and preprocessing DCE-MRI was performed after intravenous injection of gadolinium contrast agent. Detailed acquisition parameters could be seen in Supplementary Table 8 . Preprocessing included N4 bias field correction and resampling with B-spline interpolation to achieve an isotropic voxel size of 1 × 1 × 1 mm. Tumor segmentation The DCE-MRI phase with peak tumor enhancement was selected for image segmentation. For patients with multiple lesions, only the largest lesion was analyzed 23 , 24 . The entire tumor region (including areas of enhancement, necrosis, calcification, hemorrhage, and other components) was initially segmented semi-automatically using the Dr Wise multimodal research platform ( https://keyan.deepwise.com , Version 2.6.2) (Supplementary Fig. 7 ). Manual refinements were performed by two radiologists with 5 and 6 years of experience in breast MRI, respectively. A final review was conducted by a senior radiologist with 9 years of experience in breast MRI. In cases of disagreement regarding tumor margins, the senior radiologist made the final determination after carefully evaluating the tumor region. All radiologists were blinded to the patients’ clinical and pathological information during the segmentation process. Fractal analysis To ensure consistency, all images underwent standardized preprocessing before fractal analysis. Tumor regions were segmented in three dimensions to generate binary masks, where a value of 1 denoted tumor and 0 denoted non-tumor regions. To capture the spatial heterogeneity of intratumoral signal intensity, a modified fractal analysis method incorporating signal-weighted box-counting was developed. After segmentation, the tumor volume was covered using cubic grids of varying side length L . The tumor mask was then covered using three-dimensional cubic boxes at multiple scales. The box side length L started at 2 pixels and increased in steps of 2 pixels. The maximum L was limited to 25% of the shortest axis of the tumor volume to ensure sufficient coverage and scale resolution. An arithmetic progression was selected to allow for a greater number of usable box sizes, particularly in small tumors, where geometric progressions tend to yield fewer scale points. This choice helped improve the robustness and interpretability of the linear regression by capturing spatial complexity across more levels. Zero-padding was applied at each scale to enable complete tiling of the image volume without leaving out any boundary voxels. For each scale, the mean signal intensity within each valid box inside the tumor mask was computed, and the average of these values across all boxes was defined as the signal-weighted box count N L . Reproducibility of fractal analysis Fifty patients (25 with pCR and 25 without pCR) were randomly selected from the training cohort. Tumor segmentation was performed twice by one radiologist with 3 years of experience in breast MRI at a two-week interval and once by another radiologist with 4 years of experience in breast MRI. A linear mixed-effects model with patient ID as a random effect and measurement time as a random slope was used for variance component analysis. Interobserver agreement was evaluated using the ICC, with ICC 0.75 indicating good consistency. Statistical analysis Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.0) and Python (version 3.8.2). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and non-normally distributed data as median and interquartile range (IQR). Differences in clinicopathologic variables among the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test, χ² test, or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Longitudinal changes in FD and FA and their influencing factors were evaluated using a linear mixed-effects model, with patient identification number as a random effect, pCR as a fixed effect, and clinicopathologic variables as covariates. In the training cohort, optimal cutoff values of fractal features for predicting pCR were determined using the Youden index. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify independent predictors of pCR. Model performance was assessed using the AUC, and AUCs were compared using the DeLong test. Calibration curves and decision curves were used to evaluate model calibration and clinical utility. Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess whether delta FD and delta FA remained independent predictors of pCR across molecular subtypes. In the genomics cohort, patients were categorized into high- and low-risk groups based on the cutoff value derived from the training cohort. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two groups were identified using the R package “limma”, with a p -value threshold of 0.05. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was then performed to explore biologic pathways associated with risk stratification. To further characterize the immune microenvironment, immune cell infiltration was quantified using the CIBERSORT algorithm implemented in the IOBR R package.
