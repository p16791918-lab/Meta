Introduction Breast cancer is the leading cause of cancer death among non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic women 1 – 3 . Breast cancer incidence rates are highest in non-Hispanic White women, followed by non-Hispanic Black women, and lowest in Hispanic women. Breast cancer mortality rates are higher for non-Hispanic Black women, followed by non-Hispanic White women, and lowest in Hispanic women 4 . Invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) is the most common ‘special’ histological subtype of invasive breast carcinoma 5 , accounting for 10–15% of all breast cancers. Multifocal or multicentric as well as contralateral diseases are more frequently found among patients with ILC 6 . Imaging evaluation of ILC is challenging. Due to lack of opacity and low rate of calcifications, the sensitivity of mammographic to detect breast cancer is lower in ILC. 7 . Other studies have reported lower ultrasound sensitivity to detect axillary nodal metastasis in ILC 8 . Therefore, breast magnetic resonance imaging is commonly used to evaluate the extent of ILC and to identify additional foci of disease 7 . ILC tumors are typically hormone-receptor positive, low grade, and less sensitive to chemotherapy. While this subtype generally infers a similar or possibly more favorable short-term prognosis than invasive ductal carcinoma, recent data suggests worse long-term outcomes in patients with invasive lobular carcinoma 9 , 10 . Although current treatment paradigms do not distinguish between ILC and the more common invasive ductal carcinoma, differences in biology and clinical features have led to increased research in this subtype and its’ recognition as a unique entity 11 – 13 . Trends in the incidence of ILC have not always mimicked the trends in invasive breast cancer that have been traditionally reported by US population studies 14 – 16 . This may be in part due to socioeconomic factors, environmental factors, and lifestyle risk factors having different effects in the formation of lobular neoplasms than that of other breast cancer subtypes 17 . For example, hormone-related risk factors are more strongly associated with ILC. When combined hormone replacement therapy became popular among postmenopausal women, the incidence of invasive lobular carcinoma increased at a greater rate than invasive ductal carcinoma 18 – 20 , and similarly after the decrease in hormone replacement therapy use, the incidence of invasive lobular carcinoma decreased more rapidly 21 . Racial disparities in breast cancer incidence, treatment, and prognosis have been reported by multiple authors; moreover, socioeconomic position may have different effects on such disparities 22 – 24 . Kaur et al reported a concerning increase in invasive breast cancer incidence among non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic women, particularly among those living in poverty and rural areas 25 . There is scarce literature about racial disparities in ILC incidence trends in the last decade. Given the different tumor biology and potential differing risk factors, we sought to assess trends in ILC incidence by race/ethnicity across age, cancer stage, and socioeconomic status subgroups in the United States from 1992 to 2019. Materials and Methods Study Population Cancer incidence data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program (SEER), 12 Registries from 1992–2019 were obtained (2021 submission). The deidentified data were determined to be exempt from informed consent by the Albert Einstein Institutional Review Board, which approved the study. We included non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, and Hispanic women diagnosed with ILC (International Classification of Diseases for Oncology [ICD-O], Third Edition histological codes 8520/3). Staging information was collected using SEER summary staging, and categorized as local, regional (via direct extension or regional lymph node involvement), or distant. Demographic and socioeconomic data was also collected including age at diagnosis ( 60, 60–69, ≥70 years), percent of each county living below the federal poverty line ( 10%, 10%−19.99%, or ≥20%), urban/rural county status, and the percent of individuals in the county with at least a high school education ( 10%, 10–14%, 14%). County urban/rural status was defined by US Department of Agriculture’s rural-urban continuum codes as follows; metropolitan counties (counties in metropolitan areas, codes 1–3) and nonmetropolitan counties (urban, codes 4–7; rural, codes 8–9; unknown, codes 88 and 99). Statistical Analysis Using NCI SEER*Stat (version 8.4.0.1), we extracted age-adjusted incidence rates for invasive lobular carcinoma per 100,000 women, 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and standard errors for each year from 1992 to 2019 by a combination of race/ethnicity (Hispanic, non-Hispanic Black, and non-Hispanic White) and stage, county-level poverty, county-level metro/nonmetro status, county level-high school education, and age. Age-adjusted incidence rates were compared to the 2000 US standard population using US Census Data (Census P25–1130). Trends for poverty, and high school education were available only from 2007 to 2019. We used the NCI’s Joinpoint Regression Program (version 4.9.1.0) to estimate longitudinal trends in age-adjusted breast cancer incidence rates from 1992 to 2019. This software uses piecewise regression to test for significant trends over time. First, it identifies the optimal number of joinpoints by using a Monte Carlo permutation, and then, the model segments the entire time period by those joinpoints and estimates an annual percent change (APC) for each segment, and then calculates an average annual percent change (AAPC) for the entire study period (1992–2019) 26 . We used a log-linear model with age-adjusted incidence rate as the outcome and years as the independent variable. We first ran the analysis on the entire study population and then by a combination of race/ethnicity and stage, poverty, county metro/nonmetro status, high school education, and age. Tests for significance were 2-sided with P values .05 considered statistically significant. Results From 1992 to 2019, 56,540 new cases of ILC were reported among non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and Hispanic women. The incidence rate was highest for non-Hispanic White women (13.52 per 100,000 people), with non-Hispanic Black women (8.60 per 100,000 people) and Hispanic women (7.52 per 100,000 people) having a lower incidence ( Table 1 ). The APC in the incidence rates from 1992 to 2019 are shown in Table 2 and their curves depicted in Figure 1 . In non-Hispanic White women, the age-adjusted incidence rate increased from 1992 to 1998 (APC 4.1%), then decreased from 1998 to 2004 (APC −3.2%), and then increased again from 2004 to 2019 (APC 2.6%). The age-adjusted incidence rate increased during the entire study period (1992–2019) for non-Hispanic Black women (APC 2.6%). Among Hispanic women, the age-adjusted incidence rate increased from 1992–1999 (APC 3.3%), then was unchanged between 1999 to 2002 (APC −6.6%, p=0.429), and then increased from 2002 to 2019 (APC 3.3%). From 1992 to 2019, ILC incidence rates increased across race/ethnicity groups, with the greatest AAPC increase in non-Hispanic Black (2.6%), followed by Hispanic (2.2%), and then non-Hispanic White women (1.6%). Stage at diagnosis The incidence rate of local stage ILC was higher in non-Hispanic White (8.41 per 100,000 people), than in non-Hispanic Black (4.94 per 100,000 people) or Hispanic (4.35 per 100,000 people) women. However, the incidence rate of distant stage ILC was lower in Hispanic (0.47 per 100,000 people), than in non-Hispanic White (0.71 per 100,000 people) or non-Hispanic Black (0.72 per 100,000 people) ( Table 1 ). The incidence rate of local stage ILC in non-Hispanic White women initially increased from 1992 to 1998 (APC 4.2%), then decreased from 1998 to 2004 (APC −4.5%), and then increased again from 2004 to 2019 (APC 3.7%), whereas it increased in non-Hispanic black women from 2002 to 2019 (APC 4.6%) and in Hispanic women from 2003 to 2019 (APC 3.6%) ( Table 2 Supplementary Figure S1 ). The age-adjusted incidence rate of regional stage ILC in non-Hispanic White increased from 1992 to 1999 (APC 4.6%), remained unchanged from 1999 to 2002 (APC −4.5%, p=0.509), and then increased again from 2002 to 2019 (APC 0.7%). Similar trends were seen in Hispanic women (APC for 1992–2002, 5.2%; APC for 2000–2004, −4.2%, p=0.549; APC for 2004–2019, 2.4%). Non-Hispanic Black women had increase in incidence through the study period (APC 2.2%). The age-adjusted incidence rate of distant stage ILC in non-Hispanic White women increased from 1992 to 2013 (APC 3.6%) and then remained unchanged from 2013–2019 (APC −4.4% p=0.118). Whereas non-Hispanic Black (APC 2.6%) and Hispanic (APC 2.2%) women had an increase in incidence rate throughout the entire study period. From 1992 to 2019, all racial/ethnic groups had an increase in the incidence rate of local stage ILC (AAPC for non-Hispanic White 1.9%; AAPC for non-Hispanic Black 2.1%; AAPC for Hispanic 1.8%) and distant stage ILC (AAPC for non-Hispanic White 1.8%; AAPC for non-Hispanic Black 2.6%; AAPC for Hispanic 2.2%). Only non-Hispanic Black women had an increase in incidence in regional disease (AAPC 2.2%). County-level Poverty For all county-level poverty categories, the age-adjusted incidence rate of ILC was the highest among non-Hispanic White women, followed by non-Hispanic Black women, and then Hispanic women ( Table 1 ). Trends in ILC incidence rates by poverty were available from 2007 onward ( Table 2 Supplementary Figure S2 ). The age-adjusted incidence rate of ILC in counties with 10% poverty increased annually by 2.3% in non-Hispanic White, by 4.3% in non-Hispanic Black, and by 3.6% in Hispanic women from 2007 to 2019. Similarly, the incidence rate of ILC in counties with 10% to 19.99% poverty increased for non-Hispanic White (AAPC 2.3%), non-Hispanic Black (AAPC 2.1%), and Hispanic women (AAPC 2.1%) from 2007 to 2019. The incidence rate of ILC in counties with ≥ 20% poverty increased in non-Hispanic White (AAPC 3.3%), whereas it was unchanged for non-Hispanic Black (AAPC −0.7% p=0.729) and Hispanic women (AAPC 4% p=0.074). It is noteworthy that the number of cases was small for all race/ethnicity groups in counties with 20% poverty, especially non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic women. The trends in ILC incidence rate across race/ethnicity and poverty subgroups were driven primarily by incidence trends in local and regional disease with non-significant contribution from the distant stage ILC ( Figure 2 Supplementary Table S1 ). County Metro/Nonmetro Status The age-adjusted incidence of ILC in metro counties was the highest for non-Hispanic White women, followed by non-Hispanic Black women, and then Hispanic women, whereas the incidence in nonmetro counties was the highest in non-Hispanic White women, followed by Hispanic, and then non-Hispanic Black women ( Table 1 ). In metro counties, the age-adjusted incidence rate of ILC in non-Hispanic White women increased from 1992 to 1998 (APC 4.0%), then decreased from 1998–2004 (APC −3.5%), and then increased from 2004 to 2019 (APC 2.6%) ( Table 2 Supplementary Figure S3 ). In non-Hispanic Black women, the ILC incidence rate increased from 1992 to 2019 (APC 2.7%), whereas it only increased from 2004 to 2019 (APC 3.1%) in Hispanic women. Overall, during the entire study period of 1992 to 2019, the incidence of ILC increased in all race/ethnic groups, particularly non-Hispanic Black women (AAPC for non-Hispanic White 1.5%; AAPC for non-Hispanic Black 2.7%; AAPC for Hispanic 1.7%). In nonmetro counties, the age-adjusted incidence rate of ILC increased for non-Hispanic White (AAPC 1.2%) and Hispanic women (AAPC 1.6%). There was no significant trend in non-Hispanic Black women, likely due to the small number of women in this group. In metro counties, the ILC incidence trends showed a larger increase for non-Hispanic Black women in local, regional, and distant stage as compared to non-Hispanic White or Hispanic women. In nonmetro counties, the trends were unchanged for all race/ethnic groups in local stage ILC; however, the ILC trends evidenced a larger increase for non-Hispanic White in regional and distant stage ILC as compared to their non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic counterparts ( Figure 2 Supplementary Table S1 ). High School Education Across all the subgroups of high school education, the age-adjusted incidence of ILC was the highest for non-Hispanic white as compared to non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic women ( Table 1 ). In counties with 14% high school education, the trends in incidence showed a large increase in Hispanic women (AAPC 2.7%) as compared to non-Hispanic Black (AAPC 1.6%) and non-Hispanic White women (AAPC 2.2%) ( Table 2 Supplementary Figure S4 ). In counties with 10% high school education, the ILC trends showed a larger increase in non-Hispanic Black women (AAPC 4.7%) as compared to non-Hispanic White (AAPC 2.4%) and Hispanic (AAPC 1.4%, p=0.304) women. In local stage ILC, non-Hispanic Black women had the largest increase in ILC in counties with 10% high school education (AAPC, 5.3%), whereas non-Hispanic White women had the largest increase in counties with 10–14% (AAPC 4.0%) and 14% high school education (AAPC 3.3%). In distant stage ILC, the incidence rates were unchanged across all race/ethnic and high school groups ( Figure 2 Supplementary Table S1 ). Age Across all age subgroups, the age-adjusted incidence of ILC was the highest for non-Hispanic White women, followed by non-Hispanic Black women, and then Hispanic women ( Table 1 ). In women 60 years, the age-adjusted incidence rate of ILC increased in non-Hispanic white (AAPC 1.5%) and non-Hispanic Black (AAPC 2.0%), whereas it remained unchanged in Hispanic women (AAPC 2.6% p=0.087). In women 60–69 years, the incidence of ILC increased in non-Hispanic Black (AAPC 2.8%) and Hispanic women (AAPC 2.0%), and it remained unchanged in non-Hispanic White (AAPC 1.7% p=0.105). In women ≥ 70 years, the incidence of ILC increased for all race/ethnic subgroups, with the largest increase in non-Hispanic Black women (AAPC 3.1%) ( Table 2 Supplementary Figure S5 ). In local stage ILC, Hispanic women had the largest increase in ILC incidence rates in women 60 years (AAPC 2.2%) and ≥ 70 years (AAPC 2.1%), whereas non-Hispanic Black had the largest increase in women 60–69 years (AAPC 2.8%). In regional stage ILC, non-Hispanic Black had the largest increase in ILC incidence across all age subgroups (AAPC 1.6% in women 60, AAPC 2.5% for women 60–69, and 2.4% for women ≥ 70 years). In distant stage ILC, Hispanic women had the greatest increase in women 60 years (AAPC 2.1%), non-Hispanic Black women had the greatest increase in women 60–69 years (AAPC 3.3%), and non-Hispanic White women had the greatest increase in women ≥ 70 years (AAPC 3.2%) ( Figure 2 Supplementary Table S1 ). Discussion Out study reports trends in incidence of ILC of the breast by race; evaluating patterns by age, cancer stage, and socioeconomic factors in the United States from 1992 to 2019. Our study reports that the age-adjusted incidence of ILC follows similar patterns as the overall invasive breast carcinoma 4 , with non-Hispanic White women having the largest incidence, followed by non-Hispanic Black women, and then Hispanic women. From 1992 to 2019, the incidence of ILC increased in all race/ethnic groups, with the largest increase found in non-Hispanic Black women, followed by Hispanic women. The stronger increases among non-Hispanic Black women and Hispanic women were particularly among those living in low poverty areas, metropolitan areas, and in women older than 70 years. Recent studies have reported that the incidence of invasive breast cancer has increased in local (0.9% per year) and distant stage (0.9 % per year) but decreased in regional stage (−0.7% per year) from 2015 to 2019 4 . Furthermore, Kaur et al reported similar trends in incidence of invasive breast cancer by cancer stage across race/ethnic subgroups 25 . The change in incidence in local and regional disease is attributed to earlier detection by screening mammography 27 , 28 , and the increase in distant disease is hypothesized to be due to improvement in stage classification since the rate of unknown stage has declined in parallel; however, a study of young women suggests that some of the increase in distant stage disease was real, especially among non-Hispanic Black women 29 . In our study, we found that the incidence of ILC across all race/ethnic subgroups has increased in local, regional, and distant stage from 2004 to 2019. Moreover, larger incidence increases occurred in non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic than in non-Hispanic White across all stages. ILC tends to have low density of tumor cells and lack desmoplastic stromal reaction which accounts for the difficulty in its detection on physical exam and mammography 30 , 31 . The sensitivity of mammography for the detection of ILC is only 57–79% compared to 63–98% for all invasive breast cancer 32 . These differences may explain why the incidence of regional ILC has not decreased as seen in invasive breast cancer. Moreover, dense breast tissue may have a stronger negative effect on detecting ILC than invasive ductal carcinoma (sensitivity of mammogram for invasive ductal carcinoma in dense or heterogeneously dense breasts, 60–86%; sensitivity for ILC, 11–36%) 33 . The stronger increase in ILC incidence for non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic may be explained by the effectiveness of mammography screening in these groups due to differences in breast tissue and other biological factors 34 . Prior studies have reported decreased incidence in invasive breast cancer from 2001 to 2004 that was largely attributed to the decreased use of menopausal hormones after publication of results from the Women’s Health Initiative randomized trial linking menopausal hormone use to breast cancer 35 . Kaur et al reported that around that period of time, the incidence of invasive breast cancer decreased in non-Hispanic White and Hispanic women, with no change in non-Hispanic Black women. In our study, we found that the incidence of ILC decreased from 1998 to 2004 only for non-Hispanic white women, while it increased in non-Hispanic Black and remained stable in Hispanic women. Our results show that a decrease in menopausal hormones had similar effects on ILC incidence trends as on invasive breast carcinoma where the decrease in incidence from 2001 to 2004 was seen primarily in non-Hispanic White women 36 . Furthermore, in the recent years, from 2002 to 2019, the largest increase in ILC incidence was seen in Hispanic women (APC, 3.3%) as compared to non-Hispanic Black (APC, 2.6%) and non-Hispanic White (APC, 2.6%) women. On the other hand, Kaur et al reported that the incidence of invasive carcinoma of the breast had larger increase in non-Hispanic Black women in the recent years from 2004 to 2017 (APC for non-Hispanic Black, 0.9%; APC for non-Hispanic White, 0.4%; APC for Hispanics, 0.6%). The subgroups driving the larger increase in ILC incidence in Hispanic women include, regional stage disease, nonmetro county, 14% high school education, and age 60 years. These findings are concerning given that the Hispanic population is growing fast in the US. The increase in incidence of ILC is consistent with prior reports 15 , 19 , 21 ; however, these prior studies evaluated ILC incidence trends in early 2000s and have not examined these trends within different socioeconomic and ethnic groups. It is suspected that the rise in breast cancer recently is due to increases in hormone related risk factors, such as decreased fertility rates, increased age at first birth, and obesity 37 . Our study suggests that across all ethnicity/race subgroups, ILC had a larger increase in incidence in recent years when compared to invasive breast cancer incidence trends reported by Kaur et al. The higher increase in ILC compared to invasive breast cancer could be due to the fact that hormone related risk factors tend to be more associated to ILC than invasive ductal carcinoma 38 , 39 . In addition, alcohol use may be more strongly associated with ILC than invasive ductal carcinoma 40 , 41 . This trend may become more significant given the recent increased use of alcohol among women 42 . Over the past 50 years, studies have reported an increase in obesity rates in the US 43 . Obesity is another known risk factor for breast cancer, though the association between obesity and breast cancer is less clear in premenopausal women. Due to lack of information in our database, we were unable to include body mass index in our analysis; therefore, we were unable to isolate the effect of obesity on our results. A recent study showed that obesity prevalence was inversely associated with median household income in White and Black populations at the population level of large US cities 44 , which highlights that the interplay of socioeconomic factors in the association of race and ILC can be complex and multifactorial. Multiple studies have shown an inverse association between physical activity and breast cancer risk 45 – 48 . Physical activity measurement was not available in our data set but further investigation between ILC incidence and physical activity among different racial backgrounds would provide more insight in racial disparities in breast cancer incidence and outcome. Another potential factor in ILC incidence rate differences among racial groups is hormonal uptake, which prior studies have demonstrated to have a strong association with ILC; however, this information was not available in our database. Our study results highlight that the incidence of ILC is increasing, but racial disparities in ILC exist and may be affected by socioeconomic factors; furthermore, the increase in the incidence of regional ILC warrants further studies to assess strategies to detect ILC earlier. Our study has certain limitations. First, we used the incidence - SEER research database, 12 registries which covers only 12.2% of the US population; however, this database provides longitudinal data from 1992 to 2019 which allowed us to assess inflection points in the incidence trends over three decades. Second, the socioeconomic variables in our study were collected from cancer registries which only include neighborhood factors (county-level data); therefore, we were unable to assess the effect of individual socioeconomic factors on ILC incidence trends. Third, the number of cases of ILC stratified by race/ethnic was small in some of the socioeconomic subgroups and could have affected the results of those analyses. Fourth, we limited our analysis to non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and Hispanic women; other ethnic groups were not studied. Fifth, a histologically homogeneous population was included to decrease confounding, and our analysis was restricted to classic ILC; therefore, our results cannot be extrapolated to other ILC variants such as pleomorphic ILC. Conclusions: The incidence of ILC continues to be highest in non-Hispanic White women. However, the incidence has increased across all race/ethnic groups, with the largest increases found in non-Hispanic Black women, followed by Hispanic women, especially those in low poverty areas, metropolitan counties, and age ≥ 70 years. In recent years from 2002 to 2019, the largest increase in ILC incidence was found in Hispanic women. The increase in the incidence of regional disease for ILC in recent years is concerning given that the incidence of regional disease has decreased for invasive ductal carcinoma. These ILC incidence trends warrant further research to determine potential causative factors and to appropriately target groups for screening. Supplementary Material 1
