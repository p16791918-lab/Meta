Introduction Breast cancer (BC) is the most common malignant neoplasm in women, accounting for approximately one-third of new cancer diagnoses and 15% of cancer-related deaths in this demographic [ 1 ]. In a lifetime, about one in eight American women will develop BC, with national incidence and mortality rates exceeding global averages [ 2 , 3 ]. Moreover, recent data indicate a persistent annual increase in US breast cancer incidence of 0.6% to 1%, highlighting an escalating public health burden [ 4 ]. The incidence of BC is likely influenced by population aging, lifestyle changes, and reproductive factors [ 5 ]. The associations between reproductive history and BC risk have been documented for several decades, with seminal studies establishing parity, age at first birth (AFB), and age at menarche (AM) as key determinants [ 6 – 8 ]. It is well-established that while childbirth may confer a transient increase in risk, it provides a significant long-term protective effect [ 9 , 10 ]. Generally, higher parity, earlier AFB, later AM, and prolonged breastfeeding are associated with a lower risk of developing BC [ 11 ], likely mediated by hormonal modulation [ 12 – 14 ], terminal differentiation of breast epithelium [ 15 ], and changes in mammographic density and genetic patterns [ 16 , 17 ]. However, much of this foundational evidence is based on older cohorts and small sample sizes. Over the past few decades, the United States has experienced significant demographic and reproductive shifts, including delayed childbearing and declining fertility rates [ 18 – 20 ]. These changes underscore the need to re-evaluate these associations in a large, contemporary, and nationally representative population to ensure risk models and public health guidance remain relevant. Furthermore, the influence of reproductive factors can vary by menopausal status, as pre- and postmenopausal breast cancers often have distinct etiologies. Age 50 is commonly used as a proxy to distinguish between these states [ 21 ]. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the associations of key reproductive and menstrual factors with the risk of developing BC using nearly two decades of data from a nationally representative US sample of women aged ≥ 35 years. We specifically sought to assess these associations overall and stratified by age ( 50 and ≥ 50 years). The findings are intended to provide updated, robust evidence to inform individualized risk assessment and public health strategies. Materials and methods Study population This cross-sectional study utilizes data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a nationally representative survey in the United States that captures information on the non-institutionalized population. NHANES employs a stratified, multi-stage, probability sampling approach to collect comprehensive health data through questionnaire interviews and physical examinations. Ethical approval is not necessary for this study, as participants in NHANES have already given informed consent. A dataset covering a 20-year period from 1999 to 2018 was acquired, comprising information on 101,316 individuals (Fig. 1 ). Of these, 20,298 female participants with documented pregnancies and deliveries were identified, all of whom had available data on BC diagnosis status and the age at follow up. Considering that women under the age of 35 were in the reproductive stage and the research factors were not fully exposed, 4292 individuals were excluded from the population. In order to address the interference of potential factors such as outliers and excessive pregnancy, 68 individuals with over 13 pregnancies were excluded. Fig. 1 Patient selection flowchart Exposure and outcome events The exposure factor was reproductive and menstrual history, obtained through the Reproductive Health questionnaire. This includes information on AM, number of pregnancies, number of deliveries, AFB, and whether the participant has breastfed a child. These variables were categorized into high and low groups based on optimal cut-off values. Whether a miscarriage had occurred was determined based on the difference between the number of pregnancies and the number of deliveries. The study focused on the occurrence and timing of BC as the primary outcome. Information regarding BC status and diagnostic age were derived from the patient’s self-reported Medical Conditions questionnaire, which was based on their previous doctor’s diagnosis and lacked supporting evidence. The age at BC diagnosis and the age at interview for individuals without BC were considered as the temporal markers for the outcome event, may existing recall bias. Among them, people diagnosed with BC were defined as BC survivors. To investigate short-term and long-term BC risks, we delineated age 50 as the threshold. Short-term risk defined as diagnosis before age 50, while long-term risks defined as diagnosis at age 50 or older. Covariates This study includes several potential confounding factors as covariates. Demographic and socioeconomic status variables obtained from the self-reported demographics data, including age ( 60 years, ≥ 60 years), race/ethnicity (Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Other Race [Including Multi-Racial]), education level (less than college, college or above), marital status (single, partnered), and annual family income according to the ratio of family income to poverty ( 1.3 [low], ≥ 1.3 and 3.5 [medium], ≥ 3.5 [high]). Lifestyle habits include smoking (yes/no) and alcohol consumption (yes/no). Comorbidities included information on whether the subject had hypertension, high cholesterol, coronary heart disease, stroke, or diabetes. Hypertension was defined as a self-reported history of hypertension or the use of anti-hypertensive medication or measured a systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or a diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg during physical examination. High cholesterol was defined as a self-reported history of high cholesterol or the use of prescriptn for cholesterol. Coronary heart disease and stroke were directly extracted from the self-reported Medical Conditions questionnaire. Diabetes was defined as a self-reported history of diabetes, or the use of anti-diabetic medication, or a fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL, an HbA1c level ≥ 6.5% from lab results. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters, and participate in the analysis as a continuous variable. Statistical analysis Statistical analysis was performed using R-Studio software (version 2023.03.0 + 386) and R (version 4.4.2). Data with missing values were excluded from the corresponding analysis. We accounted for the complex survey design of NHANES in all analyses using survey-weighted methods. The analysis incorporated stratification (SDMVSTRA), clustering (SDMVPSU), and sampling weights (WT20YR, the 20-year longitudinal weight designed for multi-cycle analyses). The “maxstat” package was used to find the optimal cutoff values of exposure factors, which served as the basis for classification. Individuals with zero delivery were categorized into the group with a later AFB. Continuous variables were expressed by the weighted mean and standard deviation, and the weighted linear regression of the survey was used for comparison. Categorical variables were described by the unweighted sample counts and the weighted proportion, and the weighted chi-square test of the survey was used for comparison. A survey-weighted univariate Cox model was employed to evaluate the impact of various factors on the occurrence of BC. Demographic variables, as well as significant disease and reproductive factors, were included in a multivariate weighted Cox model. Multiple weighted Cox regression models were used to evaluate the actual values of AM, number of deliveries, AFB, their respective groupings, and interactive groupings on the occurrence of BC and under different menstrual statuses. Model 1 only considered research factors, while Model 2 incorporated demographic and significant disease factors. Model 3 further included reproductive and menstrual factors beyond the study factors based on Model 2. We used Schoenfeld residual test to evaluate the proportional hazards (PH) hypothesis of the multifactor Cox model, and the PH hypothesis of the model’s global and all variables were all satisfied ( P 0.05). Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to explore the potential nonlinear relationship between the research factors and the risk of breast cancer occurrence, and the anova test was used to screen for the optimal number of nodes. To address potential confounding in the relationship between reproductive factors (delivery history and age at first birth) and breast cancer risk, we performed an Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPTW) analysis based on the covariates included in Model 3. The IPTWs were derived from a multivariable logistic regression model. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD), with an SMD of less than 0.1 indicating adequate balance. The Kaplan-Meier (KM) curve and Log-rank test were employed to verify and demonstrate the respective impacts on the age of breast cancer occurrence. Statistical analysis was considered significant at a two-sided P -value 0.05. Results Participant characteristics This study included 15,938 individuals for analysis, with a median weighted age of 54 years (Table 1 ). Among them, the proportion of BC survivors was 4.06%. In terms of population distribution, a higher proportion was found among the elderly, non-Hispanic whites, singles, and those with high incomes. BC survivors had lower BMI values and higher proportions of comorbidities. In terms of reproductive factors, there were no significant differences in the distribution of AM, pregnancy, delivery, miscarriage, and breastfeeding between BC survivors and healthy patients. The age AFB for BC survivors was later. However, BC survivors had a later AFB. The occurrence of BC mainly occured in the stage over 50 years old. Non-Hispanic whites were found to have an increased risk of developing BC (HR 1.688, 95% CI 1.132–2.516, P = 0.010), while no significant differences were observed among other ethnic groups. Higher levels of education and income were associated with an elevated risk of BC. Hypertension was found to be a protective factor (HR 0.799, 95% CI 0.650–0.981, P = 0.032). After adjusting for confounding variables such as age, ethnicity, education, socioeconomic status, and hypertension, their influence on BC risk was no longer significant. Regarding reproductive factors, a later AM, a higher number of deliveries, and an earlier AFB were identified as protective factors against BC, consistent with univariate Cox regression results. Various models were employed to assess the practical implications of reproductive factors on BC risk (Table 3 ). The risk of BC occurrence decreased by approximately 10% per additional childbirth (Model 3, coef = −0.08, P = 0.034) or per additional year of AM (Model 3, coef = −0.12, P = 0.001), while it increased by 4% per additional year of AFB (Model 3, coef = 0.04, P = 0.019). Notably, the risk of BC decreased by 31% for individuals with more than four childbirths (Model 2, HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.49–0.98, P = 0.038), and increased by 49% for those with an AFB exceeding 25 years (Model 2, HR 1.49, 95% CI 1.16–1.93, P = 0.002) (Table 2 ) (Fig. 3 ). Additionally, a 26% decrease in BC risk was observed for individuals with an AM exceeding 13 years (Model 2, HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.58–0.95, P = 0.018). A higher parity demonstrated a protective association across all four racial groups identified, with statistical significance observed in Mexican (Model 3, HR 0.22, 95% CI 0.09–0.50, P 0.001) and non-Hispanic Black (Model 3, HR 0.28, 95% CI 0.13–0.60, P = 0.002) populations. The categorization of AM influenced BC incidence, with subgroup analyses aligning with the overall patterns observed (Table S1 ). Linear relationship and validation with KM curves This study investigated the nonlinear association between parity, AFB, and BC risk using restricted cubic splines (RCS) analysis (Fig. 2 ). Parity demonstrated a negative linear correlation with BC risk (non-linearity P = 0.223) (Fig. 2 A), displaying a U-shaped pattern with a nadir at 2 births (HR 0.98, 95% CI 0.85–1.13, P 0.05), although this was not statistically significant. BC risk decreased steadily with increasing parity, showing a significant risk reduction at ≥ 4.58 births. A non-linear relationship between AFB and BC risk was not confirmed (non-linearity P = 0.258); however, an overall U-shaped trend was observed, with the lowest risk at an age of 21.20 (Fig. 2 B). Beyond 27.79 years of age, a significant increase in risk was noted (HR 1.15, 95% CI 1.00–1.32, P 0.05). The IPTW achieved excellent covariate balance, with all standardized mean differences below 0.05 after weighting. In the weighted Cox regression,, higher parities (HR 0.315, 95% CI 0.23–0.43, P 0.001) and younger AFB (Older AFB vs. younger AFB, HR 2.42, 95% CI 1.90–3.06, P 0.001) remained significantly associated with delayed onset of BC (Fig. 2 C-D). The protective association for ≥ 4 deliveries corresponded to a 32% lower risk. We observed racial interaction patterns—greater protection from higher parity among Mexican and non-Hispanic Black women and from earlier AFB among non-Hispanic White women, found that earlier AFB, particularly in women with higher parity, appeared more influential than increasing the number of deliveries alone. Our findings were consistent with established evidence [ 22 ]. The 32% lower risk of developing BC for women with ≥ 4 deliveries fell within previously reported ranges of 30%–50% [ 23 – 25 ]. The inverse association for earlier AFB aligned with international data, including large-scale reports from the MacMahon study and the Oxford Collaborative Group [ 26 , 27 ], and the protective association of later AM agreed with meta-analytic evidence [ 28 , 29 ]. We found no association between abortions and BC risk, consistent with large-scale syntheses showing no link [ 30 – 32 ]. Although we did not detect a statistically significant association with breastfeeding, prior pooled evidence supports a dose-response protective effect [ 27 , 33 , 34 ]; our null result likely reflected limited information on breastfeeding duration and coarse categorization in the survey data. Otherwise, this study demonstrates that earlier AFB has a better effect than increasing the number of deliveries. In a study on the relationship between infertility and the risk of postmenopausal BC, approximately 50% of the risk was attributed to having fewer children and a later AFB, with the AFB exhibiting a stronger mediating effect [ 35 ]. An epidemiological study has indicated a decrease in parity increases the incidence of BC, but this only accounts for 0%−4% of the variation [ 36 ]. Numerous studies have underscored that the protective influence of reproductive factors against BC development is significantly modulated by AFB [ 15 , 35 ]. Specifically, a younger AFB corresponds to a more conspicuous protective effect against tumors induced by pregnancy. Given the prevailing reproductive trends characterized by lower parity and delayed AFB, and their linear correlation with BC risk, promoting an earlier AFB (ideally around 27.79 years or even 25 years of age) appears to be a more viable strategy than advocating for increased parity (two births, or potentially up to 4.58 births). Early menarche and postmenopausal status have been linked to an elevated risk of breast cancer [ 37 ]. Interestingly, some research indicates that the association between increased AFB and BC risk is more pronounced in pre-menopausal women, deviating slightly from the age-related risk pattern observed in this investigation [ 38 , 39 ]. This study affirms the protective influence of earlier AFB among high-risk subgroups, such as women with early age at menarche and individuals over 50 years old, suggesting its promotion. While this study primarily explored the interplay of racial and reproductive factors, the observed trends remained largely consistent across black and white women. Although the impact of race on reproductive factors and breast cancer risk has been extensively documented, a consensus is yet to be reached [ 40 , 41 ]. Parity and earlier AFB may confer long-term protection via sustained hormonal modulation, accelerated terminal differentiation of breast epithelium, and remodeling of epithelial-stromal interactions [ 11 ]. A transient postpartum increase in risk has been attributed to mammary gland involution related inflammatory signaling, whereas longer-term protection likely reflects reduced cumulative exposure to estrogens and progestogens [ 9 , 42 ]. Later menarche similarly reduces lifetime estrogen exposure, thereby lowering the risk of developing hormone-sensitive cancers like BC [ 28 ]. These results support incorporating detailed reproductive history into individualized risk assessment and counseling, which can inform decisions about screening initiation and intervals. Earlier AFB, particularly among women with higher parity, emerged as a salient protective pattern. This aligns with evidence suggesting that later AFB may mediate a substantial portion of the risk attributed to lower parity [ 35 , 36 ]. From a public health perspective, these findings can help refine risk prediction models and reinforce the importance of collecting comprehensive reproductive histories in clinical practice. This study’s primary strength is its use of a large, nationally representative US dataset, enhancing the generalizability of our findings. However, limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design is susceptible to Neyman bias and, importantly, survivorship bias; because participants had to be alive at assessment, our results are likely biased toward a lower observed incidence and attenuated associations. Additionally, self-reported reproductive history introduced potential for recall bias, and our analysis focused on the risk of developing BC rather than its specific pathology or survival outcomes [ 43 – 47 ]. Future work should aim to refine exposure measurement, particularly for breastfeeding duration and intensity. Prospectively evaluating subtype specific risks and the influence of postpartum timing will be critical. Furthermore, examining gene and environment interactions, including the role of BRCA1/2 mutations, could significantly enhance risk prediction models in contemporary, diverse populations. Conclusion The reproductive history of women significantly influenced BC incidence. Factors such as lower parity, delayed AFB, and advanced AM were associated with higher BC risk. Encouraging a later AFB could be a viable strategy to mitigate BC risk and onset, especially given the prevalent adverse reproductive trends, particularly in high-risk populations. Further research is warranted to explore the impact of reproductive and menstrual factors on women’s overall health risks in the context of contemporary reproductive patterns and to inform potential adjustments to fertility policies.
