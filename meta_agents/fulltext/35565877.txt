Introduction Maintaining muscle mass and strength through proper nutrition and exercise is crucial for physical activity and reducing the risk of obesity, heart disease, and diabetes 1 . Sarcopenia, the loss of muscle mass and strength due to aging, leads to muscle wasting and decreased functional capacity 2 , 3 . The causes of sarcopenia are multifactorial, including alterations in endocrine function, chronic diseases, loss of proteostasis, and nutritional deficiencies 4 , 5 . FOXO (Forkhead box O) transcription factors, conserved from C. elegans to humans, are key regulators of aging and longevity. Inhibition of FOXO leads to premature aging, while its overexpression in muscle reduces protein aggregation by activating autophagy and the ubiquitin-proteasome system, delaying muscle decay and extending lifespan 6 – 8 . Muscle also functions as an endocrine/secretory organ, producing and releasing myokines (cytokines released by myocytes) into the circulation. These myokines mediate communication between muscles and other organs, including the brain, adipose tissue, liver, gut, pancreas, skin, or within the muscle itself 9 . Numerous studies have demonstrated that physical exercise stimulates the release of myokines from skeletal muscle, some of which may exert anti-cancer effects and potentially inhibit tumor growth 10 , 11 . Extracellular vesicles (EVs) represent another mode of intercellular communication utilized by skeletal muscle, although they remain relatively underexplored 12 . Aging alters the profiles of muscle myokines and EVs, as evidenced by differences in muscle-secreted factors between young and old mice 13 , 14 . These changes may contribute to tumorigenesis, as aging is a strong risk factor for cancer development. Notably, muscle-derived EVs containing specific microRNAs have been shown to suppress FOXO1 expression in the liver and improve insulin sensitivity 14 , further highlighting the systemic regulatory functions of muscle-secreted components. Sarcopenia is an independent prognostic factor for poor overall survival in cancer patients. It is significantly associated with increased mortality in most cancers, except hematopoietic and hormone-related cancers such as endometrial, breast, ovarian, and prostate cancers 15 . A prospective cohort study using Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD) revealed that patients with pre-existing sarcopenia had a significantly increased risk of developing cancers such as lung, colorectal, gastric, pancreatic, and esophageal cancers, but not prostate cancer 16 . This suggests a potential causal relationship between sarcopenia and certain cancer types, rather than a mere association. However, this hypothesis has not yet been explored in animal models, and the biological mechanisms underlying the increased cancer risk in the context of sarcopenia remain poorly understood. While exercise has demonstrated anti-cancer effects in numerous studies 10 , 11 , 17 , its efficacy in reversing or mitigating age-related muscle decline—and its subsequent influence on cancer risk—remains uncertain. Here, we show that young, healthy muscle suppresses aging- and oncogene-induced intestinal dysplasia in vivo using Drosophila genetics, whereas aging muscle fails to do so. These anti-tumor effects are mediated by muscle-derived EVs (hereafter referred to as muscle EVs). Consistently, mouse xenograft and Apc Min/+ models confirm that muscle functions as an anti-tumor organ by inhibiting cancer cell proliferation and inducing cell death through EVs. Sarcopenic muscles, however, exhibit reduced EV secretion and altered EV cargo, leading to increased tumorigenesis. We find that the tumor suppressor miRNA, miR-7a-5p, is decreased in EVs from aging muscles, resulting in failure to suppress TEAD1 activity in tumors and thereby promoting tumor growth during aging. This age-dependent change in EV secretion is linked to reduced expression of SDC2, a regulator of EV biogenesis, in aging muscles. Notably, activation of NOTCH signaling during exercise restores SDC2 levels and suppresses tumor growth in old mice. Together, our findings reveal a mechanism by which healthy muscles suppress tumor growth and how aging muscles lose this anti-tumor activity, highlighting potential targets for selective and effective cancer therapies. Results Healthy muscle represses age-related intestinal dysplasia in Drosophila Due to its short lifespan, Drosophila melanogaster is an excellent model for studying the impacts of aging on tissue renewal. In aged flies, the intestinal epithelium degenerates due to over-proliferation of intestinal stem cells (ISCs), resulting in intestinal dysplasia 18 . ISCs can divide asymmetrically to self-renew and generate two different cell types: transient enteroblasts (EBs) and enteroendocrine mother cells (EMCs). These two progenitor cells then differentiate into enterocytes (ECs) and enteroendocrine (EE) cells, respectively (Fig. 1A ). ISCs, EBs, and EMCs can be labeled by an Escargot ( Esg )-GFP reporter, which involves the expression of GFP under the control of progenitor cells-specific driver, Esg 19 , 20 (Fig. 1A ). To investigate how muscle influences aging-related tumorigenesis, we used the muscle driver mef2-Gal4 to express either FOXO or FOXO -RNAi in the adult muscles of young and old flies. We then monitored changes in the number of Esg-GFP -labeled intestinal progenitor cells. As expected, 40-day-old flies exhibited a marked increase in polyubiquitin staining in muscle tissue—an established marker of protein aggregation and aging 21 (Fig. S1A ). These aged flies also showed reduced climbing ability, consistent with age-associated decline in skeletal muscle function (Fig. S1B ). FOXO is a key regulator of muscle aging; its depletion induces premature muscle degeneration, whereas its overexpression improves muscle function across species 5 – 7 , 22 . In line with prior studies, we observed hyperproliferation of progenitor cells in old flies (age: 40 days), assessed by Esg -GFP and phospho-H3 immunostaining, compared to young flies (age: 3 days), confirming that aging induces intestinal dysplasia in Drosophila (Fig. 1B–D ). Interestingly, overexpression of FOXO in young muscle had no effect on ISCs, but its overexpression in aged muscle significantly suppressed ISC proliferation and rescued intestinal dysplasia phenotypes (Fig. 1B–D ). In contrast, FOXO knockdown in muscle further worsened age-related dysplasia (Fig. 1B–D , S 1C ). These results suggest that muscle health, modulated by FOXO activity, plays a critical role in regulating gut homeostasis during aging. EVs from young, but not old muscles, attenuate Drosophila ISC proliferation The muscle has been characterized as a major secretory organ capable of releasing various signals through myokines or EVs 23 . To investigate how muscle regulates ISC homeostasis during aging, we conducted a genetic screen targeting components of secretory pathways. Among the candidates, ALIX, a regulator of EVs that modulates exosomal cargo incorporation, was noteworthy 24 . We found that Alix -RNAi expression in muscles slightly enhances ISC proliferation in young flies and exacerbates gut dysplasia phenotypes in old flies (Fig. 1B–D ). These results indicate the potential roles of ALIX-mediated muscle EVs in suppressing intestinal dysplasia during aging. To test if muscle EVs affect ISC proliferation, we cultured young or old Drosophila muscles ex vivo with or without Alix -RNAi expression and purified EVs as described previously 25 , 26 . The average diameter of the secreted vesicles was ~100 nm, consistent with the size range of exosomes (Fig. 1E ). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed the morphology of the collected vesicles as exosome-like (Fig. S1D ). EV concentrations were reduced in aging muscles and in Alix -RNAi-expressing young muscles compared to young muscles, indicating decreased EV secretion from aging muscle (Fig. 1F ). To determine if muscle EVs influence Drosophila gut tissue, we labeled muscle EVs by expressing mCherry-CD63 in muscles 27 . Consistent with our observations, mCherry-labeled muscle EVs were detectable in the gut, with levels decreased in aging flies exhibiting intestinal dysplasia (Fig. S1E ). Notably, mCherry-labeled muscle EVs were absent in oocytes, suggesting tissue-specific uptake of muscle EVs (Fig. S1E ). Knockdown of Alix in young muscles further decreased muscle EV levels in the gut and significantly increased ISC proliferation (Fig. S1E ). These findings suggest that EVs from young muscles can suppress ISC proliferation, while EVs from old muscles are ineffective. Previous studies have shown that the Mef2-Gal4 driver is active in visceral muscles 28 , a type of smooth muscle found in the digestive tract, while MHC-Gal4 specifically drives expression in skeletal muscle 28 . Indeed, we detected reporter expression in visceral muscles using Mef2-Gal4 but not MHC-Gal4 (Fig. S1F ), indicating that Mef2-Gal4 -driven mCherry-CD63 may also label EVs from visceral muscle. To confirm muscle specificity, we used MHC-Gal4 to drive GFP-CD63 and label skeletal muscle-derived EVs (Fig. 1G, H ). Consistent with our previous results, GFP-labeled muscle EVs were detectable in the gut but not in oocytes, and levels declined with age (Fig. 1G, H ). Alix knockdown in young muscles also decreased GFP-labeled EV levels in the gut (Fig. 1G, H ). These findings confirm that skeletal muscle-derived EVs are selectively taken up by gut tissue and are diminished with age. To further examine the effects of muscle EVs on ISC cells, we overexpressed activated Yorkie ( Yki act ), the Drosophila orthologue of YAP and TAZ, to induces ISC hyperproliferation 29 , 30 (Fig. 1I ). We injected purified EVs isolated from young or old muscles into the abdomen of flies and induced Yki act expression in ISCs for three days (Fig. 1I ). Notably, Yki act -expressing ISCs in old flies exhibited more rapid proliferation than those in young flies (Fig. 1I ). Injection of 0.05 µg of EVs from young muscles significantly suppressed Yki act -induced ISC proliferation (Fig. 1I ), whereas the same amount of EVs from old muscles had no effect (Fig. 1I ). These results suggest that EVs from young muscles suppress ISC proliferation, whereas EVs from old muscles lack these effects, possibly due to reduced EV numbers or altered content in aging muscles. The antitumorigenic function of muscle EVs is conserved in mammals Building on our findings that muscle EVs can repress oncogene-induced ISC proliferation in Drosophila (Fig. 1I ), we explored whether these antitumorigenic functions are conserved in mammals. We purified EVs from the conditioned media of C2C12 myocytes (muscle EVs) and from C2C12 myocytes treated with 100 μM H 2 O 2 for 48 hours (damaged muscle EVs) using differential centrifugation to remove microparticles, followed by ultracentrifugation to collect EVs, as previously described 31 . Since oxidative stress induced by H₂O₂ is a hallmark of aging 32 , we assessed senescence and detected elevated β-galactosidase activity in H₂O₂-treated C2C12 myocytes, confirming the induction of aging-related senescence (Fig. S2D ). We also cultured myofibers from young and old mouse muscles and collected EVs from their conditioned media 33 . The sizes and morphologies of EVs from C2C12 myocytes and mouse myofibers were comparable (Fig. 2A, B ). These EVs were taken up by HCT116 (colorectal carcinoma), TFK-1 (cholangiocarcinoma), and LL2 (Lewis lung carcinoma) cancer cells, but not by N2a neuroblasts or LNCaP prostate cancer cells (Fig. 2C, D ), indicating tissue-specific uptake of muscle-derived EVs, consistent with our previous results in Drosophila . Treatment with muscle EVs from either C2C12 myocytes or mouse myofibers significantly reduced the number of HCT116, TFK-1, and LL2 cells, with no impact on N2a and LNCaP cells (Fig. 2E, F ). These results suggest a selective anti-proliferative effect of muscle EVs on specific cancer cell types. In contrast, EVs from damaged muscles had no effect on cancer cells (Fig. 2E ). EVs from old mouse myofibers similarly showed diminished or no effects on cancer cell growth (Fig. 2F ). Further characterization revealed that EVs from C2C12 myocytes, but not from H 2 O 2 -damaged C2C12 myocytes, suppressed the tumorigenicity of HCT116 cells in a colony formation assay (Fig. 2G ). HCT116 cells treated with muscle EVs exhibited decreased mitosis and increased cell death (Fig. 2H ), although muscle EVs did not affect cell migration (Fig. 2I ). These findings indicate that muscle EVs specifically target certain cancer types, suppressing proliferation and inducing cell death. The anti-tumor effects of muscle EVs are compromised when derived from damaged or aged muscles, suggesting that age-related changes in EV content reduce their effectiveness against cancer cells. Our results align with a recent study showing that pre-existing sarcopenia is a significant cancer risk factor in humans 16 , underscoring the importance of aging muscle in tumorigenesis. EVs secreted from healthy muscles suppress tumor growth in mice We next assessed the effects of muscle EVs on tumor growth using both mouse xenograft and Apc min/+ models. For xenograft experiments in aged mice, we utilized NSG mice over 12 months old due to their immunodeficient background, which is essential for human cancer cell engraftment. NSG mice typically have a lifespan of approximately 1.5 years under clean housing conditions 34 , with mortality increasing after 1 year. Importantly, immunodeficient mice, which lack a fully functional immune system, have been reported to exhibit accelerated aging compared to immunocompetent counterparts 35 , 36 . In line with this, we performed grip strength and wire hanging assays, which revealed significant muscle weakness in 12-month-old NSG mice compared to 3-month-old controls (Fig. S2A ). Histological and immunofluorescent analyses further showed marked reductions in muscle size, accumulation of protein aggregates, and elevated expression of cellular senescence markers in aged muscle tissues (Fig. S2B , C, S2E ). These findings confirm that the aged NSG mice used in our study display hallmark features of sarcopenia. We observed that subcutaneous implantation of 1.5 × 10⁶ HCT116 cells per tumor resulted in significantly larger xenografts in aged NSG mice ( 12 months) compared to young controls (Fig. 3A–C ). Muscles in the thigh, such as the quadriceps and hamstrings, undergo more pronounced atrophy with age than those in the upper arm or pelvis 37 , 38 . Given the diminished tumor-suppressive capacity of aged muscles, we focused on these highly affected muscle groups and administered intramuscular injections of GW4869 into the quadriceps and hamstrings of young mice (0.1 μg/μL; 50 μL per muscle every two days for 10 days) 39 . GW4869 is an inhibitor of neutral sphingomyelinase (nSMase), a key enzyme involved in exosome/EV biogenesis 40 . By blocking nSMase activity, GW4869 prevents the formation of ceramide, a critical lipid that affects membrane curvature and vesicle budding. Consequently, inhibition of ceramide synthesis disrupts endosomal membrane dynamics and broadly reduces the release of all types of EVs 40 , 41 . As expected, intramuscular GW4869 treatment reduced nSMase activity (Fig. S3A ) and markedly decreased ceramide levels in muscles (Fig. S3B ), confirming effective target engagement. Furthermore, GW4869 treatment significantly reduced EV production from muscle (Fig. 3B and S3C, D). Strikingly, inhibition of muscle EV secretion in young mice led to a substantial increase in HCT116 tumor size (Fig. 3A–C and S3E), accompanied by enhanced tumor cell proliferation, as indicated by Ki-67 staining (Fig. 3D, E and S3F, G), and reduced tumor cell apoptosis (Fig. 3D, F and S3F, H). These GW4869-induced effects were reversed by direct injection of purified EVs from the conditioned medium of C2C12 myocytes (muscle EVs) into HCT116 tumors or by adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9)-mediated overexpression of nSMase2 in muscle (Fig. S3C, H ), further confirming that the effects of GW4869 are specifically mediated through nSMase2 inhibition. To directly assess the role of muscle EVs in tumor modulation, we purified EVs from the conditioned medium of C2C12 myocytes (muscle EVs) or from myocytes pretreated with H 2 O 2 for 48 hrs (Damaged muscle EVs) 42 . These EVs were delivered into HCT116 or TFK-1 tumor-bearing mice via osmotic pumps or intratumoral injections (Fig. 3G and S4A). Notably, muscle EVs were detectable within both HCT116 and TFK-1 tumors following delivery, suggesting that muscle EVs can effectively enter tumor cells in vivo (Fig. S4B ). Administration of muscle EVs significantly suppressed tumor growth, reduced the number of Ki-67–positive proliferating cancer cells, and increased tumor cell death. In contrast, damaged muscle EVs had no significant effect on tumor progression (Fig. 3G–J , S4C–I). These findings indicate that EVs derived from muscle exert tumor-suppressive effects, whereas EVs from damaged muscles do not. To further confirm the role of muscle EVs in tumorigenesis, we used the Apc Min/+ mouse model of colorectal cancer, which harbors a heterozygous mutation in the Apc tumor suppressor leading to spontaneous intestinal tumorigenesis 43 . We administered PBS, isolated muscle EVs, or damaged muscle EVs to 12-week-old Apc Min/+ mice via osmotic pumps and evaluated the number and size of tumors (polyps) at 20 weeks of age. Muscle EVs significantly reduced the number and size of polyps in the large intestines compared to controls (Fig. 3K–M ). Damaged muscle EVs did not affect polyp numbers (Fig. 3K–M ). Similar effects were observed in the small intestine, with fewer and smaller polyps in muscle EVs-treated Apc Min/+ mice compared to control or damaged muscle EVs-treated mice (Fig. S4J, K ). These results confirm that muscle-derived EVs have an antagonistic role in tumor development in the Apc Min/+ model. In EVs from young and old myofibers, we identified 317 miRNAs, of which 65 were differentially expressed (FDR 0.1) (Fig. 4B, C and Supplementary Data 1 ). In parallel, sequencing of EVs from C2C12 myocytes treated with or without H₂O₂ revealed 449 miRNAs, with 122 showing differential expression (FDR 0.1) (Fig. S5B, C , and Supplementary Data 2 ). Functional enrichment analysis of target genes of these differentially expressed miRNAs indicated strong associations with key signaling pathways involved in tumorigenesis (Fig. 4D , S5D), suggesting that muscle-derived miRNAs may regulate cancer development by dysregulating components of these pathways. By intersecting both datasets (Fig. 4E ), we identified miR-7a-5p as a candidate miRNA that was significantly downregulated in both old myofiber EVs and damaged muscle EVs (Fig. 4F , S5E). This downregulation was further confirmed by qPCR analysis of EVs isolated from the skeletal muscles of young and old flies and mice (Fig. 4G, H ), supporting a conserved, age-associated decline of miR-7a-5p in muscle-derived EVs. Functionally, overexpression of miR-7a-5p through transfection of synthetic miRNA mimics—a double-stranded RNA designed to replicate endogenous miRNA activity—significantly inhibited cell proliferation in HCT116 and TFK-1 cancer cells (Fig. 4I ). Furthermore, intratumoral injection of a synthetic miR-7a-5p agomir—a chemically modified RNA designed to mimic endogenous miR-7a-5p for in vivo use—led to a marked reduction in tumor size and weight in both HCT116 (Fig. 4J, K ) and TFK-1 (Fig. 4L, M ) xenografts. These results collectively suggest that miR-7a-5p, enriched in young and healthy muscle EVs, exerts potent anti-tumor activity and may underlie the tumor-suppressive effects of muscle-derived EVs. miR-7a-5p targets Tead1 and suppresses tumor growth miR-7a-5p is evolutionarily conserved across species, from Drosophila to humans (Fig. 5A ). It has been described as a tumor suppressor and proposed as a prognostic biomarker in cancers 46 . A recent study has suggested that miR-7a-5p suppressed Hippo pathway by targeting the transcriptional cofactor Yki in Drosophila 47 . The Hippo pathway regulates tissue growth and tumorigenesis, with Yki (the Drosophila ortholog of YAP and TAZ) overexpression leading to increased cell proliferation and tumor formation 47 , 48 . YAP/TAZ, which lack DNA-binding capabilities, rely on DNA-binding partners like Transcriptionally enhanced associate domains (TEADs) to form complexes that drive transcription of downstream genes, promoting cell and tumor growth 49 . We observed that incubation of HCT116 cells with muscle EVs resulted in decreased YAP/TAZ/TEAD reporter signals compared to controls or cells treated with damaged muscle EVs (Fig. 5B ). A similar effect was noted using an alternative TEAD luciferase reporter, 8xGTIIC-luciferase (Fig. S6 ). These findings suggest that muscle EVs inhibit the Hippo pathway in mammals, possibly through miR-7a-5p targeting Yap/Taz. However, no miR-7a-5p seed sequences were found in the 3′-UTR regions of Yap and Taz in mice and humans. Instead, the miRDB database predicted Tead1 as a potential target for miR-7a-5p in these species 50 (Fig. 5A ). To validate this prediction, we transfected HCT116 cells with a miR-7a-5p mimic and assessed its regulatory effect on Tead1 via its 3’-UTR. The mimic significantly suppressed luciferase activity from a reporter construct containing the wild-type Tead1 3’-UTR but had no effect on a construct with a mutated miR-7a-5p binding site (Fig. 5C ). In addition, miR-7a-5p mimic reduced both YAP/TAZ/TEAD1 activities and Tead1 mRNA levels in cancer cells (Fig. 5D, E ). Reducing miR-7a-5p levels in muscle EVs—by treating C2C12 myocytes with a miR-7a-5p inhibitor—diminished the anti-tumor effects of the EVs (Fig. S7A ). Furthermore, overexpression of Tead1 rescued the proliferation defects caused by miR-7a-5p mimic (Fig. 5F ), confirming that TEAD1 is a functional target of miR-7a-5p and mediates the anti-tumor effects of muscle EVs. In mouse xenograft models, miR-7a-5p levels were elevated in tumor cells isolated from young hosts compared to those maintained in vitro, suggesting that a youthful host environment enhances miR-7a-5p accumulation in tumors (Fig. S7B ). In contrast, tumors implanted into aged mice exhibited markedly reduced miR-7a-5p expression (Fig. S7B ). Notably, miR-7a-5p was detected in primary mouse myofiber cultures but not in adipocytes (Fig. S7C ), indicating that muscle—rather than adipose tissue—is the likely source. These findings suggest that host age influences tumor miR-7a-5p levels, potentially through age-dependent delivery by muscle-derived EVs. To test this, we generated HCT116 and TFK-1 cells stably expressing a luciferase reporter fused to the Tead1 3′-UTR, implanted them into aged mice, and administered muscle EVs, damaged EVs, or a synthetic miR-7a-5p agomir (Fig. 5G , S7D). Bioluminescence imaging revealed that both muscle EVs and the miR-7a-5p agomir—but not damaged EVs—significantly reduced Luciferase activity (Fig. 5H–I , S7E–F). Moreover, treatment with the miR-7a-5p agomir decreased TEAD1 expression, suppressed tumor cell proliferation, and promoted cell death in HCT116 and TFK-1 tumors in vivo (Fig. 5J–M , S8A-B). Conversely, depletion of miR-7a-5p from muscle EVs impaired their anti-tumor efficacy (Fig. S8C ). Together, these results demonstrate that muscle-derived miR-7a-5p is transferred to tumor cells in vivo, where it targets Tead1 , downregulates oncogenic TEAD1 expression, and suppresses tumor growth. Reduction of Sdc2 expression in aging muscles induces defects in muscle EV biogenesis Our findings indicate that aging alters the content of muscle-derived EVs, including a marked reduction in miR-7a-5p levels in EVs from aging or damaged muscles (Fig. 4F–H ). To uncover the mechanisms behind aging-related EV biogenesis defects, we examined the differential expression of genes involved in EV biogenesis and secretion between young and old muscles. Previous research has identified the Syndecan (SDC)–Syntenin-ALIX pathway as a key regulator of EV biogenesis 51 . Syndecans (SDCs) interact with and recruit Syntenin and ALIX, regulating membrane budding and EV formation 51 . Although the number of SDC proteins varies across species - ranging from one in invertebrates to four ( Sdc1-4 ) in mammals - their structure and functions are highly conserved 52 . Our study revealed that expression of Drosophila Sdc (an ortholog of human Sdc1-4 ) is significantly reduced in aging muscles (Fig. S9A ). Overexpression of Drosophila Sdc in old muscles increased muscle EV secretion and mitigated aging-induced intestinal dysplasia (Fig. S9B–E ). Conversely, depletion of Sdc further diminished muscle EV secretion and exacerbated intestinal dysplasia (Fig. S9B–E ). These genetic data underscore the critical role of Sdc in regulating muscle EV biogenesis and release. Given that mammals possess four SDC family members, we next explored whether their expression levels change during aging. Analysis of RNA-seq data from young and old mouse muscles 53 showed that only Sdc1 and Sdc2 were significantly downregulated in aging muscles, while Sdc3 and Sdc4 levels remained unchanged (Fig. S9F ). Similarly, Syntenin and Alix mRNA levels did not differ between young and old muscles (Fig. S9G ). Our qPCR data further confirmed that Sdc1 and Sdc2 , but not Sdc3 or Sdc4 , were reduced in aging mouse muscles (Fig. 6A ). These results suggest that reductions in Sdc1 and Sdc2 expression may underlie aging-induced changes in muscle EVs. Specifically, decreased SDC1 and SDC2 levels likely impair EV biogenesis by reducing the recruitment of ALIX, whose mRNA levels remain unchanged in old muscle 54 . However, we were unable to detect SDC1 protein in skeletal muscle using an anti-SDC1 antibody (Fig. 6B ), a finding consistent with the Human Protein Atlas, which reports an absence of SDC1 in muscles 55 . Consequently, we focused on SDC2 and its role in muscle EV regulation. SDC2 protein levels was decreased in aging muscles (Fig. 6C ). Consistent with previous studies, Sdc2 knockdown in C2C12 myocytes significantly reduced muscle EV secretion without affecting EV size, suggesting that SDC2 is crucial for muscle EV biogenesis (Fig. 6D–F ). Exercise activates Notch to increase Sdc2 expression and enhance antitumorigenic functions of muscles Aging is known to inhibit the Notch signaling pathway, which plays a crucial role in muscle homeostasis. However, physiological stimuli, such as exercise or muscle contractions, can reinvigorate Notch signaling in muscles 56 . Notably, Notch activation has been shown to increase Sdc2 expression in smooth muscle cells 57 . Thus, we hypothesize that reduced Notch activity in aging muscles leads to decreased Sdc2 expression, impairing SDC2-mediated muscle EV biogenesis. Consequently, exercise may restore Notch activity, reversing these aging-related defects. To test the relationship between Notch and SDC2, we treated C2C12 myocytes with the Notch inhibitor L685458. Notch inhibition markedly reduced Sdc2 expression (Fig. 7A ), leading to a significant decrease in both muscle EV abundance and miR-7a-5p levels (Fig. 7B, C ). Reintroduction of Sdc2 restored EV secretion and miR-7a-5p levels, counteracting the effects of Notch inhibition (Fig. 7B, C ). These results indicate that Notch signaling is essential for SDC2-mediated EV biogenesis in muscle cells. Further supporting this pathway, intramuscular injection of the Notch inhibitor L685458 into young mice bearing HCT116 tumors led to reduced muscle EV secretion and significantly increased tumor size and weight (Fig. 7D–G ). L685458 treatment also lowered exosomal miR-7a-5p levels, elevated TEAD1 protein expression, increased Ki-67+ proliferating cells, and reduced apoptosis as indicated by Apoptag staining (Fig. 7H–K ). Notably, these tumor-promoting effects were reversed by AAV9-mediated Sdc2 delivery (Fig. 7E–K ), positioning SDC2 as a downstream effector of Notch signaling. Exercise is widely recognized for its ability to reduce tumor growth and confer protective effects against tumor progression, particularly in animal models 58 . Additionally, exercise has been shown to activate Notch signaling in muscle tissues 56 . In light of this, we investigated whether exercise-induced Notch activation could increase Sdc2 expression and restore the impaired anti-cancer activity in aging muscles. For this purpose, we employed a combination of treadmill running and wheel exercise in a mouse model (Fig. 7L ). Consistent with previous findings, aging muscles displayed lower Notch activity compared to their younger counterparts, as assessed by the delivery of AAV9-Notch reporter into both young and old muscle tissues (Fig. S10A ). Exercise interventions in old mice resulted in a reduction of fat mass, increased lean muscle mass, and enhanced Notch activity (Fig. S10A, B ). Notably, exercise enhanced muscle EV biogenesis, elevated exosomal miR-7a-5p levels, and suppressed tumor growth (Fig. 7M–P ), suggesting that muscle-derived EVs may mediate the tumor-suppressive effects of exercise. To further investigate the role of SDC2 in this process, we assessed Sdc2 mRNA and protein levels, as well as miR-7a-5p expression, following exercise. In old mice, exercise led to increased Sdc2 mRNA and protein levels along with increased miR-7a-5p levels in muscles (Fig. S10C–E ). However, when Notch signaling was inhibited via intramuscular L685458 injection or when Sdc2 was knocked down using AAV9-sh Sdc2 , the exercise-induced effects were reversed. These reversals included reduced SDC2 expression, decreased miR-7a-5p levels, lower muscle EV production, and diminished tumor suppression (Fig. 7M–P and S 10C–E ). These findings indicate that both Notch and SDC2 are essential for exercise-induced anti-tumor effects. In summary, these results suggest that the anticancer effects of exercise are mediated by the Notch-SDC2-muscle EV pathway. Reactivating Notch in aging muscles via exercise not only restores Sdc2 expression and muscle EV biogenesis but also enhances the muscle’s intrinsic ability to suppress tumor growth. Discussion Our study highlights that muscle EVs serve as potent tumor suppressors with tissue-specific targeting abilities. These EVs, which carry the tumor-suppressive microRNA miR-7a-5p, selectively enter specific cancer cells. Within these cells, miR-7a-5p suppresses TEAD1, a transcription factor crucial for cancer cell proliferation, thereby inducing cancer cell death and significantly reducing tumor growth. However, the progression of sarcopenia, characterized by the loss of muscle mass and strength with age, leads to a decline in muscle-derived EV production and alters their cargo. This diminishes the muscle’s innate tumor-suppressive functions, thereby contributing to cancer growth. The biogenesis and secretion of muscle EVs are regulated by the Notch-SDC2 signaling pathway. In young and healthy muscle, high Notch activity promotes the expression of Sdc2 , which in turn enhances the production and secretion of muscle EVs. As muscles age, decreased Notch activity results in reduced Sdc2 levels, leading to a decline in EV secretion and altered EV content, contributing to cancer progression. Our findings further demonstrate that interventions, including exercise, pharmacological treatments, or AAV9-mediated modulation of the Notch-SDC2 axis, can restore the muscle’s anticancer properties during aging (Fig. 8 ). Tissue-specific uptake of muscle EVs EVs have the ability to induce physiological changes in recipient cells, yet the mechanisms governing EV content delivery and their selective uptake by specific cells remain incompletely understood. Recent research suggests that differences in EV size or membrane composition may influence their recognition by recipient cells. For example, while micropinocytosis can capture isolated exosomes and small EVs, it is less effective for larger EVs or small EV aggregates due to size constraints 59 . Selective uptake of EVs by different tissues is also influenced by tissue-specific cell surface receptors and adhesion molecules, such as tetraspanins, integrins, lectins, and extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins. These molecules facilitate the recognition and docking of EVs on recipient cells that express the corresponding receptors 60 , 61 . For example, lectins like CD169 on the cell surface promote the localization of exosomes from primary B cells, and CD169 depletion in mice alters exosome distribution, highlighting the role of tissue-specific receptors and adhesion molecules in the uptake of specific EVs 62 . In our in vivo study, we found that muscle-derived EVs in Drosophila are selectively taken up by Drosophila guts, with no uptake observed in the ovaries. This provides direct evidence that EVs from one tissue can specifically target another. Similar tissue-specific uptake patterns were also observed in mammalian cells. EVs collected from C2C12 muscle cells were significantly taken up by LL2 (lung), HCT116 (colon), and TFK-1 (extrahepatic bile duct) carcinoma cells, but showed no uptake in N2a (neuroblast) or LNCaP (prostate) cancer cells. These findings suggest that muscle EV uptake is tissue-specific, likely mediated by unique markers on the muscle EVs that enable recognition by target tissues. A recent quantitative proteomic analysis of EVs from C2C12 cells and human iPS cell-derived myocytes identified potential marker proteins unique to muscle EVs 63 . Determining whether these proteins are involved in the selective uptake of muscle EVs will be critical for understanding the mechanisms behind this specificity and could open pathways to control this process in future therapeutic applications. Reduction of tumor-suppressive miR-7a-5p in muscle EVs contributes to tumorigenesis during aging Skeletal muscle functions not only as a mechanical and metabolic organ but also as a secretory organ capable of influencing tumor growth through its secretome. Muscle cells release myokines, cytokines, or peptides in response to contractions, which can modulate the tumor microenvironment via endocrine signaling 64 . These myokines have been shown to enhance anti-tumor immunity and inhibit tumor growth 64 . In addition to myokines, our study has identified miR-7a-5p, an evolutionarily conserved tumor suppressor microRNA, within muscle-derived EVs. In Drosophila , miR-7 suppresses yki , activating the Hippo tumor suppressor pathway and leading to reduced wing size 47 . Similarly, in mammals, miR-7a-5p activates the Hippo pathway by targeting Tead1 , thereby suppressing cancer cell proliferation and inducing cell death, though it does not appear to affect metastasis. This tumor-suppressive role of miR-7a-5p has been confirmed in various cancer types, including colorectal cancer, melanoma, gastric cancer, and hepatocellular carcinoma 65 – 68 . A decrease in miR-7 levels has also been linked to cancer development and progression 68 . These findings, along with our results, underscore the conserved tumor-suppressive role of miR-7a-5p across multiple species and cancer types. Aging is a major risk factor for cancer development. Our study demonstrates that muscle aging not only reduces the biogenesis of muscle EVs but also significantly decreases miR-7a-5p levels within these EVs. This reduction in miR-7a-5p contributes to increased cancer cell proliferation, resulting in larger and more aggressive tumors. However, re-expression of miR-7a-5p or supplementation with muscle-derived EVs can reverse the enhanced tumor growth associated with aging, suggesting that the loss of exosomal miR-7a-5p plays a critical role in this process. Our findings establish a direct link between sarcopenia and cancer, providing a mechanistic explanation for how muscle aging contributes to tumorigenesis. These results align with a recent prospective cohort study, which showed that patients with pre-existing sarcopenia have a significantly higher risk of developing cancers such as lung, colorectal, gastric, pancreatic, and esophageal cancers, but not prostate cancer 16 . Given that muscle EV uptake appears to be tissue-specific, it is possible that prostate cancer remains unaffected by sarcopenia because muscle EVs may not enter prostate tissues. Furthermore, the increased risk of cancer in sarcopenic patients could be due to a reduction in exosomal miR-7a-5p. Future research should investigate miR-7a-5p levels in muscle EVs from patients with and without sarcopenia to further confirm the role of miR-7a-5p as a mediator between sarcopenia and cancer. It is also worth exploring whether the decrease in other anti-tumorigenic molecules from aging muscles, similar to miR-7a-5p, may contribute to this relationship. The reduction in these protective molecules during aging could be a universal phenomenon requiring further investigation. Role of SDC2 in extracellular vesicle biogenesis and miRNA packaging Syndecans (SDCs), particularly through interactions with Syntenin and ALIX, have been identified as key regulators of EV biogenesis 51 . In MCF7 cells, SDC1 and SDC2 interact with Syntenin and Alix, forming a complex detectable in EVs 51 . This interaction guides SDCs into intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) within multivesicular bodies (MVBs), which eventually fuse with the plasma membrane to secrete EVs. Depletion of SDCs, Syntenin, or ALIX significantly impairs EV secretion in these cells 51 . Furthermore, cargo-induced oligomerization of Syndecans is essential for exosome biogenesis, and knockdown of a single Syndecan family member can disrupt overall oligomerization and EV production 51 . Consistent with this, our data show that knockdown of Sdc2 alone potentially impairs Syndecan oligomerization and disrupts EV biogenesis in skeletal muscle. Importantly, among the Syndecan family members, only Sdc2 expression is significantly downregulated in aging muscles, while Sdc3 and Sdc4 remain unchanged. This selective loss of Sdc2 expression leads to defective EV formation, highlighting its predominant and non-redundant role in regulating muscle EV production. Although Sdc1 mRNA is detectable in skeletal muscle by qPCR and RNA-seq, we observed no detectable levels of SDC1 protein via immunohistochemistry. This observation aligns with data from the Human Protein Atlas, suggesting that post-transcriptional or translational regulation might be affecting SDC1 expression in muscle tissue. Instead, we found that SDC2 plays a critical role in muscle EV biogenesis, particularly in aging muscles. As muscles age, the reduction of SDC2 directly impairs EV biogenesis and secretion. Importantly, the mRNA levels of Syntenin and Alix remained unchanged, suggesting that aging primarily impacts SDC2, leading to these defects in EV production. Additionally, Alix has been shown to interact with Argonaute 2, a key component involved in miRNA transport into EVs 69 . This suggests that the SDC2-Syntenin-Alix axis not only regulates EV biogenesis but also governs the packaging of miRNAs within these vesicles. Our findings further support this, as we observed age-related changes in miRNA populations within muscle-derived EVs, with Notch-SDC2 signaling playing a crucial role in maintaining miR-7a-5p levels. The reduction of SDC2 in aging muscle likely disrupts the interaction between Alix and Argonaute 2, leading to defects in miRNA packaging into muscle EVs. Influence of physical exercise on Notch-SDC2 signaling in muscle homeostasis In mammals, the Notch family consists of four receptors (Notch1–4) and five ligands (Jagged 1-2, Delta-like 1, 3, and 4) 70 . A recent study demonstrated that Notch2 is expressed in myofibers and that microvascular endothelial cells upregulate and secrete the Notch ligand Dll4, which activates Notch2 in muscles without the need for direct cell–cell contact 71 . Furthermore, Notch2 has been shown to upregulate Sdc2 expression in smooth muscle cells 57 . Our study corroborates this finding, showing that treatment with a γ-secretase inhibitor, which blocks Notch activation, significantly decreases Sdc2 mRNA levels. This suggests that Sdc2 is indeed a downstream target of Notch signaling. Exercise is known to confer protective effects against tumorigenesis 58 . Previous studies have shown that aging is associated with reduced Notch activity, whereas physiological stimuli such as exercise can enhance Notch signaling in muscle 56 , 72 . We hypothesized that physical activity could reactivate Notch signaling in aging muscles, thereby restoring Sdc2 expression, enhancing SDC2-mediated EV secretion, and ultimately contributing to tumor suppression. Supporting this, we found that exercise significantly increased muscle EV secretion via Notch activation, while pharmacological inhibition of Notch signaling suppressed EV secretion. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that Notch signaling can regulate selective EV secretion to influence cancer cell behavior, underscoring a broader role for Notch in EV-mediated communication across tissues 73 . The tumor-suppressive effects of active muscle may have evolved as a secondary consequence of its primary roles in tissue repair, regeneration, and metabolic regulation during physical activity. Muscle-secreted factors, including extracellular vesicles, contribute to systemic physiological homeostasis and may inadvertently suppress the growth of abnormal or transformed cells. While anti-cancer mechanisms are unlikely to have evolved through direct selective pressure, the capacity of active muscle to modulate systemic signaling may enhance an organism’s resilience to aging and cellular stress. This, in turn, could reduce the risk of cancer and other age-associated diseases as a beneficial side effect. Briefly, EDL and soleus muscles were dissected and digested with Collagenase/Dispase (Roche, #57028123). The muscles were then placed in DMEM with 10% horse serum and gently flushed with a horse serum-coated glass pipette to isolate single fibers. These myofibers were cultured in 6-well plates pre-coated with horse serum, in DMEM supplemented with 10% exosome-free FBS, 10% exosome-free horse serum, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 0.5% chick embryo extract (Life Science Production, #MD004A-UK), maintained at 37 °C, and the conditioned media were collected for EV isolation. Isolation of EVs from cell culture and myofibers EVs were isolated from Drosophila , mouse, and C2C12 myofiber cultures using a combination of differential centrifugation and size-exclusion chromatography. For Drosophila , EV isolation was performed as described previously 25 with minor modifications. Briefly, dissected myofibers were incubated in Schneider’s medium, and the conditioned media were sequentially centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min to remove debris and at 12,000 × g for 15 min to eliminate large vesicles. The supernatant was then passed through a SmartSEC single column (System Biosciences, USA) to obtain purified EVs. For C2C12 myotubes and mouse myofibers, cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 2% exosome-depleted horse serum (for C2C12) or 10% exosome-depleted FBS (for mouse myofibers). Conditioned media were collected and processed by differential centrifugation. Samples were centrifuged at 200 × g for 10 min to remove cells, 2000 × g for 20 min to remove debris and apoptotic bodies, and 10,000 × g for 30 min to pellet large EVs. The resulting supernatant was ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 2 h to collect small EVs, which were washed once in PBS and ultracentrifuged again at 100,000 × g for 70 min. The EV pellet was finally purified using a SmartSEC single column. To assess the effect of nSMase inhibition on EV secretion, mice were injected intramuscularly with GW4869. Myofibers were subsequently isolated from treated muscles, cultured ex vivo, and their conditioned media collected for EV purification and quantification as described above. A grip strength meter (Ametek, DFE-II) was used to measure the grip strength of each mouse. The mice were allowed to grasp the grid with all four paws and with just their two front paws, and they were gently pulled horizontally until they released their grip. The force exerted during each pull was recorded, and the hindlimb grip strength was determined by subtracting the front paw grip strength from the total grip strength (all four paws). Mice inverted screen hanging test The inverted screen hanging test was employed to evaluate the mouse muscle strength and endurance. The mice were placed on a wire grid enclosed by a wooden frame, which was carefully inverted and suspended 50 cm above a soft, padded surface. The duration the mouse was able to cling to the grid before falling was recorded. This test was conducted three times for each mouse, with rest intervals between trials. The average duration across the three trials was calculated. To quantify performance, a “holding impulse” was determined by dividing the average hanging time (in seconds) by the mouse’s body weight. Mouse exercise regime The exercise regimen for mice incorporated both voluntary wheel running and treadmill running. EVs miRNA-seq analysis Sequenced reads were aligned to mm10 using bwa 83 and quantified using featureCounts 84 against the miRBase annotation 85 . Reads mapping to different locations in the genome but to the same annotated miRNA were combined, and those miRNAs having less than 10 normalized counts on average across all samples were removed. DESeq2 was used to perform differential expression analysis 86 , and shrinkage of the estimated fold-changes was performed using Ashr 87 . miRNAs with an FDR 0.1 were defined as being differentially expressed. miRNAs were linked to their putative targets using TargetScan 88 , considering only conserved miRNA seed sites. Enrichment analysis Functional enrichment analysis was conducted using clusterProfiler 89 . Those terms with an FDR 0.1 were defined as being significantly enriched. The background/universe for these analyses consisted of all genes with a conserved miRNA seed site, as identified by TargetScan. Statistical analysis Results are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was assessed using Student’s t -test for two-sample comparisons or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD tests for multiple-sample comparisons, performed with GraphPad Prism. Differences were considered significant at p-values of less than 0.05 (*), 0.01 (**), or 0.001 (***).
