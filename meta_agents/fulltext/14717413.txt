Introduction Skin pigmentation is one of the most variable and noticeable phenotypic traits in humans. It is highly polygenic and, in recent years, the number of identified genes and allelic variants that affect constitutive pigmentation has significantly increased 1 . Nevertheless, the genetic basis of skin pigmentation has not yet been fully unraveled. Skin pigmentation is determined by the presence of melanin pigments, brown-black eumelanin, and yellow-red pheomelanin. Melanin is produced in distinct, specialized membrane-bound organelles called melanosomes by melanocytes, neural crest-derived cells migrating as melanoblasts, during embryogenesis, into the epidermis. Melanogenesis is a complex process involving several enzymes, structural proteins, and molecules that regulate melanocytes development, melanosomes biogenesis and survival, melanin synthesis and melanosomes transfer to approximatively 40 keratinocytes at the dermal-epidermal junction, establishing the so-called epidermal melanin unit 2 , 3 . Once transferred, the majority of melanosomes are found in keratinocytes of the basal layer and localized predominantly over the nuclei to protect the cells from DNA damage induced by ultraviolet (UV) exposure 4 , 5 . Variations in constitutive pigmentation are attributed to differences in melanin content as well as in the size, amount, distribution, and fate of melanosomes within the epidermis rather than to differences in melanocyte density, as the latter is quite comparable for a given anatomical site among skin of variable pigmentation. Skin color diversity has implications in its response to sun exposure 6 . UV radiation causes acute consequences on the skin such as erythema 7 , 8 and DNA damage 9 as well as chronic consequences such as skin cancer 10 , 11 , photoaging 11 – 13 and pigmentary disorders 14 , 15 . These consequences are highly dependent on skin color type. Because melanin provides protection against UV exposure, light skin types are more susceptible to photodamage. Indeed, the lighter skin types showed greater susceptibility to erythema, as the Minimal Erythema Dose (MED) is lower for light skin and higher for dark skin 7 , 8 and, after one MED, the DNA damage is greater in lightly pigmented skin compared to darkly pigmented skin types 4 , 9 . Epidemiological data on skin cancers have shown greater incidence of basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma and melanoma in light-skinned individuals as compared to dark-skinned ones 11 , 16 , 17 . The onset of the clinical signs of photoaging such as wrinkle formation and solar elastosis is earlier in lighter skin types 11 , 18 . On the other hand, pigmentary disorders, such as post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, melasma and actinic lentigo, are more frequent in moderately pigmented skin (Asian skin) and pigmented skin (African or African American skin) 14 , 19 , 20 . In previous studies we showed on ex vivo that skin color, objectively measured by the Individual Typology Angle (ITA), significantly influences the skin’s response to UV radiation. Lighter skin types (Very Light, Light, Intermediate, and Tan) exhibited a lower Biologically Effective Dose (BED) - equivalent to the MED in vivo - based on the induction of sunburn cells, indicating greater sensitivity to UV radiation 21 . These lighter skin types also showed more UV-induced DNA damage, cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs), in both basal and suprabasal skin layers, explaining their increasing risk of basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma 21 . Furthermore, melanocytes in lighter skins showed more significant DNA damage after UV exposure, potentially contributing to a higher risk of melanoma 22 . Darker skin types (Brown and Dark) exhibited higher BED values and less DNA damage, predominantly confined to the suprabasal layers, suggesting greater UV protection. These data complete or were confirmed by others on ex vivo samples of different Fitzpatrick’s phototypes or ethnic origin 5 , 9 . Chemical analysis revealed a correlation between ITA and melanin content, particularly eumelanin, with lighter skin types having lower eumelanin levels, which explains their increased sensitivity to UVB radiation 23 , 24 . The genetic basis of constitutive skin pigmentation is complex. Only a few genes, not directly linked to melanogenesis, have been identified in skin of different ethnic origin or Fitzpatrick’s phototypes. Microarray analysis revealed similar gene expression in Asian and Caucasian skin, distinct from African skin 25 . Key differentiating genes included NINL (Ninein Like; involved in microtubule organization) and S100A4 (S100 Calcium Binding Protein A4; involved in cell cycle regulation). A meta-analysis of microarray data from various hyperpigmentation conditions identified known pigmentation genes 26 . In addition, a microarray analysis of dermal fibroblasts of different phototypes demonstrated that the fibroblast-derived factor NRG-1 (Neuregulin-1), required for neurons and dendritic development, is highly expressed in fibroblasts from dark skin 27 . Another microarray study showed that increased DKK1 (Dickkopf-related protein 1) expression in palmoplantar fibroblasts inhibits melanocyte activity, explaining the lighter pigmentation in these areas 28 . RNA-Seq analysis of lightly and darkly pigmented melanocytes identified 15 differentially expressed genes, including the melanocyte-specific gene SLC45A2 (Solute carrier family 45 member 2) 29 and a recent CRISPR-Cas9 screen identified novel pigmentation genes 30 . A significant limitation of existing research is the reliance on skin samples or cells from individuals of different ethnic origins or phototypes, rather than using objectively measured constitutive pigmentation levels. This makes it difficult to isolate the specific genetic factors responsible for variations in skin color. Our study addresses a key limitation of previous research by using skin samples with instrumentally measured variable pigmentation. Our objective was to obtain a global gene expression profile from skin of variable pigmentation to identify new biological pathways which could be involved in the pigmentation phenotype. We performed a next generation NGS RNA sequencing study on ex vivo epidermis with different levels of pigmentation. We used the ITA an objective, colorimetric measure – to define skin pigmentation instead of relying on ethnicity or Fitzpatrick phototypes, which are inadequate descriptors of skin color. Gene expression analysis combined with machine learning (ML) allowed us to identify novel biomarkers and biological pathways associated with pigmentation phenotypes. Key genes identified were validated using qPCR and immunolabelling. These findings could be valuable for developing tailored cosmetic products based on objective skin color, paving the way for future personalized skincare strategies. Results and discussion Twenty-one ex-vivo skin samples with variable skin color types were objectively classified into six groups: Very light, Light, Intermediate, Tan, Brown, and Dark based on the measurement of their Individual Typology Angle (ITA), determined through colorimetric parameters of the L*a*b* system (CIELAB 1976). The samples were further classified into 3 larger groups: ‘Light’ Group with 8 specimens (2 Very light and 6 Light skin types); ‘Intermediate’ Group with 4 specimens (3 Intermediate and 1 Tan); and ‘Dark’ Group with 9 specimens (5 Brown and 4 Dark) (Table 1 ). Following this, pathway analysis and identification of upstream transcriptional regulators were conducted using GO, KEGG databases 31 , 32 , and Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA). The resulting lists of associated terms, pathways, and upstream regulators are provided in Fig. 2 and Supplementary Tables 3–5. The top enriched molecular functions included ‘pigmentation and melanin biosynthetic process’; ‘lipids and arachidonic acid metabolism’, ‘Interferon gamma (IFN-γ) signaling’, ‘detoxification and response to xenobiotics’; ‘nervous system’, ‘hyaluronan metabolism’ and ‘insulin signaling’. Interestingly, both manual curation and multiple tool-based analyses converged on the same robust set of newly enriched terms and pathways. Given the comprehensive nature of these findings, our subsequent in-depth analysis prioritized the pathways that are highly relevant to skin biology. This selective focus allowed us to not only reinforce understanding of already known pathways but also to highlight novel mechanisms, which are described in detail in the following sections. Pigmentation melanin biosynthetic process Manual sorting revealed 17 differentially expressed pigmentation/melanogenesis-associated genes between Dark and Light skin groups. These included canonical pigmentation genes, such as TYRP1 (Tyrosinase related protein 1), GPR143 (G protein-coupled receptor 143), EDNRB (Endothelin receptor type B), PMEL (premelanosome protein), TYR (Tyrosinase), MLANA (Melanoma antigen recognized by T cells 1 or MART-1), SLC24A5 (Solute carrier family 24 member 5), PAX3 (Paired Box 3), KIT (KIT Proto-Oncogene, Receptor Tyrosine Kinase), OCA2 (Oculocutaneous Albinism type 2), RAB32 (Ras-related protein Rab-32) 33 but also TRPS1 (Transcriptional repressor GATA binding 1), DBNDD1 (Dysbindin Domain Containing 1), PSEN2 (Presenilin-2), MEF2C (Myocyte-specific enhancer factor 2 C), CLEC12B (C-Type Lectin Domain Family 12 Member B) and ODC1 (Ornithine-decarboxylase 1) (Table 2 ). Reinforcing this observation, IPA analysis identified MITF (Melanocyte inducing transcription factor), a key transcription regulator of melanogenesis 34 , as the most activated upstream regulator (activation z-score of + 3.49) ( Supplementary Table 6 ). qPCR on the original RNA samples validated the increased expression of TYRP1 , PMEL , EDNRB , MLANA , and GPR143 . While KIT and TYR also showed a tendency towards increased expression in Dark skin, these changes were not statistically significant (Fig. 3 a). Furthermore, GPR143 protein expression was confirmed by immunostaining corresponding skin sections (Fig. 3 b-c). Collectively, these findings confirm enhanced melanogenesis in darkly pigmented skin, consistent with established knowledge 35 . Further supporting this, we observed upregulation of TRPS1 and DBNDD1. TRPS1, is a transcription factor impacting the development of various tissues, including kidney, bone and hair follicles 36 , recently shown to be implicated in pigmentation within African descents 37 . TRPS1 and DBNDD1, were both shown to be associated with tanning ability in a genome-wide association study in Europeans 38 , further supporting these findings. While this could be anticipated, this study represents, to our knowledge, the first comprehensive characterization of differentially expressed pigmentation/melanogenesis-associated genes within this context. Antioxidant, detoxification, and stress response Among the top enriched pathways, we also observed differential expression of genes related to antioxidant activity, detoxification, and stress response. Specifically, AKR1B10 (Aldo-Keto Reductase Family 1 Member B10), GSTM3 (Glutathione S-transferase mu 3), UGT1A7 (UDP glucuronosyltransferase family 1 member A7), GLRX (Glutaredoxin), BCHE (Butyrylcholinesterase), GSTA3 (Glutathione S-Transferase Alpha 3), CYGB (Cytoglobin), and GMPR (Guanosine Monophosphate Reductase) were upregulated in Dark skin compared to Light skin (Table 3 ). Conversely, TXNIP (Thioredoxin Interacting Protein), a key modulator of thioredoxin (TXN) activity 39 was down-regulated in Dark skin. The overexpression of genes related to antioxidant, detoxification and stress responses in Dark skin could suggest enhanced defense against environmental stressors in darker skin types. Of particular interest, AKR1B10 , an enzyme involved in carbonyl and xenobiotic reduction 41 and lipid metabolism, has been implicated in several skin diseases. Its upregulation has been associated with dysregulated lipid metabolism, inflammation, keratinocyte proliferation in psoriasis 42 , 43 , keratinocyte differentiation in atopic dermatitis 44 , immune pathways in vitiligo 45 , and abnormal scar formation in keloids 46 . These findings underscore the potential role of AKR1B10 in various inflammatory skin disorders. Arachidonic acid, a membrane-bound fatty acid precursor to prostaglandins and leukotrienes, plays a role in skin inflammation and interacts with melanocytes through prostaglandin receptors 47 – 49 . Our data indicated increased expression in Dark skin of enzymes involved in AA release ( HRASLS1 ) and its conversion to prostaglandins H2 ( PTGS2 ), D2 ( PTGDS ), and ultimately F2α ( AKR1C3 ) (Fig. 4 d), suggesting elevated prostaglandin F2α production. This aligns with previous studies demonstrating PGF2α’s role in stimulating melanocyte dendrite formation and tyrosinase activity without affecting proliferation 48 – 50 . Diacylglycerol, a key second messenger in various signaling cascades, can be derived from membrane phosphatidylcholine either directly via phosphatidylcholine (PC)-PLC or indirectly through phospholipase D1 (PLD1) and phosphatidic acid phosphatase (PLPP1). DAG can then be hydrolyzed by diacylglycerol lipases like DAGLA (diacylglycerol lipase alpha) (Fig. 4 e). Our analysis revealed an inverse relationship between PLPP1 and DAGLA expression, with PLPP1 upregulated in darker skin, suggesting potentially higher DAG levels. This aligns with previous research indicating DAG’s role in pigmentation, where it has been shown to increase melanin content in cultured melanocytes and guinea pig skin 51 , 52 . But the link between impaired IFN-γ signaling and darker skin pigmentation is complex and not fully understood. Datasets comparative analyses To further contextualize our findings, we compared our gene list with two published datasets. First, we re-analyzed microarray data from a study investigating pigmentation differences across Caucasian, Asian, and African skin 25 using GEO2R (( https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/geo2r/ ), focusing on differentially expressed genes (adjusted p -value 5%, |fold-change| ≥ 1.25) between African and Caucasian skin. While the original study did not find a clear association between ethnicity/pigmentation and known pigmentation genes, it highlighted novel genes like NINL and S100A4 . Our comparison revealed 28 common genes (Table 6 a), with 18 out of 28 concordantly modulated, including genes related to antioxidant defense ( GSTM3 ), cytoskeleton ( TNS2 , SHROOM4 ), vesicle trafficking ( PSPH , DENND3 ), and lipid metabolism ( ABHD3 , CYP2E1 ). Second, we compared our data with a meta-analysis of five microarray datasets examining hyperpigmentation across various models: UV exposure, long-lasting pigmentation, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, age spot, and ethnic skin 26 , using their provided gene list. This comparison identified 70 common genes (Table 6 b), of which 60 were concordantly modulated, including several directly linked to melanogenesis ( TYRP1 , TYR , PAX3 , EDNRB , GPR143 , TRPM1 ) and others implicated in our identified pathways, such as AKR1B10 (antioxidant) and DEFA6 (antimicrobial). These comparisons with existing datasets, despite differences in methodologies and specific pigmentation models, demonstrate overlap and consistent direction of modulation with our findings, reinforcing the robustness of our identified gene set and highlighting the complex interplay of pathways involved in skin pigmentation. Correlation between gene expression and ITA To leverage the full potential of gene expression data, effectively analyze multivariate relationships and continuous variables like ITA, a powerful complementary approach combining differential expression analysis with machine learning (ML) models was further employed. Using processed data from all individuals across all skin color groups, namely Light and Dark but also Intermediate groups, ML analysis identified a 21-gene predictive model using a forward-backward search, a stepwise variable selection method that iteratively adds and removes variables to optimize the model’s performance (Table 7 ). Only five genes TBC1D3D (TBC1 Domain Family Member 3D ) , TYRP1 , PMEL , PMP22 (Peripheral myelin protein-22), and GSTM3 bold in Table 7 ) overlapped with the differentially expressed genes identified previously. While the model achieved a strong average correlation coefficient of 87.4%, its high standard deviation (19.4%) indicated instability and difficulty generalizing across the limited sample size. Conclusions This study provides a valuable foundation for understanding the complex genetic architecture of constitutive skin pigmentation. By utilizing the objective Individual Typology Angle (ITA) for skin color classification, we generated comprehensive gene expression profiles across a spectrum of pigmentation levels. Our findings reveal both expected and novel biological pathways associated with constitutive skin color, including pigmentation pathways, lipid metabolism, interferon-gamma signaling, and antioxidant/detoxification processes. We validated the differential expression of key genes using qPCR and immunolabelling. Furthermore, the machine learning approach, combined with careful data visualization and biological interpretation, successfully identified a robust set of candidate genes for predicting ITA, including both established pigmentation-related genes and novel candidates, thereby expanding our understanding of the complex genetic factors influencing this important trait. This integrated strategy proved to be a valuable complement to traditional differential expression analysis, offering a more nuanced and comprehensive view of the genes associated with ITA. Notably, we identified GSTM3, a gene involved in oxidative stress regulation, as a potential modulator of melanin levels and UV response. This warrants further investigation, particularly loss- and gain-of-function studies, to elucidate the relationship between GSTM3 variations, pigmentation, and UV sensitivity. More generally, further research is needed to confirm the roles of newly identified genes and pathways using in vitro and in vivo models, and to dissect the molecular interplay between these diverse biological processes. This molecular characterization of constitutive pigmentation offers promising avenues for the discovery of novel pharmacological targets and new active cosmetic ingredients for personalized skincare. Methods Samples Twenty-one ex-vivo skin samples with variable skin color were used for the study. Normal human skin samples were obtained after breast reduction surgery from healthy women living in France with the patients’ written informed consent in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration and with Article L. 1243-4 of the French Public Health Code. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients and archived by the surgeon. The samples were blind coded before their reception and only the age, gender, and anatomical site of the samples were specified. The hair color and eye color of the subjects were not available. The authors did not participate in the sample collection. Given its special nature, surgical residue is subject to specific legislation included in the French Code of Public Health (anonymity, gratuity, sanitary/safety rules). This legislation does not require prior authorization by an ethics committee for sampling or use of surgical waste ( http://www.ethique.sorbonneparis-cite.fr/?q = node/1767). The age (years) and average age per group is reported is reported in Table 1 . No significant difference in age was observed between the groups. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) did not reveal any significant differences between the groups (data not shown). Skin color assessment Skin color type was determined instrumentally with a Check Spectrophotometer (Datacolor; using D65, 10°, SCI, d/8° specifications) through the measurement of the Individual Typology Angle (ITA), based on L* (lightness) and b* (blue-yellow chroma) colorimetric parameters of the CIE Lab system according to the formula: ITA = [arctan(L*- 50)/b*] x 180/3.14159. This allowed us to objectively classify the skins into six groups: Very light 55° Light 41° Intermediate 28° Tan 10° Brown 30° Dark 21 , 60 regardless of ethnic origin. Samples were further gathered into 3 larger groups: ‘Light’ Group with 8 specimens (2 Very light and 6 Light); ‘Intermediate’ Group with 4 specimens (3 Intermediate and 1 Tan); and ‘Dark’ Group with 9 specimens (5 Brown and 4 Dark) (Table 1 ). RNA extraction Epidermis was separated from the dermis using NaBr (1 h at 37 °C), and RNA was isolated from the epidermis. Total RNA was isolated from the 21 samples with the RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA). Purity and integrity of the RNA was assessed on the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with the RNA 6000 NanoLabChip reagent set (Agilent, Palo Alto, CA, USA) and the Nanodrop spectrophotometer. Library preparation and RNA sequencing Library preparation and RNA-seq were carried out as described in the Illumina TruSeq stranded mRNA User Guide (Illumina inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Briefly, the first step in the workflow involved purifying the poly-A containing mRNA molecules from 600 ng of total RNA using poly-T oligos attached magnetic beads. Following purification, the mRNA was fragmented into small pieces using divalent cations under elevated temperature. The cleaved RNA fragments were copied into first strand cDNA using reverse transcriptase and random primers. The cDNA fragments were then purified with Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Missisauga, Ontario, Canada) and enriched with 13 cycles PCR to create the final cDNA library. cDNA quality was assessed on the TapeStation 2200 (Agilent technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and the library was quantified using the Qbit 3.0 fluorimeter (ThermoFischer Scientific, Canada). Equimolar amounts of each library were sequenced on a HiSeq 2500 (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) instrument, for a 101-nucleotides paired-end sequencing. Read quality control, quantification and normalization Quality of raw reads was assessed using the FastQC (v0.11.2) and MultiQC (v1.3) quality control tools. Raw reads were of a very good quality; the scores were systematically located within the 30 to 40 score area. Transcript annotation was performed using the Gencode database (v27). Transcript expression quantification was performed from the raw read data (raw counts) using Kallisto (v0.43.1) tool. To generate the normalized signals, raw count values were transformed into TPM (Transcript per Million) using Kallisto tool, or FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase per Millions reads aligned) using R (v.3.4.0) scripts. Principal component analysis (PCA) Principal Component Analyses (PCA) of sample expression levels were performed using the R packages FactoMineR (v1.40) and ggplot2 (v2.2.1). The genes with a mean TPM value of at least 5 across all samples were kept as dimensions. Gene-level analyses Differential expression analyses were first computed using the standard DESeq2 package (v1.16.1) 61 , which returns the results table with log2 fold change and Wald test p-values (adjusted by Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method and unadjusted). DESeq2 usually identifies genes with extreme values (called outliers) based on Cook’s distance and replaces the outlier counts with an imputed value, namely the trimmed mean over all samples, scaled by a sample-specific size factor. Although DESeq2 imputation method was applied, several genes still showed outlier values. For compensation, another method was used: outlier values were converted into missing values and then imputed with the Expectation-Maximization (EM) SVD approach using the package pcaMethods 62 . As the EM algorithm assumes a multivariate gaussian distribution of the data, the latter were log-transformed before running the algorithm. In total, 15,838 values have been identified as extreme values, corresponding to 11,329 different genes. The log-transformed values were then transformed back to their original scale, genes with null variance removed, and those having at least 10 reads in total were kept for further analyses (35879 genes). Differential expression analyses were finally computed using the standard DESeq2 package. Although some values were imputed with the EM-SVD algorithm, DESeq2 identified 184 genes with extreme values based on Cook’s distance. We chose not to replace those values with DESeq2’s imputation function, but genes were ‘flagged’ in the results table. For enrichment analyses, only genes annotated as ‘protein_coding’, ‘non-sense mediated decay’ (NMD) and ‘retained intron’ (RI) and displaying a mean Transcript Per Million (TPM) value of at least 1 in any of the 3 groups were considered for differential expression analyses. Genes were considered as differentially expressed (DE) if they were significant with a p-value 0.05 and with a −1,5 ≥ fold-change ≥ + 1,5. Quantitative reverse transcription PCR Equal amounts of RNA were used for first strand cDNA synthesis using the Advantage RT-for-PCR kit (Clontech, Saint Quentin en Yvelines, France). Quantitative PCR was performed using the LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master (Roche Diagnostics, Meylan, France). After 5 min of heat denaturation at 95 °C, 40 amplification cycles were performed: 20 s at 95 °C, 15 s at a primer-specific temperature, 20 s at 72 °C. House-keeping genes glyceraldhehyde-3-phosphate (GAPDH), ribosomal protein S9 (RPS9), beta-2-microglobulin (B2M), ribosomal protein L13a (RPL13A) and beta-actin (ACTB), were used for normalization with geNorm application 63 , 64 . Results are mean ± SD of target-gene expression normalized to housekeeping genes expression. Student t-test (two tailed) were performed with Graphpad Prism Version 10.2.3. Immunostaining and quantification Immunohistochemical labeling was performed on paraffin-embedded skin sections from the same specimens used for total RNA extraction. Five µm histological skin sections were probed with GPR143 antibody (HPA003648, Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, Missouri, USA) or with HRASLS1 antibody (HPA051179, Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, Missouri, USA) followed by detection with anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (A11008, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Nuclei were stained with propidium bromide. HRASLS1 and GPR143 immunostaining was quantified with NIH ImageJ Software. For HRASLS1, the fluorescence intensity of the stained surface of the epidermis was divided by the surface of the epidermis measured with the “segmented line” ImageJ tool. For GPR143, the number of stained cells in the basal layer measured with the “cell counter” ImageJ tool was divided by the length of the basal layer expressed in mm. Graphpad Prism Version 10.2.3. was used for Student (two tailed) t-test. Gene expression profiling and data interpretation For enrichment analyses, first manual sorting was conducted based on databases (functional Gene Ontology (GO) terms, KEGG pathways 31 , 32 , and GeneCards) and literature on skin biology (PubMed), then the gene sets were analyzed by the web-based bioinformatics application Ingenuity Pathway (IPA; QIAGEN Inc., https://www.qiagenbioinformatics.com/products/ingenuitypathway-analysis ) for enrichment of canonical pathways and cascades of upstream transcriptional regulators and enzymes. With IPA, two statistical measures, an overlap p-value and an activation z-score, were computed. For ML, the enrichment analyses were conducted using the ToppGene portal 65 . For all enrichments, a p-value 0.05 was used as cutoff. Machine learning To perform multivariate analysis using regression to predict ITA values, the biomarker discovery software BioDiscML 66 was utilized. BioDiscML automates the training of numerous combinations of machine learning algorithms and feature subsets, defined by various feature search methods. Initially, 58 243 expressed gene transcripts were processed by removing those with low expression. Genes with at least one transcript per million (TPM) value greater than 10 across the samples were retained, resulting in 11 087 transcripts. Subsequently, non-protein-coding transcripts were filtered out using the BioMart database, narrowing the dataset to 8 196 genes. The dataset was split into training (two-thirds) and testing (one-third) sets. BioDiscML then assessed the informativeness of each protein-coding gene through a feature ranking algorithm called RELIEFF 67 , generating a ranked list of the most informative genes relative to the ITA value. This ranked list was used for stepwise learning and signature search, with models optimized using metrics such as the correlation coefficient and root mean square error (RMSE), measured by 10-fold cross-validation in the train set. The trained models were further evaluated through 100 iterations of bootstrapping, repeated holdout, and testing on the reserved test set. Finally, to capture genes with similar expression profiles that might be missed during training, correlated genes (above 90% correlation) were identified using Spearman and Pearson approaches to create an extended signature.
