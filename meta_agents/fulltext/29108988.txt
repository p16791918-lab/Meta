1 Introduction Breast cancer rates in rural US counties is 9% higher compared to urban counties [ 1 ]. This disparity is largely attributed to rural–urban differences in mammography screening rates [ 2 ]. Mammography screening is associated with improved identification of breast cancer at early tumor stages [ 3 ], and decreased rates of cancer‐related mortality [ 4 ]. However, rural women are less likely to receive mammography screening, indicating an urgent need for the development of community‐level interventions to improve mammography screening rates in rural areas [ 5 ]. Mammography screening disparities among women are attributed to several factors, including poverty, discrimination, and poor access to health care services [ 6 , 7 ]. Women with low socioeconomic status have less access to mammography screening services, receive less education related to breast cancer screening, and live in communities with specific cultural beliefs and norms (e.g., fatalism) that may reduce health‐seeking behaviors [ 8 ]. Women who identify as a member of a marginalized racial or ethnic group may experience discrimination by the health care system, leading to medical mistrust and a delay in mammography screening [ 9 ]. Women who live in geographically isolated communities, such as rural communities, experience poor access to mammography screening due to provider area shortages and long drive times [ 6 , 10 , 11 ]. Importantly, women in rural areas are more likely to experience poverty, and the poorest rural communities have higher proportions of minoritized racial and ethnic populations [ 12 , 13 ]. The intersection of poverty, racial and ethnic diversity, and rurality contributes to significant health outcomes disparities across the breast cancer continuum [ 13 ]. Social associations fostering positive interactions have been found to increase mammography screening rates by increasing women's knowledge of mammography screening and providing emotional support [ 14 ] to complete mammography screening [ 15 ]. Social associations have been found to impact health‐seeking behaviors among high‐risk women, such as those who identify as low‐income and African American or Latina [ 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 ]. Among women in low‐income communities, social associations temper mammography screening disparities [ 14 ]. Among women who identify as a member of a minoritized racial or ethnic group, trusted family, friends, and community members may reduce women's medical mistrust and influence their decision to seek mammography screening [ 16 , 17 ]. Interestingly, in rural communities, there have been mixed findings on the role of social associations supporting health‐seeking behaviors [ 20 , 21 , 22 ]. While women living in rural Appalachia report high rates of social associations, these support systems did not impact mammography screening rates [ 21 ]. However, peer community support groups have effectively increased mammography screening rates among rural women in some communities [ 23 , 24 ]. Thus, the relationship between social associations and mammography screening in rural communities remains unclear. Understanding the role of social associations on mammography screening rates will provide urgently needed data to inform the development of evidence‐based, community‐level interventions that address existing rural–urban breast cancer rate disparities. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to explore the relationships among rurality, social associations, and mammography screening. 2 Materials and Methods We conducted a secondary data analysis to examine the relationships between rurality, social associations, and mammography screening using the 2022 County Health Rankings and Roadmaps dataset, a publicly available national dataset. We extracted data on rural county type, rate of social associations within a county, and mammography screening rate within a county [ 25 ]. Additionally, we extracted data for the county's racial and ethnic composition, primary care access, and uninsured population [ 25 ]. These variables were chosen a priori based on the established literature [ 7 , 16 , 18 , 21 , 26 , 27 ], and were considered for moderating the relationship between social associations and mammography screening. This study was determined to be exempt by the Michigan State University Institutional Review Board. 2.1 Measures 2.1.1 Endogenous Variable We defined mammography screening as the percentage of female Medicare enrollees aged 65–74 who received an annual mammography screening within each county [ 25 ]. 2.1.2 Exogenous Variables We evaluated mammography screening rate by geographic (e.g., rural vs. urban) location, the racial/ethnic composition of the county, and access to primary healthcare services. For geographic location, rurality was defined at the county‐level as geographically isolated communities [ 28 ] within the United States using the Federal Information Processing Standard (FIPS) codes. These unique identifiers allowed for the precise analysis of data across 3143 counties in the 48 contiguous states and Washington D.C. Rural–Urban Continuum Codes (RUCC) from the United States Department of Agriculture were employed to classify counties as rural or urban (e.g., 1–3 urban, 4–9 rural) [ 28 ]. We measured racial and ethnic composition within counties as percentages of Non‐Hispanic Black, American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, Hispanic, and Non‐Hispanic White based on the 2021 census population estimates. Access to primary care services was expressed as the ratio of primary care providers per 1000 patients in each county. The proportion of uninsured adults under 65 years of age within each county was expressed as a percentage [ 25 ]. 2.1.3 Mediator Variable Social associations, or social support resources and networks [ 29 ] were expressed as the number of social organizations per 10,000 residents. Social organizations encompassed various groups focused on activities such as sports, civic participation, religion, and other social interactions [ 25 ]. 2.2 Analytic Methods Descriptive statistics (mean, median, mode, standard deviation) were run to characterize the County Health Rankings and Roadmaps demographic data and their distributions. A structural equation model (SEM) was run to examine the direct effects of social associations and rurality on mammography screening and the indirect effect of rurality through social associations on mammography screening. SEM provides more precise, unbiased parameter estimates than traditional regression because SEM separates error variance from the true score variance. SEM allows for missing data on the endogenous variable using full maximum likelihood methods which assume that the data are missing at random. Figure 1 illustrates the SEM model for the direct effects of rurality on social associations, rurality on mammography screening, and social associations on mammography screening. The model also included the direct effects for % uninsured, population/PCP ratio, % Non‐Hispanic White, % Non‐Hispanic Black, % Hispanic, % Asian, % Native American/Alaskan Indian, and % Pacific Islander on rurality, social associations, and mammography screening. The indirect effect of rurality through social associations on mammography screening was also specified. Standardized beta coefficients and standard errors were examined and model fit indices computed using maximum likelihood estimation. Following conventions outlined by Kline [ 30 ], several model fit indices are reported: χ2/df ratio 2, comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI) 0.90, RMSEA 0.05, and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR) 0.08. With the exception of Pacific Islanders ( β = 0.005, p = 0.50), ethnicity was significantly associated with increased mammography screening (all p 0.05). 4 Discussion In this ecological study, we conducted a secondary data analysis with the 2022 County Health Rankings and Roadmaps dataset to explore the relationships among county‐level rurality, social associations, and mammography screening. Findings indicate that while the mammography screening rate is significantly lower in rural counties compared to urban counties, there is a greater density of social associations in rural counties than urban ones. Importantly, social associations appear to buffer the negative impact of rurality on mammography screening. Our findings are consistent with the well‐documented low rates of mammography screening in rural counties compared to urban US counties [ 7 , 10 , 11 , 20 ]. However, prior research has had mixed findings regarding the role of social associations on mammography screening rates [ 15 , 21 , 31 ]. Our study findings expand on prior research with analysis of a national, representative dataset that clearly indicates stronger social associations mitigate mammography screening disparities. Additionally, this is the first study to our knowledge that evaluates the mediating role of social associations on the relationship between rurality and mammography screening. Social associations were found to buffer the existing rural–urban mammography screening disparity. Although rural counties had significantly less women completing mammography screening, social associations within these counties were found to mitigate, and almost eliminate, rural–urban differences in mammography screening rates. This finding highlights the critical importance of social associations on health‐related behaviors to address health inequities. Social associations may improve mammography screening rates because rural residents model health‐seeking behaviors, trust, and empower self‐efficacy by their network of family, friends, and community members [ 15 , 32 ]. While prior literature suggests mixed findings regarding the impact of social associations on mammography screening for rural‐dwelling women, our findings indicate that nationwide, social associations were associated with higher mammography screening. Prior mixed findings may be due in part to the nuanced and highly differentiated environments and resources across rural communities. For example, some studies that did not find social associations connected to higher mammography screening rates were conducted in Appalachia [ 21 ], a region known for having multiple adverse social determinants of health, such as low socioeconomic status, low health literacy, and poor access to healthcare [ 33 , 34 ]. Exposure to these adverse social determinants of health potentially overwhelms the positive impact of social associations on mammography screening rates. Additionally, this may indicate that the impact of social associations on mammography screening may differ based on a community's cultural norms, beliefs, and trust in the medical system, and values related to health‐seeking behaviors [ 21 ]. The mechanism through which social associations reduce mammography screening disparities is largely unknown. One potential mechanism may be by addressing cultural health values, beliefs, and norms. In some rural counties, health beliefs related to cancer, such as fatalism and medical mistrust, may decrease health‐seeking behaviors, including cancer screening [ 35 , 36 ]. However, social associations may increase health‐seeking behaviors as women receive health‐related information, education, provider referrals, and narratives of seeking and receiving healthcare from their trusted social networks [ 15 , 21 ]. Further research is needed to examine the impact of cultural values, beliefs, and norms on women's decision‐making processes related to mammography screening. Social community was found to buffer the existing rural–urban mammography screening disparity. Although rural counties had significantly less women completing mammography screening, social community within these counties were found to mitigate, and almost eliminate, rural–urban differences in mammography screening rates. This finding highlights the critical importance of social community on health‐related behaviors to address health inequities. The role of social community may have particular importance for increasing mammography screening among rural women who identify as a marginalized race or ethnicity. Marginalized racial/ethnic women in rural counties experience a multitude of barriers to mammography screening, including poverty and discrimination [ 37 , 38 ]. While there is often greater racial/ethnic diversity in urban counties compared to urban ones, the poorest rural counties have higher racial/ethnic diversity [ 12 , 13 ]. In these low‐income rural counties, marginalized racial/ethnic groups of women can face discrimination leads to medical mistrust and reduces health‐seeking behaviors [ 37 , 38 ]. Social associations have been found to change the health education that Black women receive, and to impact the decision making processes of Latina and Black women [ 17 , 18 , 19 ]. Social associations for rural, marginalized racial/ethnic women may therefore counteract multiple and intersecting barriers to mammography screening (e.g., poor access to healthcare, lack of insurance, discrimination, and poverty). Strengths of this study include a national, publicly‐available dataset, so findings are reproducible and generalizable. Additionally, we conducted analysis using structural equation modeling rather than traditional regression models, which accounts for the direct and indirect impacts of the exogenous and endogenous variables, leading to a more nuanced and comprehensive analysis of social associations as a mediator between rurality and mammography screening. However, our findings are limited by the cross‐sectional nature of the dataset, the possibility of additional exogenous variables impacting the findings that were not included in analysis, and the lack of a measure of cultural health values, beliefs, and behaviors. Mammography screening rates were limited within the dataset to female Medicare enrollees aged 65–74. Finally, the number of social associations in a community does not necessarily provide a measure of community engagement, trust, or perceived social support. Future qualitative research is needed to understand the role of cultural health values, beliefs and behaviors on health‐seeking decisions, like mammography screening, such research may illuminate the mechanism through which social associations minimizes mammography screening disparities. Additionally, our findings are at the county level and do not represent the individual level factors impacting mammography screening. Our study findings have important research and clinical implications. First, additional research is needed to examine the mechanism through which social associations address mammography screening disparities, looking at the various aspects of social associations such as the role of community engagement, trust, and perceived social support. Second, researchers and clinicians can develop and implement interventions by building clinical‐community partnerships to leverage the buffering effect of social associations to improve mammography screening rates in rural communities. 5 Conclusion Rural counties have more social associations, but a lower rate of mammography screening than urban counties. Social associations buffer the relationship between rurality and mammography, which indicates that rural/urban disparities in mammography would be even worse if not for the protective effects of social associations. Future research leveraging clinical‐community partnerships could minimize rural–urban mammography screening disparities. Author Contributions V.
