1. Introduction Breast cancer is the most common cancer globally, significantly threatening women’s health. [ 1 ] It accounts for a substantial number of female mortalities annually and imposes a considerable burden on public health systems. Despite significant advancements in the diagnosis and treatment of breast cancer over the past few decades, a multitude of challenges still persist, such as the absence of affordable early risk markers and difficulties in linking routine clinical indicators to breast cancer risk. [ 2 ] Platelets, which are small cytoplasmic fragments derived from megakaryocytes in the bone marrow, play a crucial role in the body’s hemostatic function. Relevant research indicates that critical stages in breast tumor progression, including cell transformation, proliferation, survival, and angiogenesis, can be modulated by elements of the hemostatic system, such as platelets. [ 3 ] Among cancer patients, the prevalence of thrombocytosis spans from 10% to 57%, and the majority of studies have established that it serves as a negative prognostic factor for breast cancer. [ 4 – 6 ] Nevertheless, certain scholars have arrived at a contrary finding and contend that the evidence concerning the incidence and prognostic implications of thrombocytosis in breast cancer patients is insufficient, and its utility in clinical decision-making is relatively restricted, [ 7 , 8 ] signifying that the association between platelets and breast cancer remains ambiguous. Despite a large number of relevant studies, [ 4 – 8 ] traditional epidemiological and observational research struggles to effectively control for confounding variables and reverse causal associations, significantly undermining the reliability of conclusions. Mendelian studies employ genetic variations as instrumental variables to identify and quantify causal relationships, which can effectively mitigate the adverse effects of potential confounding factors and reverse causality, thus pioneering a novel approach for deeper exploration of the relationship between exposure and outcome. In this study, a 2-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) strategy is utilized to explore the relationship between platelet count and breast cancer. 2. Materials and methods 2.1. Data sources An MR analysis method was used for causal association study. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) of instrumental variables were mainly obtained from MR Base Database ( www.mrbase.org ), which contains a large collection of summary statistics of genome-wide association studies. We used research on platelet count in East Asian population published by Sakaue S in 2021 (ID: ebi-a-GCST90018749, n = 148,623) and research on breast cancer (ID: ebi-a-GCST90018579, n = 79,550) as exposure and outcome variables respectively. The breast cancer dataset included 6325 patients and 73,225 control individuals, with 12,429,464 SNPs. 2.2. Study design This study evaluated the causal association between platelet count and risk of breast cancer using a 2-sample MR analysis. In the MR analysis, platelet count was taken as the exposure variable, SNPs related to the exposure variable were used as instrumental variables, and breast cancer was set as the outcome variable. After extracting corresponding information of instrumental variables in the outcome variable dataset, the causal association between platelet count and risk of breast cancer was inferred by multiple analysis models. The analysis process was shown in Figure 1 . Figure 1. Diagram of Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis.MR = Mendelian randomization, SNPs = single-nucleotide polymorphisms. 2.3. Screening of instrumental variables Genetic instrumental variables used in MR analysis need to meet 3 assumptions: being closely related to the exposure variable, being independent of confounding factors, and being related to the outcome variable only through the exposure variable. [ 9 ] Based on the first assumption of MR analysis, SNPs with a genome-wide significant association level ( P 5 × 10⁻¹¹) and without linkage disequilibrium ( r ² 0.001, distance 10,000 kb) were selected as instrumental variables in the exposure variable dataset. In MR analysis, default parameters were adopted (for missing SNPs, linkage disequilibrium proxies were found with a minimum r ² of 0.8; for palindromic SNPs, the minor allele frequency threshold was set at 0.3). F -statistic was used to evaluate instrumental variable effect of each SNP. In principle, SNPs with an F -value 10 were selected for subsequent MR analysis. [ 10 ] Screened instrumental variables should have information about reference allele, effect allele(EA), effect allele frequency, effect value (beta), standard error (SE), and P -value in both exposure and outcome datasets. To meet the second and third assumptions, horizontal pleiotropy of instrumental variables was tested according to the intercept of MR-Egger regression and its P -value. 2.4. Statistical analysis 2.4.1. Evaluation of causal association effects This study used 4 methods, namely inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method, weighted median estimator (WME) method, MR-Egger method, and Weighted mode method, to evaluate the causal relationship between platelet count and risk of breast cancer. [ 11 ] Among them, the IVW method assumes that all SNPs are valid genetic instruments, i.e., assuming no horizontal pleiotropy. The WME method allows existence of horizontal pleiotropy in instrumental variables, provided that more than 50% of SNPs are valid instrumental variables. The MR-Egger method allows 100% of SNPs to be invalid instrumental variables. The Weighted mode method adjusts the impact of differences in genotype frequencies on analysis results by weighting different genotypes. 2.4.2. Heterogeneity test and sensitivity analysis To meet second and third assumptions of MR analysis, IVW method and MR-Egger method were used to analyze heterogeneity of instrumental variables. Leave-one-out method was used for sensitivity analysis. Leave-one-out method would sequentially remove 1 instrumental variable SNP, and IVW method was used to evaluate causal association of remaining SNPs. Whether MR analysis results would be affected by SNP was judged by whether effect value crossed zero point. 2.4.3. Statistical software Statistical analysis and graphing in this study were all completed in R language. Main R language packages used included TwoSampleMR. 3. Results 3.1. Instrumental variables In the MR analysis, 97 SNPs (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q982 ) were identified as strong instrumental variables for studying the causal link between platelet count and breast cancer risk, with each SNP having an F -statistic above 10 (minimum F = 43.603), indicating no weak instrument bias. The MR-Egger regression intercept (−0.005) and P -value (.384) confirmed the absence of horizontal pleiotropy among the variables. 3.2. MR results Taking platelet count as the exposure variable and breast cancer as the outcome variable, MR analysis was carried out (Table 1 ). The results indicated that the causal relationship between platelet count and the risk of breast cancer was statistically significant. The IVW method revealed that each standard deviation increase in platelet count raised breast cancer risk by 13.1% (odds ratio [OR] = 1.131, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.005–1.274, P = .042). The WME method showed that each standard deviation increase in platelet count raised breast cancer risk by 21.6% (OR = 1.216, 95%CI = 1.017–1.455, P = .032). Although the MR Egger regression and Weighted mode analyses did not find a statistically significant causal link, they aligned in direction with the IVW and WME methods. Discussion With the ongoing diversification of breast cancer treatment modalities, there has been a continuous improvement in the survival rates of breast cancer patients. Based on clinical detection indicators, the early identification, timely management, and treatment of breast cancer are crucial for enhancing patient prognosis. Previous studies have already indicated that there is an increase in the number of platelets among breast cancer patients. [ 4 , 13 ] However, traditional epidemiology faces challenges in elucidating whether elevated platelet levels are a causative factor for breast cancer or a consequence of the disease. Mendelian analysis, grounded in Mendel’s inheritance laws, minimizes environmental influences to better assess the genetic impact on traits, thus reducing variations caused by environmental factors. This study employed Mendelian analysis to investigate the causal link between platelet count and breast cancer, revealing that higher platelet counts may elevate breast cancer risk, which is similar to the conclusions of previous studies. [ 13 , 14 ] Specifically, An elevation of 1 standard deviation in platelet count, within the clinically observed range of 30–50 × 10⁹/L, correlates with an increase of 13.1% to 21.6% in the risk of breast cancer development. Although modest, this suggests a mild prothrombotic state and aids in risk stratification. For instance, the lifetime risk of breast cancer among Chinese women is approximately 5.73%, [ 15 ] and with a 20% increase in risk, this probability escalates to 6.88%, potentially resulting in an additional 115 breast cancer cases per 10,000 Chinese women. In clinical practice, the integration of platelet count with other risk factors, such as family history, can further optimize breast cancer screening strategies. A prospective cohort study of 50,000 participants showed significantly higher cancer incidence in individuals with thrombocytosis compared to those with normal platelet levels the following year (11.6% vs 4.1% for men; 6.2% vs 2.2% for women). [ 16 ] As the platelet count increases, the risk of developing cancer also increases. [ 16 ] In addition, elevated platelet counts are significantly linked to HER2 overexpression and a worse prognosis. [ 17 , 18 ] Furthermore, breast cancer patients with thrombocytosis may experience more invasive tumors and higher chances of lymph node metastases, [ 19 ] suggesting that platelets are crucial in breast cancer development and progression. [ 20 ] Platelets contribute to the initiation and progression of breast cancer through several mechanisms. Firstly, platelets can promote the proliferation of tumor cells. By releasing various growth factors, such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), they can activate a series of intracellular signaling pathways, such as the PI3K/Akt pathway, enabling cancer cells to enter the active proliferation phase from the quiescent phase. [ 21 ] For example, in vitro experiments showed that after treating breast cancer cell colonies with PDGF antagonists, the proliferation rate of cancer cells decreased significantly, indicating that PDGF plays a crucial role in promoting the proliferation of breast cancer cells. [ 22 ] Secondly, platelets aid tumor invasion and metastasis by shielding tumor cells from the immune system. Once in the bloodstream, tumor cells activate platelets, which cluster around them to form a “protective shell.” This protective shell conceals tumor cell antigens, hindering immune cells from identifying and destroying them. [ 23 ] Simultaneously, platelets secrete matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which degrade the extracellular matrix to facilitate tumor cell migration. [ 20 ] In a breast cancer metastasis model, platelets surrounding cancer cells released MMP-9, breaking down the vascular basement membrane and extracellular matrix, aiding tumor cells in entering blood vessels for distant spread. [ 24 ] Thirdly, platelets enhance tumor angiogenesis by releasing factors like vascular endothelial growth factor, which supports the growth and spread of breast cancer by stimulating vascular endothelial cell proliferation and migration. [ 25 ] Studies show that breast cancer tissues with elevated platelet counts exhibit higher vascular density, suggesting a contributory role of platelets in tumor angiogenesis. [ 26 ] This study has limitations: Firstly, this study is limited by genetic and environmental differences across populations. Since our sample was Asian, the findings may not apply to other groups. Secondly, palindromic SNPs were not fully excluded, potentially affecting allele frequency estimates and genetic instrument accuracy. Thirdly, an increase in platelet count by 1 standard deviation is associated with a 13.1%-21.6% increase in breast cancer risk. However, this increase is moderate, limiting the utility of platelet count as a standalone indicator for risk assessment. 5. Conclusion Based on the genome-wide association study database, this study used MR analysis to confirm that an elevated platelet count is causally associated with an increased risk of breast cancer, providing a new perspective for research on breast cancer risk factors.
