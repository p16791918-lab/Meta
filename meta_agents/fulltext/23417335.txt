Introduction Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a major comorbidity in women with breast cancer and remains the leading cause of death among women in the United States. 1 , 2 Studies show that women with breast cancer have an increased incidence of CVD, cardiometabolic risk factors, and CVD-related mortality compared with women without breast cancer. 3 , 4 The heightened risk of CVD and cardiometabolic risk factors in women with breast cancer is multifactorial, likely driven by the direct adverse effects of cancer treatments, shared underlying biological pathways, and social determinants of health that predispose patients to CVD, cardiometabolic risk factors, and cancer. 5-8 In both CVD and cancer, a disproportionate burden of illness occurs in women of historically marginalized groups. 2 , 9-12 Similar disparities are emerging in CVD outcomes for women with breast cancer. 13-17 One study found that in women with invasive breast cancer between the ages of 55 and 69 years, Black women had higher CVD-related mortality than non-Hispanic White women. 14 Studies to date elucidating racial and ethnic health disparities in cardio-oncology have been focused on differences in CVD-related mortality and incident cardiomyopathy after exposure to cardiotoxic chemotherapies, 18-20 yet other CVD outcomes have not been fully characterized. Few studies have explored CVD racial and ethnic disparities in women with breast cancer by race or ethnicity, apart from those who identify as White or Black. 21-23 In addition, data on disparities in the development of cardiometabolic risk factors within these populations are limited. The Pathways Heart Study at Kaiser Permanente Northern California (KPNC) is a prospective, racially and ethnically diverse cohort of 14 942 women with breast cancer that presents a unique opportunity to study CVD in breast cancer survivors. Our recent study found that women with breast cancer compared with women without breast cancer had increased incidence of CVD events, CVD-related mortality, and all-cause mortality, which varied by treatments received. 24 In addition, women with breast cancer experienced elevated risks of diabetes and hypertension, depending on cancer treatments received and body mass index. 25 Building on these findings, the current study investigated whether risk of incident cardiometabolic risk factors and CVD events in women with breast cancer differs by self-identified race and ethnicity. We expand on the current literature by not only examining White and Black populations but also considering the experience of Hispanic and Asian women with breast cancer, including disaggregating the Asian women into subgroups. Methods Study population The Pathways Heart Study is a prospective cohort study of women with invasive breast cancer diagnosed between November 2005 and March 2013 at KPNC and followed up through December 31, 2021. Human subjects approval was obtained from the KPNC Institutional Review Boards and the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center. Eligibility criteria included age older than 20 years at breast cancer diagnosis, female sex, first-time occurrence of invasive breast cancer (American Joint Committee on Cancer stage I-IV), and continuous enrollment at KPNC for at least 12 months before diagnosis. For this secondary analysis on cancer health disparities, 9 women with missing race or ethnicity data were excluded. Data collection and measures The primary data source was the KPNC Virtual Data Warehouse (VDW). The VDW contains data extracted from the KPNC electronic health record (EHR) system as well as clinical and administrative databases for research purposes. 26 Data of interest included sociodemographic characteristics, healthcare utilization and enrollment, cancer treatments, vital signs, baseline conditions at diagnosis, laboratory results, pharmacy records, and mortality. Baseline hypertension and dyslipidemia were defined using 2 unique International Statistical Classification of Diseases ( ICD ) diagnostic codes at outpatient and inpatient visits, laboratory values, and medication dispensed within 3 years before breast cancer diagnosis. Baseline diabetes status came from the KPNC Diabetes Registry. 25 Baseline CVD was determined from a combination of ICD diagnostic codes, Current Procedural Terminology codes, or hospital discharge records. Health plan utilization was defined using the number of primary care visits 1 year before diagnosis. 24 Comorbidity was characterized by ICD codes from visits 1 year before breast cancer diagnosis using the Comorbidity Point Score, version 2. 27 Data on cancer treatments (chemotherapy, radiation therapy, and endocrine therapy) were obtained from the VDW and EHR data. Specific criteria used for incident hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia have been described previously. 25 Incident hypertension and diabetes after breast cancer diagnosis were defined using the criteria described by the KPNC Hypertension Program and KPNC Diabetes Registry, respectively. 28 , 29 Dyslipidemia was captured through an algorithm of diagnosis codes, laboratory results, or lipid-lowering prescription medications. Cardiovascular events were identified from the KPNC VDW by ICD diagnostic codes and Current Procedural Terminology codes from inpatient, outpatient, and emergency department visits and hospital discharge records. 25 Primary CVD outcomes included ischemic heart disease, heart failure (HF)/cardiomyopathy, stroke, and CVD-related deaths. 29 Inherited cardiomyopathies were excluded. Mortality data through December 31, 2021, were obtained from the KPNC VDW mortality file, which combines information from the California State Department of Vital Statistics, US Social Security Administration, National Death Index, and KPNC membership data. Self-classified race and ethnicity data were obtained from the VDW and EHR and categorized as Hispanic participants and, among non-Hispanic participants, White, Black, Asian, Pacific Islander, or American Indian or Alaska Native. The ethnicities used for disaggregated Asian subgroup analyses were the most prevalent groups in our database; these included Chinese, Filipino, Japanese, and South Asian. Statistical analysis Baseline patient characteristics were described with means (SDs) for continuous variables and frequency distributions for categorical variables. Cumulative incidence functions were estimated, accounting for the competing event of non-CVD mortality and subdistribution hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for incident hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, ischemic heart disease, HF and cardiomyopathy, and stroke after breast cancer diagnosis and were estimated using Fine-Gray proportional hazards regression. Incidence was calculated using a competing risk framework for non–CVD-related death in the cardiometabolic risk factors models and overall death in the CVD models. Individuals were followed from the date of breast cancer diagnosis until the earliest CVD event, KPNC disenrollment, death, or December 31, 2021 (the end of follow-up). The proportionality hazards assumption was tested for each model separately using Schoenfeld residuals. When there was evidence of violation, 30 the specific variable was modeled as a covariate by time interaction term (ie, cross-product term for covariate and time-varying log survival time). Models were adjusted for the following: age at breast cancer diagnosis; baseline body mass index; menopausal status; smoking status; neighborhood median household income; neighborhood education level; primary care outpatient utilization 1 year before index date; and receipt of aromatase inhibitors, tamoxifen, chemotherapy, or radiation therapy. Age was used as the underlying time scale for regression models to control for age effects. All variables were categorical apart from the following continuous variables: median household income, primary care utilization, and age at diagnosis. Models for incident cardiometabolic risk factors were adjusted for CVD baseline conditions. Models for incident CVD outcomes were adjusted for baseline cardiometabolic conditions. For each cardiometabolic risk factor and CVD event model, women with a history of that risk factor or event within the past 12 months were excluded. For the composite cardiometabolic risk factor and CVD analyses, censoring occurred at time of first event. Analyses were stratified by exposure to chemotherapy, radiation therapy, and endocrine therapy and not mutually exclusive by type of treatment. Statistical analyses were performed in SAS, version 9.4, statistical software (SAS Institute Inc). A 2-sided P .05 was considered statistically significant. Results Baseline characteristics A total of 14 942 women with breast cancer had a mean (SD) age of 61.2 (12.8) years ( Table 1 ). Distributions by race and ethnicity were 65% non-Hispanic White, 7.5% Black, 14.4% Asian, 11.9% Hispanic, 0.4% Pacific Islander, and 0.8% American Indian or Alaska Native women. Non-Hispanic White women were diagnosed at a later age (63.2 years vs 57.4 years) and had a higher percentage of American Joint Committee on Cancer stage I disease at diagnosis (55% non-Hispanic White vs 48% Black). A higher percentage of non-Hispanic White women were former smokers compared with most other groups (34% non-Hispanic White vs 10% Black). Baseline cardiometabolic risk factors were highest for Black women for baseline hypertension (43% non-Hispanic White vs 60% Black). Incident cardiometabolic risk factors During a median follow-up period of 5.14 years (IQR = 1.52-10.30 years), 1790 (11.98%) women developed hypertension, 1004 (6.72%) developed diabetes, and 1775 (11.88%) developed dyslipidemia. Black women with breast cancer compared with non-Hispanic White women with breast cancer had a higher cumulative incidence of hypertension at all time points, especially by 10 years (36% vs 27%). For diabetes, Black, Asian, Hispanic, and Pacific Islander women compared with non-Hispanic White women had a consistently higher cumulative incidence. Incidence rates of dyslipidemia were higher for Asian, Hispanic, and American Indian or Alaskan Native women at all endpoints. Black women had a lower cumulative incidence than non-Hispanic White women during each endpoint (19% vs 26% by 10 years) ( Figure 1, A-D ). Figure 1. Cumulative incidence function curves for (A) hypertension, (B) diabetes, (C) dyslipidemia, and (D) any cardiovascular disease risk factor in women with breast cancer, stratified by race and ethnicity. In Fine-Gray models, compared with non-Hispanic White women, Black (subdistribution HR = 1.31, 95% CI = 1.02 to 1.67) and Asian (subdistribution HR = 1.19, 95% CI = 1.00 to 1.42) women had a higher risk of incident hypertension. Black (subdistribution HR = 1.45, 95% CI = 1.14 to 1.84), Asian (subdistribution HR = 3.53, 95% CI = 2.96 to 4.20), Hispanic (subdistribution HR = 1.95, 95% CI = 1.63 to 2.33), and Pacific Islander (subdistribution HR = 3.78, 95% CI = 2.10 to 6.80) women had a higher risk of incident diabetes ( Table 2 ). Asian women had a higher risk of incident dyslipidemia (subdistribution HR = 1.20, 95% CI = 1.02 to 1.42). Black women had a lower risk of incident dyslipidemia (subdistribution HR = 0.63, 95% CI = 0.49 to 0.82). The disaggregated analysis of Asian subgroups showed that, compared with non-Hispanic White women, all were at increased risk of incident diabetes. In addition, Japanese women were at elevated risk of developing dyslipidemia, and Filipina women were at increased risk of developing hypertension and diabetes. Incident CVD outcomes During a median follow-up period of 9.21 years (IQR = 3.90-11.92 years), a total of 640 (4.28%) women developed ischemic heart disease, 984 (6.59%) developed HF/cardiomyopathy, and 499 (3.34%) developed stroke, with 1511 (10.11%) experiencing any primary CVD outcome. Black women with breast cancer compared with non-Hispanic White women with breast cancer had a higher cumulative incidence of ischemic heart disease, HF and cardiomyopathy, stroke, and CVD mortality at all endpoints, especially at 10 years (7% vs 5%, 10% vs 7%, 4% vs 3%, and 15% vs 11%, respectively) ( Figure 2 ). Discussion Our study, which followed 14 942 women with breast cancer for a median of 9.7 years (IQR = 5.0-12.3 years), uncovered statistically significant racial and ethnic disparities in the development of cardiometabolic risk factors and CVD. Cardiometabolic risk factors Black women in our study had a 1.3 to 1.5 times higher risk of developing hypertension and diabetes than did non-Hispanic White women. Notably, Black women demonstrated a 0.6 times lower risk of developing dyslipidemia, consistent with previous findings described in women without breast cancer. 2 , 31 Asian women with breast cancer showed a 1.2 times higher risk of developing hypertension and dyslipidemia and a 3.5 times higher risk of diabetes compared with non-Hispanic White women. This finding contrasts with the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, which shows lower hypertension prevalence (37.6% vs 42.6%) and similar dyslipidemia rates in Asian women without breast cancer. 2 Although diabetes prevalence is higher in Asian women without breast cancer than in non-Hispanic White women (9.9% vs 7.7%), the 3.6 times higher risk observed in our study is greater than expected. 2 All Asian subgroups showed an increased risk of diabetes, with varying risks for hypertension and dyslipidemia. Hispanic and Pacific Islander women also had elevated risks of incident diabetes (2.0 and 3.8 times, respectively). CVD outcomes Black and Asian women with breast cancer are at increased risk of CVD events. Our findings corroborate previous reports indicating that Black women face increased CVD-related mortality compared with non-Hispanic White women in both invasive 14 , 16 and noninvasive breast cancer. 15 In our study, Black women who underwent chemotherapy had a 1.7 times elevated risk of developing HF/cardiomyopathy compared with non-Hispanic White women, consistent with previous reports that Black individuals who have been treated with doxorubicin 18 or trastuzumab 19 , 20 develop reduced ejection fraction more frequently than do non-Hispanic White individuals. Importantly, the racial diversity of our cohort enabled us to demonstrate that Asian women, a group that has been understudied, are also at higher CVD risk than non-Hispanic White women. In addition, the incidence of CVD varied among Asian subgroups after disaggregation, highlighting the importance of not grouping ethnically diverse populations into a single category. Disaggregation of the Asian category into subgroups Historically, diverse Asian subgroups have often been aggregated into 1 category, but research has shown statistically significant variations in disease burden among these distinct subgroups. One study 32 found that mortality patterns were heterogenous between Asian subgroups based on cancer site and risks associated with the specific cancer type. A recent American Heart Association Scientific Statement 33 underscored the varying risk of CVD across Asian subgroups, with individuals of South Asian and Filipino descent being at the highest risk. Our data reveal that among Asian women with breast cancer, all individual Asian subgroups are at an increased risk of incident diabetes. Notably, Filipino and South Asian women face a higher risk of any CVD event (1.3 and 2.3 times, respectively), while Chinese and Japanese women have similar risks to non-Hispanic White women. Specifically, Filipino women in our study have a 1.6 times increased risk of stroke, and South Asian women are at 2.5 to 2.6 times increased risk for ischemic heart disease or HF. Previous studies have explored the underlying mechanisms contributing to the elevated cardiovascular and cardiometabolic risk among South Asian and Filipino women in the general population. US national mortality records from 2003 to 2011 indicated that South Asian and Filipino populations have the highest CVD death rates compared with other Asian subgroups. These disparities are attributed to a combination of genetic predispositions, lifestyle factors, socioeconomic conditions, and cultural practices. For instance, South Asian individuals exhibit high population-attributable risk of high lipoprotein(a) and myocardial infarction (MI; 9.5%). 34 Analysis of the National Health Interview Survey (2013-2018) revealed that unfavorable social determinants of health correlate with CVD risk factors, with the link to high blood pressure being strongest for Filipino individuals and suboptimal sleep in South Asian individuals. 35 Disaggregating subgroups as we have done is essential for accurately assessing CVD risk among Asian women with breast cancer, understanding which groups are at differential risk, and developing effective screening and prevention strategies. A range of strategies can include prioritized interventions, education, and improved healthcare access. Disparities in cardio-oncology Racial and ethnic disparities in cardiometabolic risk factors and CVD outcomes among women with breast cancer are driven by a complex interplay of social determinants and structural racism. 36 These disparities reflect both historical and ongoing inequities in society and the healthcare system. 37 , 38 Key factors include demographic, socioeconomic, and environmental conditions as well as medical mistrust. 17 , 39 Racial and ethnic disparities in breast cancer screening and treatment also contribute to disparate outcomes. 40 Despite overall progress in population-wide screening, mammographic screenings remain underutilized among Black women, who frequently present with more advanced stages of breast cancer. 41 , 42 Black women are also less likely than their non-Hispanic White counterparts to receive adjuvant radiation and chemotherapy after surgery. 43 , 44 Our findings support developing targeted clinical strategies, such as individualized CVD risk factor surveillance and survivorship care plans, which aim to better communicate with patients and their primary care doctors. 45 Community engagement and outreach efforts are also crucial for advancing health equity in cardio-oncology. 17 Strengths Our study adds to the literature by examining outcomes in a large, prospective cohort with diverse racial and ethnic representation and long-term longitudinal follow-up. Using data from an integrated healthcare system with a robust EHR system, we achieved detailed demographic and comorbidity characterization. The breadth of data also allowed for the adjustment of several factors (eg, sociodemographic factors, access to care, existing comorbidities) that otherwise may have been confounders. Finally, our data collection was not limited to diagnostic codes; it also incorporated laboratory values and medication dispensing records. Based on a literature search using the terms ‘cardiooncology’, ‘race’ and ‘ethnicity’, this is the first study reporting on racial and ethnic health disparities in incident cardiometabolic risk factors and CVD—specifically, ischemic heart disease and stroke—in a large, prospective breast cancer cohort. We also highlight the importance of disaggregating Asian populations to accurately reflect varied social, economic, and environmental exposures. 46 Limitations First, external validity may be limited because our study population generally had higher socioeconomic status than the US average. Socioeconomic status was estimated based on neighborhood-level data, potentially misrepresenting individual status. Despite adjustments for income, education, insurance, and primary care utilization, residual confounding may still be present. Second, small sample sizes in specific racial and ethnic subgroups limit statistical power. In addition, our dataset did not capture multiple racial or ethnic identities simultaneously. Finally, although aggregation can obscure disparities, 25 we did not further stratify racial and ethnic groups beyond the Asian cohort due to power constraints. Conclusion In this large, prospective cohort study of a diverse population of women with a history of breast cancer, we observed statistically significant racial and ethnic disparities in the incidence of diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia, especially among Black and Asian women. Racial and ethnic differences were also evident in cardiovascular outcomes, with Black women showing a higher risk of incident primary CVD, ischemic heart disease, and HF. Heterogeneity in risk of CVD after disaggregation of the individual Asian subgroups was observed, with the highest risk in Filipino and South Asian women. These findings underscore the importance of better characterizing health disparities in cardio-oncology to optimally inform breast cancer survivorship care with targeted CVD prevention and treatment strategies.
