INTRODUCTION Breast cancer is the highest incident neoplastic disease in women, with a high burden in the population in terms of costs and quality of life impact, especially for more advanced disease. Rural, low‐income, and non‐White women are more likely to have locally advanced or metastatic breast tumors than more affluent or urban White women. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Barriers in access to screening mammograms are an important source of this disparity to consider. The US Preventive Services Task Force found sufficient evidence of a moderate net benefit from biennial screening mammography in women aged 40‐74 years, of earlier diagnosis, and better overall survival when breast cancer is detected. 5 Barriers to accessing recommended screenings have been reported at the individual patient level, related to community characteristics, and associated with broader societal factors. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 In previous work, we reported on the role of attributes of the breast imaging center in screening uptake in rural and urban areas of Appalachia. 14 We found lower mammogram uptake in rural communities served by imaging facilities with fewer organizational resources, such as the use of patient reminders, staff training programs, and the number of staff employed by a facility. In addition, communities served by centers with higher market share and offering diagnostic services had higher population rates for current screening mammograms. In this study, we examine the extent to which organizational factors influence recall and biopsy rates, as well as timely follow‐up care. Delays in diagnosing breast cancer are important because they could worsen patient outcomes, such as increased treatment complexity and cost, and shorten survival. 15 , 16 , 17 Previous work has shown that women are likely to experience delays in follow‐up care not related to insurance possession, especially non‐Hispanic Black and rural women. 18 Few studies have investigated organizational factors in this regard to determine whether interventions are needed at the facility level. We hypothesized that rural areas may be structurally disadvantaged in timely follow‐up care than metropolitan areas due to having weaker facility organizational strength—measured in terms of screening capacity, technology, and operational infrastructure. 19 Recall rates are a quality measure for breast imaging centers that reflect an optimal balance of improved cancer detection and risk from false‐positive exams, as the rate of cancer detection is 0.5% among those screened. 20 Recommended recall rates for additional imaging for an abnormal mammogram range from 5% to 12%. 21 , 22 , 23 Delays in diagnostic mammograms may occur along several steps in the flow and handling of screening information, including: interpretation and consultation, patient notification, and scheduling a diagnostic exam and follow‐up procedures. 24 Organizational factors such as screening technology, staffing levels, resources, and services offered (diagnostic vs screening only) could influence efficiency in screening recalls. Although the majority of recalls can rule out further suspicion, those that do not require biopsy. Facilities that only offer screening services or that lack resources may experience system‐level delays due to the need to refer women with abnormal findings elsewhere. METHODS The study utilized data from 191 (of 337) Federal Drug Administration (FDA) registered screening sites located in central and northern Appalachia, including regions of Kentucky, Ohio, Pennsylvania, Virginia, and West Virginia, hereafter referred to as our study region of Appalachia. Study data included survey responses to items addressing organizational capacity, diagnostic services, outreach/marketing, operational support, and radiology review capacity and resources of screening sites. Facilities that did not participate in the 2019 study survey (opted out or did not return the survey) were excluded. Medicare claims data were obtained from the Centers for Medicare Medicaid Services (CMS) for all female beneficiaries ( N = 2,650,140) enrolled in Fee‐for‐Service (FFS) Medicare between 2016 and 2018 in the study region, based on Zip+4 codes and county codes (see Figure S1 ). Mammogram claims were identified from CPT codes for screening and diagnostic mammograms. 25 , 26 Women between the ages of 65‐75, with a mammogram in the most recent 2‐year period, were considered up to date with screening as of December 31, 2018. Mammography claims were assigned to a facility based on its 9‐digit Zip code and National Provider Identifier. The final database was quality‐checked by mapping the 9‐digit Zip code distributions of patients assigned to each facility to examine possible geographical anomalies. For each facility, we calculated a service area using a kernel density estimator for the spatial area that included 80% of a facility's patients. From these data, we calculated the size of the area (sq. miles), number of age‐eligible female Medicare beneficiaries residing in the area, and total number of mammograms among residents linked to a facility. The latter was used to calculate both the overall receipt of mammograms in the area and the proportion delivered by that facility as a measure of service penetration and relative market share of each facility. PRIMARY OUTCOMES Days from screening mammogram to recall with diagnostic imaging We defined “diagnostic imaging” as the presence of a claim for diagnostic mammography, with or without digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT), or unilateral or focused breast ultrasound, conducted in the 45 days after the index mammogram. The latter was defined based on: (1) same‐day diagnostic mammogram codes with—GG modifier; (2) any diagnostic breast exam within 45 days following the index screening mammogram event; or (3) screening mammogram codes and diagnostic codes on the same day with modifier—GG or screening mammogram with ultrasound on the same day. Patients referred for diagnostic follow‐up services that were not performed or billed cannot be observed in claims data and, thus, were considered as not recalled. Days from diagnostic recall to biopsy Biopsies were identified from claims codes (HCPCS 19081‐19086, 19100, 19281‐19288, 19499, 10004‐10012, 10021‐22, 76098, see Supplementary Appendix). Patients with a claims‐identified diagnostic procedure following a screening mammogram comprised the sample to search for biopsies. Biopsy claims within 4 months of a mammogram recall were deemed related to diagnostic breast imaging. Average days to recall from screening were calculated for each facility. Facilities with no days to recall found are missing either because no recalls occurred or no high‐quality recall claims were available. Patients with biopsy orders that were either not performed or not billed cannot be observed in claims. INDEPENDENT VARIABLES Type of facility Imaging facilities were classified as single, stand‐alone sites or affiliates of a larger health system or network based on survey data and website verification. The latter designations were aggregated from facility survey categories including: single, financially independent mammography location, member of a health system, member of a consortium of mammography/imaging facilities, or member of a large medical complex/location. Organization capacity and resources To assess facility organizational resources and capacity, we used factor scores for capacity, diagnostic services, and radiation support developed in our previous work with these survey data, called the “BIOPSI” instrument. 14 Capacity assesses the use of digital mammography machines and tomosynthesis machines, the number of screenings performed weekly, and having a patient navigator on staff. Diagnostic services include items assessing diagnostic mammography, type of interventional procedures offered, use of own pathology lab, and diagnostic mammograms interpreted onsite. Radiology review included survey items on how often performance data are provided to radiologists, the range of performance data provided, and how information is reviewed. Each factor score is standardized to have a range of 0‐100, a statistical mean of 50, and a standard deviation of 10. Facilities ( N = 32) that provided only screening services were assigned a diagnostic services score of “0.” To be precise, the number and characteristics of licensed mammography machines per center, matched to street address and facility name, was obtained from the FDA under a Freedom of Information Act request. The ratio of a center's FDA‐listed machines to the population was calculated against the number of females in the facility's service area (per 100k) using 2017 Census tract population counts. Index screening mammogram The index event was a screening mammogram billed in claims to a beneficiary with no prior screening or diagnostic mammogram within 12 months from the date of the event from 2017 to 2018 among women aged 65‐75, who resided within the service area of a facility. Events were observed in the CMS carrier and outpatient files by searching for Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes (77052, 77057, 77063, 77067, G0202). Receipt of screening digital tomosynthesis was identified via code 77063 and counted for each facility as “3D.” Diagnostic mammography service codes were similarly searched as imaging (77055, 77056, 76090, 76091, G0204, G0205, G0206, G0207, 76645, 77058, 77059, 77065, 77066, G0279, 77061, 77062, 76093, 76094), breast ultrasound (76645, 76641, 76642, 76999), and breast MRI (77046, 77047, 77048, 77049, 77058, 77059, 76093, 76094, 0159T). Patients screened in hospital outpatient settings may have different coding (C8903‐C8908, C8937). Breast screening from nonmammography modalities, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), was excluded from analyses. GEOGRAPHICAL AND CONTEXTUAL VARIABLES Area‐based mammogram uptake Mammogram services were assigned to a facility using a minimum of address‐based matching. Facility service‐area rates of mammogram uptake were used to capture broad, unobserved, contextual conditions that may prevail to enhance or hinder access or adherence to mammograms. The latter includes factorssuch as community‐level awareness, primary care provider referral patterns, and/or transportation and strength of volunteer support for outreach and navigation. We hypothesize that such community contextual norms could buffer or exacerbate the effects of imaging centers on adherence to recall mammograms or follow‐up biopsies. Area‐based market share Distinct from area mammogram uptake is the percentage of women aged 65‐75 in a service area who received their mammogram from that specific imaging center. This was used to estimate the center's strength as a provider of area mammogram services, or health care prominence within its service area We hypothesize that prominent providers of mammograms provide better quality of follow‐up care by having larger networks and priority access to hospital‐based services and specialists. For each facility service area, we also summarized area size (sq miles) and median beneficiary travel distance (9‐digit ZIP centroid to facility), and we report market share as indicators of market penetration and community screening behavior. These are presented descriptively by metropolitan status (see Supplementary Appendix, Table S1 ). 3D mammography Tomosynthesis imaging is known to offer increased diagnostic accuracy with a reduced recall rate. 13 Thus, we hypothesized that the effects of imaging center resources and capacity on recall or biopsy may be influenced by the use of 3D imaging. 27 Rurality Sociodemographic characteristics of facility locations were examined at the census tract level using 2010 Rural‐Urban Community Area codes, which reflect population density, level of urbanization, and adjacency to urbanized areas. The latter were collapsed into metropolitan or nonmetropolitan (rural) categories. Travel distance to the facility, calculated as straight‐line median distance, was calculated as the median of the distances between the 9‐digit Zip code centroids for all female Medicare beneficiaries aged 65‐75 residing in a facility service area and the street location of the imaging machine registration recorded by the FDA. Area economic distress Economic status of the facility service location was measured using an “area deprivation index” (ADI) 14 weighted from the Census tracts and major portions of tracts within a facility's service area. The latter index considers levels of household wealth, stability, family structure, percent employed, and income disparity (defined as the log ratio of the number of households with 1 person per room). Using original algorithms for the latter index, 28 the ADI was updated by our team with 2015 Census data. STATISTICAL METHODS Descriptive analyses were performed for both facility and patient‐level data. Predictive models of mammogram recall and biopsy were constructed at the patient level, including characteristics at the individual (eg, age, race), community (eg, ADI and metropolitan status), and facility accessed for mammography (eg, percent of screenings performed with tomosynthesis). We employed generalized linear mixed‐effects models (GLMM) to investigate the association between recall for additional imaging (binary outcome, coded as Y = recalled, N = not recalled), biopsy upon recall, time until recall (High ≥ median, Low median), time until biopsy (High ≥ median, Low median), and several predictor variables, including age at screening (scaled), race, dual eligibility status, ADI, rurality, patient use of tomosynthesis, and stand‐alone facility status. Patient‐level models included fixed effects for age, race, dual eligibility, exam‐level tomosynthesis use (a patient‐level exposure that reflects facility technology at the point of care), rurality, ADI, and stand‐alone facility status, with random intercepts for facility and patient to account for clustering and unobserved facility heterogeneity. The model was fit using the GLMER function from the lme4 package in R, 29 assuming a binomial distribution with a logit link. The interpretation of the odds ratio in the mixed model is an increase in the odds of an outcome predicted in a patient for a unit increase in the predictor. Missing data were addressed by listwise deletion. Our proposed model of facility characteristic effects on mammogram recall and biopsy rates was tested using structural equation models (SEMs), fit over the study sample of 191 facility observations. The SEM simultaneously assessed the relationships between the proposed latent factor for facility effects and observed variables relating to service area (ie, poverty, rurality, distance, mammogram uptake) and market share, accounting for measurement error. We employed the full information maximum likelihood method to estimate parameters of the model, which provides robust estimates in the presence of missing data (assuming data are missing at random or missing completely at random). Before fitting the models, all variables were standardized to have a sample mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1. Proc Calis in the SAS system v9.4 was used to find the parameter estimates. Two variables, 3D tomography and service area uptake, were modeled as potential mediators of facility and community‐level effects on recall and biopsy rates. An (anonymized) Institutional Review Board approved this study. DISCUSSION The Appalachian region examined in this study contains areas with significantly higher cancer burden compared to other areas of the United States, linked to chronic poverty, inadequate access to preventive health care, and the need for infrastructure investment. 6 , 7 , 30 , 31 , 32 CMS quality measures for breast imaging include having a recall exam within 45 days, with acceptable performance within 5%‐12% of all screenings. There is currently no national consensus or quality benchmark for timely biopsy after an abnormal screening mammogram. 33 , 34 This study is among the first to examine how community and health care organization attributes jointly impact breast cancer screening outcomes of recall and biopsy rates. Our focus on Medicare‐insured women reflects that women aged 65 years and older who develop breast cancer often have poor outcomes, and thus may benefit the most from breast cancer screenings. 35 This focus is especially important for rural Appalachia which has an older age population structure, and where up to 50% of older women do not undergo recommended screenings. 1 , 14 , 36 Consistent with studies in other geographic regions and settings, we found that recall exams were less likely for Medicare‐beneficiaries who were older or screened by tomosynthesis, 37 , 38 , 39 which may reflect challenges related to greater breast density and concern placed on overdiagnosis with older age. While a lower recall rate with 3DT has been shown in randomized studies, 38 , 39 this has not been uniformly observed across all studies involving Medicare‐insured women. Upneja et al. 40 compared screening modality on recall rates among women diagnosed with breast cancer in SEER‐Medicare data and found higher adjusted recall rates with 3DT versus digital mammography (11.4 vs 10.0 per 1,000 mammograms, P = .004). They hypothesized a waning benefit of tomosynthesis with increased patient age. Our study used more recent claims data and found a significant reduction of recalls with breast tomosynthesis at ages 65‐75 years. This may represent a shift in patient selection and operator training after initial implementation of DBT, and gains from serial use of DBT over time with the same patient. 41 In our study, biopsy after a recall examination was more common among women living in high deprivation areas, and those who were screened by tomosynthesis compared to their counterparts. An association of tomosynthesis and biopsy rates has been reported by other investigators. Rauscher et al. 20 in a study of over 1 million mammograms found a higher rate of biopsy recommendations for mammograms obtained with 3DT examinations (1.93%) than digital and analog examinations (1.55% and 1.47%, respectively). The latter difference was attributed as a follow‐on effect from greater accuracy of tomosynthesis in selecting women for a recall exam. Likewise, Lourenco et al. reported both a 30% lower recall rate and a higher rate of cancer detection with 3DT versus digital mammography. 41 With regard to our hypothesis that rural areas may be disadvantaged in follow‐up mammography care, we confirmed that rural‐facing breast imaging centers in our study region of Appalachia, on average, had lower organizational capacity scores and less use of 3DT. 14 However, contrary to our expectation, we found that rural and metropolitan area‐located imaging centers in the study region had similar rates of both screening recall and biopsy, but differed in the timeliness of follow‐up care. Timeliness of an additional imaging evaluation is important for prompt breast cancer diagnosis among the small percentage of women who will be subsequently diagnosed with a breast malignancy, and to reduce the length of anxiety or worry among the approximately 8% of women who need an additional exam. Interestingly, the timeliness of recall and biopsy by rurality was divergent. Patients screened at rural‐facing facilities logged a shorter interval between their screening and recall, but a longer interval between their diagnostic exam and biopsy. Specifically, patients screened at rural‐facing imaging centers had, on average, 2 days fewer between their screening mammogram and recall exam than patients seen by metropolitan centers, even though rural‐facing facilities had lower organizational capacity. Thus, we did not find an adverse effect of lower facility capacity or infrastructure on patient recall efficiency. One explanation for the lack of a direct relationship of capacity strength to timeliness of screening recall is that special effort is often made by rural‐facing facilities to prioritize patient time and transportation barriers. This is consistent with a separate qualitative study reported by the authors, from interviews of facility staff in which key informants described how rural‐facing screening facilities strive to adjust schedules around patient needs. 42 In the present study, we found that more patients screened by rural‐facing centers had same‐day recall than metropolitan centers (9.1% vs 4.2%, P = .021). We also found that rural‐facing sites had a higher ratio of machines to patient volume than metropolitan sites (mean of 4.1 vs 2.3, P .0001, per 100,000 patients), which suggests that even though rural‐facing facilities have fewer mammogram machines than metropolitan facilities, they do not appear to lack relative capacity for screening. For patients, the higher ratio of machine availability could plausibly translate into shorter waiting times for additional views to be performed. In stark contrast to recall times, delays in time from recall to biopsy were greater for patients seen at rural‐facing imaging centers. The longer delay of approximately 5 days for rural women may reflect health system‐level factors, beyond the control of the imaging center. This could include delays imposed by external labs or health care providers, and inefficiencies in navigating different electronic medical record systems. 43 Thuspatients seen at imaging centers with limited resources and weaker network affiliations may have longer delays with biopsy; however, some of this disadvantage can be offset by less delay with the preceding diagnostic exam. Our modeling strategy deliberately separated patient‐level predictors (with facility random effects) from facility‐level pathways (SEM). This reduces overadjustment and collinearity (eg, between exam‐level tomosynthesis use and facility‐level tomosynthesis adoption) while preserving interpretability. The sensitivity analysis confirms that capacity and market share relate to biopsy after recall, reinforcing the SEM inference that infrastructure is the dominant facility construct. The SEM models are perhaps most intriguing as they are one of the first to examine the role of the facility organizational factors on breast cancer screening rates for recall and biopsy. A key finding was that the underlying concept of facility infrastructure had the most prominent effect on days to recall and biopsy. By adding descriptors for service‐area size, distance, uptake, and market share, we clarify the pathways through which community context shapes timeliness—primarily by conditioning access and patient flow—without altering the core estimates. Separate indicators for the latter of screening capacity, 3DT, number of machines, and radiologic support operate wholly through the latent infrastructure factor and thus do not exert direct effects on mammogram outcomes. This means that we should broaden our models and policy initiatives to include more than just the need for a certain number of machines or 3DT, but to include the need for ancillary operational and professional resources. 33 The latter effects are distinct from a separate set of influences from the poverty of the service area and its rurality. This suggests that imaging center factors should be collected alongside measures of community access and health equity factors to better understand breast cancer screening outcomes and their disparities. Similar to findings from patient‐level data, rural‐facing facilities had a shorter time to recall than metropolitan facilities, independent of the level of facility resources. For time‐to‐biopsy, rural‐facing facilities were similar to metropolitan facilities, but facilities that only performed screening had shorter time to biopsy after the diagnostic exam. In bivariate data, the latter benefit was a mean difference of approximately 6 days (13.1 vs 19.2). Screening‐only breast centers (n = 31) tend to be clinics that operate within a larger health system or hospital, or are mobile units. These centers appear to be able to schedule biopsy services more quickly, likely through a shared health record system. Thus, being embedded within a larger health system may have the advantage of better integration with biopsy services. Another key finding is that, unlike rurality, which was associated with fewer days to recall following screening, poverty, as assessed by ADI, was not associated with facility delays in either time to recall or biopsy. Instead, the role of poverty in Appalachia was through community conditions linked to lower uptake of mammograms. This suggests that breast centers can have an important role through community outreach and navigation to reduce barriers to access. Finally, it is important to consider some limitations of our study. Some of these include: a lack of granularity in claims data as population‐health indicators; a limited focus on Appalachia imaging facilities and only among women enrolled in Medicare Part B; and exclusion of FDA‐registered facilities (n = 146), which did not complete a study survey, facilities with low‐quality claims, or missing survey items. However, we believe that our results, which found effects from ADI, rurality, capacity uptake, distance, and market share as community proxies for biopsy and recall events, are pivotal. Overall, support for the role of community and organizational infrastructure in mammogram quality is robust and makes an important contribution to the literature on rural health. While we did not estimate potential for bias from missing facility data, we note (see Figure S1 in Supplementary Appendix) that only 13 facilities had missing Capacity score data, and 7 were excluded due to low‐quality claims data. We believe that the choice of data quality versus the maximal number of study facilities improves precision. CONCLUSIONS Rural‐facing breast imaging sites in our study region of Appalachia appear to leverage their resources and community presence to achieve similar mammogram recall and biopsy outcomes to metropolitan centers. Across all centers, the level of organizational infrastructure is a key factor in the quality of mammograms. Future research is needed to examine the extent that imaging facility capacity and technology effects can have downstream influence on breast cancer mortality; screening outcomes in younger women ( 65 years); and the impact of mobile mammography units and health care system consolidation on screening uptake, recall, and follow‐up care.
