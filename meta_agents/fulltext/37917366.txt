Introduction Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection poses a substantial global health burden, affecting both women and men. 1 , 2 While most HPV infections are transient and asymptomatic, a subset of individuals with persistent infection may develop precancers and cancers. 3 Cervical cancer, which is entirely attributable to HPV, is the most prominent and well-recognized, driving high rates of morbidity and mortality, particularly in low- and-middle income countries. 4 However, HPV is also a causal agent in other cancers, including vulvar, vaginal, penile, anal, and certain head and neck cancers, collectively referred to as HPV-associated cancers. 2 In 2022, cervical cancer accounted for 662,301 new cases and 348,874 deaths worldwide. 5 Latin America contributed 63,171 new cervical cancer cases and 33,514 deaths, corresponding to approximately 9.5% of the global cervical cancer cases and deaths burden. In Brazil, there were an estimated 18,715 new cervical cancer cases in 2022, corresponding to an age-standardized incidence rate of 12.7 per 100,000, along with 9905 deaths (age-standardized mortality rate of 6.5 per 100,000). 5 These figures highlight the urgent need for comprehensive HPV prevention and control measures, aligned with the World Health Organization’s (WHO) goal of cervical cancer elimination, which aims to reduce the incidence to below 4 per 100,000 women. 6 While cervical cancer benefits from both vaccination and secondary prevention via screening, other HPV-associated cancers – despite having identifiable precursor lesions (except for head and neck cancers) – lack effective screening methods, making early detection difficult. Brazil´s national cervical cancer screening program, in place since 1988, has relied on opportunistic cytology for women aged 25 to 64 y. 7 However, coverage remains low – reaching only about 40% of eligible women – and over 60% of cervical cancer cases are diagnosed at advanced stages, limiting its effectiveness on mortality. 8 , 9 Although HPV testing was recommended for incorporation into the national screening program in 2024, 10 its implementation remains limited and is primarily restricted to pilot studies. 11 In parallel, Brazil has invested in improving cancer treatment infrastructure to reduce disparities in access and outcomes across the country. 12 Efforts include expanding diagnostic and treatment services, training programs for oncology professionals, and integrating cancer care into the broader health system. Despite progress, challenges persist in ensuring timely access to care and adopting new technologies. 13 A major milestone in primary prevention was the introduction of the quadrivalent HPV vaccination program in 2014 for girls, followed by the inclusion of boys in 2017. Over time, the program has expanded to include additional high-risk subpopulations – such as people living with HIV, transplant recipients, and cancer patients – and extended age eligibility up to 45 y for these groups. 14 , 15 This study aims to provide a comprehensive overview of HPV-associated cancer trends in Brazil, capturing patterns in hospital discharge and mortality data from 2011 to 2019. The COVID-19 pandemic years were excluded due to their disruptive effects on healthcare services and data reporting. By offering a breakdown by cancer type and sex, this analysis enables an in-depth understanding of population-specific impacts and identifies areas where public health efforts need to be intensified to effectively reduce the burden of HPV-associated cancers. Materials and methods Study design and population This observational, descriptive, and retrospective study analyzed national secondary data on HPV-associated cancers in Brazil from 2011 to 2019, including both females and males, and incorporated an ecological time-trend analysis. The study population included all individuals diagnosed with HPV-associated cancers, identified through national health databases using ICD-10 codes for cancers of the cervix, vulva, vagina, penis, anus, oropharynx, oral cavity, and larynx. These codes were recorded either as the primary diagnosis for hospitalizations or as the underlying cause of death (Table S1). Data sources Data were obtained from the Hospital Information System (SIH) and the Mortality Information System (SIM), both maintained by the Brazilian Ministry of Health. 16 , 17 The SIM captures nearly all deaths nationwide, while the SIH includes hospitalizations within Brazil’s public health system, covering about 75–80% of all hospitalizations in the country. Demographic data from the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE) were used to calculate population-based rates. 18 All datasets are de-identified and publicly accessible. Definitions HPV-associated cancers were defined by anatomical sites where HPV-DNA is frequently found, irrespective of individual HPV confirmation, representing the total number of hospitalizations and deaths recorded in the databases. 19 HPV-attributable cancers were defined as those likely caused by HPV, estimated using HPV-attributable fractions. 2 To derive these fractions, we reviewed recent literature, prioritizing large, robust studies from Latin America, particularly Brazil, when available (Table S2). Eligible studies applied polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to detect HPV-DNA in tumor samples; for vulvar and specific types of head and neck cancers, p16 INK4a overexpression was also considered. The HPV-attributable fraction was defined as the proportion of positive samples for these biomarkers. 2 , 20 , 21 Oral cavity, oropharyngeal, and laryngeal cancers were studied both separately by anatomical site and collectively, with the latter grouping them under head and neck cancers. Statistical analysis We calculated crude rates of HPV-associated and HPV-attributable cancer-related hospitalizations and deaths, stratified by cancer type and sex. Crude rates were computed by dividing the number of events by the corresponding population and multiplying the result by 100,000. For each outcome, we calculated the average number of events per period, annual rates, and the average rate for the full 2011–2019 period. To visualize and compare age patterns, we generated ridgeline plots depicting the relative frequency of hospitalizations and deaths across age groups, normalized within each cancer type and sex. Median ages were calculated by cancer type and sex for the same period and were overlaid to highlight central tendencies. We also examined the age distribution of HPV-associated cancer hospitalizations and deaths across predefined age groups ( 30, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, and ≥60 y), disaggregated by cancer type and sex. To assess differences between sexes, rate ratios were calculated by dividing male by female hospitalization rates for oropharyngeal, laryngeal, and oral cavity cancers, and female by male rates for anal cancer. Additionally, joinpoint regression was used to analyze time-trend changes in hospitalization and mortality rates for HPV-associated cancers, which were age-standardized using the WHO world standard population to enable comparability over time and between groups. 22 The joinpoint method partitions the study period into contiguous linear segments, each characterized by its own slope, thereby revealing changes in trend direction or magnitude. 23 The maximum number of joinpoints allowed by the software was set to one. The optimal model was selected using the Weighted Bayesian Information Criterion, a data-adaptive approach that balances goodness-of-fit and model simplicity, retaining only meaningful changes in trend. 24 After logarithmic transformation of the rates, we calculated the annual percentage change (APC) for each segment defined by joinpoints, as well as the average annual percentage change (AAPC) for the entire study period, both with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Analyses were stratified by sex, and statistical significance was set at p = .05. Significant APC or AAPC values ( p ≤ .05) were interpreted as evidence of change in rates over time, while non-significant values ( p .05) indicated relative stability. Segments with statistically significant trends are marked with an asterisk in figure legends and corresponding tables. Ethics Committee approval was not required for this study, as it utilized publicly accessible and de-identified data. 25 Statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.13.0) and joinpoint regression analyses were conducted using the Joinpoint version 5.4.0 (Statistical Research and Applications Branch, National Cancer Institute). 26 Results Burden of HPV-associated and HPV-attributable cancers hospitalizations Hospitalizations for HPV-associated and HPV-attributable cancers are summarized in Table 1 and Figure S1, stratified by condition and sex, along with average annual numbers and crude rates per 100,000 population. Between 2011 and 2019 in Brazil, a total of 493,462 hospitalizations for HPV-associated cancers were recorded, with 262,397 estimated to be attributable to HPV. Among females, there were 277,893 HPV-associated cancer hospitalizations, of which 224,292 were attributable to HPV. This corresponds to an average of 24,921 HPV-attributable hospitalizations per year and a crude rate of 24.0 per 100,000 women. Among males, 215,569 HPV-associated hospitalizations were reported in the same period, with 38,105 estimated to be attributable to HPV, resulting in a crude rate of 4.3 per 100,000 men. Cervical cancer accounted for the highest burden, with 195,035 total hospitalizations, all attributable to HPV, representing 39.5% of all HPV-associated hospitalizations and 74.3% of HPV-attributable hospitalizations across both sexes. Anal cancer also had a notable impact, with 27,320 hospitalizations, contributing 5.5% of HPV-associated and 10.4% of HPV-attributable hospitalizations. Head and neck cancers combined accounted for 235,815 hospitalizations (47.9% of total HPV-associated hospitalizations), though only 24,094 (9.2%) were attributable to HPV. Within HPV-associated head and neck cancers in males, laryngeal cancer had the highest hospitalization burden, with 89,105 hospitalizations (9.9 per 100,000 men), followed by oropharyngeal cancer with 56,102 hospitalizations (6.3 per 100,000 men). However, after applying HPV-attributable fractions, oropharyngeal cancer emerged as the leading contributor to HPV-attributable hospitalizations (12,399; 1.4 per 100,000 men) compared with 4010 hospitalizations (0.4 per 100,000 men). Males generally had higher hospitalization rates for head and neck cancers compared to females, with male-to-female rate ratios of 6.2 for laryngeal cancer, 4.2 for oropharyngeal cancer, and 2.5 for oral cavity cancer. Among males, penile cancer represented 8.0% of HPV-associated hospitalizations (17,338; 1.9 per 100,000 men) and 23.3% of HPV-attributable hospitalizations (8,843; 1.0 per 100,000 men). Burden of HPV-associated and HPV-attributable cancers mortality During the study period, 71,995 HPV-associated cancer deaths were recorded among females (7.7 per 100,000 women), of which 57,870 were estimated to be attributable to HPV (6.2 per 100,000 women), resulting in an average of 6430 deaths annually. In comparison, 68,335 HPV-associated cancer deaths occurred in males (7.6 per 100,000 men), with 9866 estimated to be attributable to HPV (1.1 per 100,000 men), corresponding to an average of 1096 deaths per year. Cervical cancer again represented the largest mortality burden, with 52,383 deaths (5.6 per 100,000 women) accounting for 37.3% of all HPV-associated deaths and 77.3% of HPV-attributable deaths across both sexes. Vaginal and vulvar cancers had smaller, yet noteworthy contributions, with 1081 and 3597 deaths, respectively, of which 843 and 770 were attributable to HPV. Although smaller in absolute numbers, anal cancer remained significant, with 4099 deaths (0.2 per 100,000), making up 2.9% of HPV-associated and 6.0% of HPV-attributable deaths. Among females, the average annual mortality rate was 0.3 per 100,000 women, compared to 0.2 per 100,000 men, resulting in a female-to-male rate ratio of 1.5. Head and neck cancers collectively contributed heavily to mortality, with 75,519 total deaths (7,780 attributable to HPV), representing 53.8% of HPV-associated mortality and 11.5% of HPV-attributable mortality. Mortality rates for these cancers were consistently higher in males than females. The male-to-female mortality rate ratios were 7.6 for laryngeal, 5.5 for oropharyngeal, and 2.8 for oral cavity cancer. Penile cancer accounted for 3651 HPV-associated deaths (1,862 attributable to HPV), representing 2.2% of HPV-associated deaths and 15.3% of HPV-attributable deaths in males ( Table 2 and Figure S2). Age patterns of HPV-associated cancer hospitalization and mortality Age-specific hospitalization and mortality rates for HPV-associated cancers in Brazil from 2011 to 2019 reveal distinct patterns by age and sex ( Figure 1 ). In females, cervical cancer hospitalizations peaked at a median age of 47, while mortality increased progressively with age, remaining highest among older females. Other HPV-associated cancers in females showed later onset, with median hospitalization ages between 58 and 60 y, and vulvar cancer peaked at age 65. Mortality generally occurred 5 to 14 y after hospitalization, with the oldest age groups showing the highest mortality rates. Among males, median ages at hospitalization ranged from 58 to 61 y. Mortality patterns followed closely, with a lag of approximately 2 to 6 y, and the highest rates observed in the oldest age groups, particularly for laryngeal and oropharyngeal cancers. Figure 1. Age-specific hospitalization and mortality rates and median age for HPV-associated cancers, by cancer site and sex, Brazil, 2011–2019. These age patterns are reflected in the distribution of HPV-associated cancer hospitalizations and mortality across age groups (Figure S3). Among females, cervical cancer displayed the youngest age profile, with a substantial proportion of hospitalizations and deaths occurring before age 50. Vulvar cancer, in contrast, was more frequent among older women, showing the oldest age distribution across both hospitalization and mortality outcomes. Other cancer types in females exhibited intermediate patterns, with a gradual shift toward older age groups. In males, age distributions were relatively consistent across cancer types, with hospitalizations and deaths predominantly concentrated in individuals aged 60 and older, particularly for oropharyngeal and laryngeal cancers. Minor variations across cancer types were observed, but the general pattern in males was more homogeneous than in females. Although vulvar cancer exhibited the oldest age distribution among HPV-associated cancers in females, this pattern shifted when HPV-attributable fractions were applied. Vulvar cancer is the only cancer for which age-specific attribution fractions differ—48% in females under 60 and only 15% in those 60 and older – resulting in a meaningful difference between the age distribution of HPV-associated and HPV-attributable hospitalizations. After applying these fractions, hospitalizations were distributed as follows: 5.4% in females under 30, 8.1% in those aged 30–39, 20.5% for 40–49, 32.6% for 50–59, and 33.4% for those 60 and older. For mortality, the distribution was 1.6%, 2.7%, 12.2%, 26.9%, and 56.6%, respectively. These adjustments shift the hospitalization profile toward middle age and reduce the relative contribution of the oldest age group. For all other HPV-associated cancers, because the attributable fraction does not vary by age, the age distribution of HPV-attributable hospitalizations mirrors that of HPV-associated hospitalizations. For all HPV-associated cancers combined, females showed a decline from 2011 to 2016, followed by an increase from 2016 to 2019, resulting in an overall reduction (AAPC: −1.4%) over the period. While overall patterns were consistent with age-adjusted trends, some discrepancies were observed. For cervical cancer, crude rates indicated stability rather than decline. Discussion This study provides a comprehensive assessment of the burden of HPV-associated and HPV-attributable cancers in Brazil from 2011 to 2019, based on national hospitalization and mortality data. The findings reveal a substantial and persistent public health impact, with an estimated 29,000 hospitalizations and 7500 deaths attributable to HPV per year across both sexes. The burden was disproportionately higher among females, primarily due to cervical cancer. In males, the leading contributors were penile, anal, and oropharyngeal cancers. Overall, most HPV-associated cancers exhibited stable hospitalization and mortality trends, with exceptions including increases in cervical and anal cancers and reductions in certain head and neck cancer types. Specifically, our analysis showed that cervical cancer hospitalization rates declined significantly until 2016 (APC: −4.7%), followed by a rebound from 2016 to 2019 (APC: 3.9%), along with a slight increase in mortality rates over the period (AAPC: 0.7%). In 2022, 94% of cervical cancer deaths occurred in low- and middle-income countries, including Brazil, where elimination remains a distant goal due to persistent inequalities in prevention and care. 27–29 The North and Northeast regions, marked by poverty and limited healthcare access, have the highest incidence and mortality rates in Brazil, while the more affluent South and Southeast benefit from better screening and treatment. Women with lower education and income, particularly those relying on the public health system, are more likely to be diagnosed at advanced stages. 30 , 31 Ethnic disparities also persist, with Black and mixed-ethnicity women showing higher mortality even within the same regions. 32 These patterns underscore the urgent need for equitable health policies that address structural barriers and ensure timely, high-quality care. Implementation barriers are most acute in distant municipalities, riverine areas, and Indigenous Special Health Districts, where limited laboratory capacity and long travel distances reduce screening uptake. In these contexts, HPV testing with self-collection can increase coverage, and point-of-care adjuncts such as the TruScreen device – evaluated in Brazil with high sensitivity for cervical intraepithelial neoplasia grade 3 or worse (~80%) – may support same-day triage when cytology infrastructure is limited. 33 Although not a substitute for HPV testing or Pap smears, TruScreen provides immediate results, requires minimal training, and operates without laboratory support, making it suitable for low‑resource settings and helping reduce delays and loss to follow‑up. 33 , 34 Beyond cervical cancer, strengthening linkages to referral centers, implementing telemedicine, and deploying mobile outreach models can enhance access, timeliness, and continuity of care across HPV-associated cancers, particularly in geographically isolated communities. 35 , 36 This analysis highlights the broader impact of HPV beyond cervical cancer, particularly among men in Brazil. Although head and neck and penile cancers account for a substantial share of HPV-associated events, after applying site-specific attributable fractions, oropharyngeal cancer emerged as the leading HPV-attributable cancer among men, followed by anal cancer. This distribution is consistent with evidence from Latin America indicating a limited etiologic role of HPV in laryngeal and oral cavity cancers, contrasted with a more prominent contribution in oropharyngeal and penile cancers. For these latter sites, the attributable fractions applied should be considered conservative. Limitations in HPV detection can bias attribution in both directions: HPV-DNA may be undetectable in HPV-driven tumors, with false-negative PCR results due to low viral copy number, integration-related loss of target regions, assay selection, or sample degradation. 37 Conversely, DNA positivity alone is not specific for causality in head and neck cancers, underscoring the need for biomarkers of transcriptionally active infection in epidemiologic studies. 38 While some site-specific declines were observed in our analysis, such as in laryngeal and oral cavity cancers in males, overall trends remained largely stable. These declines are consistent with the impact of tobacco control policies, which have reduced smoking prevalence and, in turn, the incidence of these cancers. 39 , 40 In contrast, HPV-related oropharyngeal cancer has been rising among individuals born after 1984 in Brazil, driven by HPV infection and mirroring global trends. 41 Anal and penile cancers, which receive comparatively less attention in public health discourse, contributed significantly to the HPV burden in men in the present study. Penile cancer, in particular, remains a significant public health concern due to its disproportionately high incidence in Brazil, with age-standardized rates reaching up to 8.3 per 100,000 men in the North and Northeast regions, where it accounts for approximately 2% of all male cancers. 42–44 We observed a notable rise in anal cancer burden from 2017 to 2019 in both sexes in hospitalization and mortality in Brazil. These trends are consistent with increasing incidence observed in other regions 45–47 and may reflect broader changes in sexual behavior and limited screening options 45 , 48–51 The literature identifies distinct risk factors for anal cancer by sex. In females, a history of genital pre-cancer or cancer is associated with increased risk, likely due to shared exposure to oncogenic HPV types and the presence of transformation zone in both the cervix and anal canal. 52–54 In males, elevated risk is primarily observed among those who engage in receptive anal intercourse and among individuals living with HIV, where immunosuppression contributes to persistent HPV infection. 55 Although international guidelines recommend anal cancer screening in high-risk groups, including people living with HIV, 56 Brazil’s public health system (SUS) does not yet offer formal screening protocols. The 2024 national HIV clinical guidelines highlight clinical vigilance and recommend cytology and high-resolution anoscopy as diagnostic tools, but access remains limited, and implementation is not standardized. 57 Distinct age-related patterns in HPV-associated cancers were observed in our analysis, aligned with global observations. 58 Cervical cancer stands out for its earlier onset, with hospitalizations peaking at a median age of 47 and mortality at 56, likely due to the high susceptibility of the cervical transformation zone. In contrast, other HPV-associated cancers tend to occur later in life, with median age at hospitalization typically between 58 and 65 y. Head and neck cancers are more common in men, likely due to greater exposure to risk factors like smoking, alcohol use, and oral HPV infection. These cancers also reflect the cumulative effects of aging, cofactors, and the potential for reinfection or reactivation of latent high-risk HPV types. 59–63 Additionally, the later onset and narrower interval between hospitalization and death observed in males align with patterns seen in other cancers affecting both sexes. Delayed treatment initiation and higher rates of hospitalization are more frequent in males, contributing to poorer outcomes. 64 , 65 These patterns are influenced by delayed health-seeking behavior, higher burden of multimorbidity among males, and inequalities in access to prevention, early detection, and treatment services. 66–69 This study has some limitations. First, it relies on secondary data from national health information systems (SIH and SIM), which, while comprehensive, may be affected by underreporting, misclassification, or regional inconsistencies in data quality. 70 Second, HPV attribution was estimated using published fractions rather than individual-level HPV testing, which may introduce uncertainty, especially for cancers with lower attributable fractions. Third, the exclusion of private healthcare data may lead to underestimation of the total burden. In Brazil, patients in the private sector are more likely to be diagnosed at earlier stages due to better access to screening and diagnostic services. This often results in lower hospitalization and mortality rates compared to those treated in the public system. Finally, interpreting disease trends requires careful consideration of epidemiological factors as HPV prevalence – shaped by behavioral patterns, vaccination, and cofactors like HIV – along with access to screening and cancer treatment, all of which influence hospitalization and mortality rates. 71 Among the strengths of our study were the use of Brazil´s SIH and SIM systems, which provide a solid foundation for analyzing trends in HPV-associated cancer. Despite some limitations, these systems have consistently captured health data over recent decades, enabling valuable insights into cancer epidemiology. 72 The SIH covers around 75% of hospitalizations through the public health system, though regional disparities and data quality issues – such as limited training and high staff turnover – may affect consistency. 73 , 74 However, this impact may be minimal for cancer diagnoses, which are generally well-defined. The SIM has achieved nearly complete national coverage, with significant improvements over the last two decades to reduce the percentage of ill-defined and garbage codes as well as including better classification of previously unspecified cancer deaths. 75 Our findings reflect the pre – COVID-19 period (2011–2019). Since then, the pandemic has substantially disrupted cervical cancer control in Brazil, with national and state-level data indicating missed or delayed Pap tests, reduced oncology admissions and procedures, and a shift toward later-stage disease, including increased initiation of palliative care. 76–78 Internationally, programs deployed HPV self-sampling to sustain screening during service disruptions. 79 In Brazil, pilots of HPV testing with self-collection have shown high acceptability and operational feasibility, yet nationwide implementation remains limited. Scaling these approaches could accelerate catch-up screening in underserved regions. 80 Taken together, the post-2020 period may have adversely affected HPV-associated cancer outcomes, indicating that our estimates likely represent a conservative baseline. Brazil’s national immunization program currently uses the quadrivalent HPV vaccine (6/11/16/18). Genotype data show substantial burdens of high‑risk types 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58 in invasive cervical cancer, together accounting for 20%. 81–83 These types also appear – at varying prevalences – in other HPV-associated cancers, including anal and penile cancer, while HPV-16 overwhelmingly dominates HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancers, supporting the relevance of continued molecular surveillance to contextualize prevention strategies and anticipate shifts in HPV-associated cancer burden over time. 21 , 82 , 84–86 Because the 9-valent vaccine (6/11/16/18/31/33/45/52/58) covers genotypes responsible for roughly 90% of cervical cancer in Brazil, its adoption would broaden prevention and better align national policy with the country’s epidemiology. 83 Reversing current trends of HPV-associated cancers in Brazil will require a coordinated approach aligned with the WHO cervical cancer elimination strategy: high vaccination coverage, effective screening, and timely treatment. HPV vaccination remains the cornerstone of long-term prevention, with early outcomes from Brazil’s immunization program already encouraging. 87 Despite the availability of treatment through Brazil’s public healthcare system, supported by initiatives such as the “60-Day Law,” 88 gaps remain in both the capacity and geographic distribution of specialized oncology centers. Achieving equity across regions and socioeconomic groups will depend on inclusive clinical perspectives – covering women, men, and gender-diverse populations at risk – and on post-pandemic recovery of screening and treatment capacity. Incorporating new technologies and therapeutic strategies is essential to improve survival and quality of life, especially for patients diagnosed at advanced stages.
