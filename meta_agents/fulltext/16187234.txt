1 Background Male breast cancer (MBC) is a rare disease accounting for approximately 1 % of all breast cancer. 1 , 2 Breast cancer has been traditionally regarded as a female-specific disease, leading to the lack of awareness of MBC. However, the disease burden of MBC has kept growing over the past decades. 3 Due to the deficiency of randomized controlled trials of MBC, treatment regimes were usually extrapolated from guidelines for female breast cancer (FBC) nowadays, 4 , 5 but substantial disparities have been observed between MBC and FBC. 6 Therefore, treatment regimens for females might not be fully applicable in males, leading to poor adherence and worse prognosis. 7 However, the prognostic outcomes of MBC compared with FBC in Asians remain inconsistent across studies. In Republic of Korea, the 5-year overall survival rate was lower for MBC than for FBC (76.2 % vs 88.9 %), 8 but a study in Hong Kong of China suggested that MBC did not show lower survival until 10 years later. 9 Previous studies in Chinese mainland were not convincing enough because of the small sample sizes (ranging from 24 to 106 male patients). 10 , 11 , 12 Furthermore, healthcare utilization for MBC has not been well studied so far, with only one study conducted globally. 13 Therefore, it is crucial to explore the current situation of MBC and provide more evidence for formulating policies and guidelines for this rare disease. Notably, race/ethnicity plays a part in the disparities in the disease burden of breast cancer. 14 , 15 Most studies on racial/ethnic differences have focused on African Americans for both MBC and FBC, whereas Asians were included in only a few studies and often classified into “others”. 16 , 17 , 18 In terms of MBC, a few studies reported the racial/ethnic disparities in incidence in the general population, of which Asian was combined with Pacific Islanders, Hispanic, or others. 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 Significant disparities in disease burden of FBC have been observed within Asian countries at the national level, 23 , 24 but the situation of MBC cannot be extrapolated due to the prominent differences between FBC and MBC. The situation within Asian ethnic groups remains unclear as well. In a word, no studies of ethnic disparities have been conducted in China, which contributed the most breast cancer patients (18.4 % in 2020) in the world. 25 , 26 As the third largest provincial-level administrative division in China, Inner Mongolia has a population of 4.25 million Mongolian, accounting for over 70 % of the Mongolian population in China and 50 % in the world. Inner Mongolia Regional Health Information Platform (IMRHIP) aims to address cancer health disparities across ethnicities by collecting and administrating healthcare information of residents in Inner Mongolia. 26 In this study, we aimed to outline ethnic disparities of disease burden of male breast cancer based on IMRHIP to provide further understanding of the rare disease. 2 Materials and methods 2.1 Data sources This retrospective cohort study used data from the IMRHIP. The IMRHIP integrated and collected data from National Basic Medical Insurance (NBMI), National Cancer Registry (NCR), Inpatient Medical Record Homepage Data, and Cause-of-death Reporting System (CDRS) since 2012 with provincial coverage of 95 % by 2020. 26 , 27 , 28 The configuration and working flow of the platform were shown in the Supplementary methods and Supplementary Fig. 1. The NBMI included the Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance database covering urban employees and the Urban Resident Basic Medical Insurance database covering unemployed urban and rural residents. Socioeconomic information, including ethnicity and residence, records of inpatient and outpatient services, diagnostic information, and cost information were obtained in NBMI. The information was registered when patients purchase health insurance and generated when they use the insurance account. The NCR collected and managed tumor incidence, death, and survival data, providing variables such as date of onset, disease diagnoses, tumor characteristics, etc. Inpatient medical record homepage data was derived from electronic medical records, including demographic information, hospitalization information, diagnosis and treatment information, etc. The CDRS gathered data from the National Health Commission, national disease surveillance sites, and provinces and cities through an information management system, where we obtained information on the direct cause of death, date of death, etc. Patient information, including the abovementioned variables, was recorded and linked by encrypted ID numbers among databases. All records were deidentified to protect patients’ privacy. The information about the Surveillance Cohort of All Cancers Species has been shared on the China Cohort Consortium (No. CCC2023050801). Informed consent from participants was approved for exemption, approved by the medical ethics committee (NMCDCIRB2021001). 2.2 Study population Patients diagnosed with breast cancer between January 1, 2012 and March 9, 2021 in the NBMI were identified by diagnostic text and the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) code of breast cancer (C50). First, the dictionary of breast cancer diagnostic names was created using records in the NBMI. Next, the diagnostic information in other sub-databases was used to match and double-check the results. Natural language processing was applied to standardize the diagnostic text and code, with two researchers conducting this process independently and a third one double-checking. More methodological details were shown in the Supplementary methods, Supplementary Table 1, and Supplementary Fig. 2. Patients without ID numbers ( n = 10) and female patients ( n = 34 955) were excluded. The patients were followed up until death or the end of the study, whichever came first. 2.3 Measurements The tumor stage was defined into early (stage 0–Ⅲ), advanced (stage Ⅳ), and unknown according to whether a breast cancer surgery can be operated on the patient. Age at diagnosis was categorized into three groups: 18–40, 41–60, and≥61 years because the association between age and outcomes were non-linear. Charlson comorbidity index (CCI) score was calculated and categorized as 0, 1, 2, and ≥3, 29 , 30 in which the comorbidities diagnosed both before and after the diagnosis of breast cancer were included. Data on alcohol consumption and smoking rates were obtained from the 2018 Adult Chronic Disease and Nutrition Monitoring Survey in Inner Mongolia. 26 Gross domestic product (GDP) per capita in 2020, 31 alcohol consumption rate, and smoking rate were stratified into two levels (high/low) by prefecture-level divisions (including leagues and cities) according to the median. Treatments for breast cancer included surgery, chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, targeted therapy, and radiotherapy. 32 2.4 Statistical analysis Our analyses included three parts. First, incidence and 5-year prevalence were calculated. For incidence rates, a window of two years (2012 and 2013) was set to exclude prevalent cases when calculating the incidence rate for each of the 7 years from 2014 to 2020. 27 , 33 , 34 For instance, if one’s diagnosis of breast cancer appeared in 2014 (or later) for the first time, he was considered to be a new case of that year. The numerator of incidence was the number of new cases of breast cancer each year. Sensitivity analysis using a 3-year washout period and a 4-year washout period was performed to test the robustness of this identifying strategy. For 5-year prevalence rates, the numerator was the number of patients alive with breast cancer diagnoses during the past 5 years for each year from 2017 to 2021. 27 The denominator for incidence and 5-year prevalence was the total number of male adult residents each year in NBMI. For example, for the rate of 2017, the numerator contained surviving patients whose incidence year was 2012, 2013, 2014, 2015, or 2016, and the denominator was the male adult residents in 2017 in NBMI. The average estimates in the subgroups were calculated by combining the estimates each year using a random effects meta-analysis 35 based on the Clopper-Pearson method. 36 The 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated by the Poisson distribution. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to the GDP level of the residence. Age-standardized rates (ASR) were determined using the World Health Organization world standardized rates, 2000 to 2025. 37 To make the ASR comparable, sensitivity analysis was demonstrated to calculate the ASR of incidence using different world standard population. 38 , 39 Second, health utilization, including overall services and specifically for breast cancer of inpatient and outpatient, was measured by costs per capita, annually per capita, and per episode (exchange rate: US $1.00 =¥6.47 yuan on July 15, 2020). The annual cost was equal to the total cost divided by the follow-up years (years after diagnosis). The cost of each kind of treatment was calculated. The number of inpatient and outpatient visits overall and specifically for breast cancer were also calculated. Third, 5-, 3-, 2-, and 1-year survival rates were calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method. 40 , 41 , 42 Log-rank test and z-test were used to compare ethnic disparities in survival rates. 43 A sensitivity analysis was conducted to offer unbiased estimations, calculating the rates by cohort analysis. 40 , 41 , 42 In survival analyses, since the proportional hazard assumption was not satisfied by stage, a stratified Cox model was performed for all-cause mortality. The Fine and Gray competing risk model and Cause-specific hazard model were performed for breast cancer-specific mortality to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and 95 % CI. Age at diagnosis, GDP level of residence, alcohol consumption rate, smoking rate, CCI, all treatments, and stage were adjusted. Mean±standard error (SD), median (interquartile range, IQR), and frequency (proportion) were presented for normal continuous variables, non-normal continuous variables, and categorical variables, respectively. χ 2 test, t-test, and Kruskal-Wallis test were performed to compare the difference between variables of interest. All statistical analyses were performed with R version 4.1.3, and a two-sided P 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data were analyzed and reported following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline 44 and the Strengthening The Reporting Of Cohort Studies in Surgery (STROCSS). 45 3 Results 3.1 Demographic characteristics In a total of 9.08 million participants (Supplementary Table 3), 630 male breast cancer patients were identified, contributing to 2539.4 person-years. Among them, 62 (9.8%) were Mongolian, and 521(82.7%) were Han. Han in MBC were more likely to be diagnosed 5 years older (60.0 years vs 55.4 years, P =0.016) and more distributed in the residence with high GDP level (56.% vs 37.1%, P =0.006). No significant results were found in follow-up years, stage, CCI score, smoking rate, and alcohol consumption rate ( Table 1 ). The difference between Han and Mongolian was not significant ( P =0.424) (Supplementary Table 4). The results of the sensitivity analysis of incidence rates calculation using different washout periods were displayed in Supplementary Table 2, showing conservative estimation. The ASRs of incidence using other world standard populations were shown in Supplementary Table 5 and Supplementary Table 6, which displayed consistent estimation. The combined crude incidence rate during 2014–2020 was 1.5 (95% CI: 0.8–2.1) per 100,000. The ASR of prevalence for MBC during 2017–2021 was 4.2 (95% CI: 3.0–5.5) per 100,000, and Mongolian’s was significantly higher than Han’s (3.2[2.5–4.0] vs 2.3[1.7–3.0], P = 0.016). The crude prevalence during 2017–2021 was 5.6 (95% CI: 4.2–6.9). The 5-year prevalence in 2021 was 3.0 (95% CI: 2.1–3.9) per 100 000 in MBC, of which Han was 3.1 (95% CI: 2.7–3.5) and Mongolian was 3.6 (95% CI: 2.5–5.0) (Supplementary Table 7). The age-specific incidence and prevalence showed unimodal distributions. For incidence, the age of peak for Han was 10 years older than that for Mongolian while for 5-year prevalence they were the same ( Fig. 1 ). Abbreviations: CI, confidence interval; GDP, gross domestic product; HR, hazard ratio. 4 Discussion Based on IMRHIP data from 2012 to 2021, we described the disease burden and healthcare utilization of male breast cancer in Inner Mongolia and concentrated on ethnic disparities between Han patients and Mongolian patients. Overall, no significant ethnic disparities were demonstrated in either aspect. We observed a moderate level of incidence of MBC in Inner Mongolia of China compared to other countries and regions. When compared with literature using different world standard populations, the ASR in this study have been transformed accordingly. The ASR of incidence of MBC (1.23 per 100,000) was first reported to be higher than previous studies in Asia including China 11 (0.17 per 100,000 in Zhongshan, Guangdong Province in 2016), Singapore 46 (0.40 per 100,000 in 2017), Japan 23 (0.18 per 100,000 during 1988–2002) and Republic of Korea 8 (0.22 per 100,000 in 2016). In contrast, it was close to the regions of high income in North America 47 (1.26 per 100,000 in 2017) and Israel 23 (1.24 per 100,000 during 1988–2002) as well as Asian Americans based on The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database 19 (0.66 per 100,000 during 1988–2000). Possible explanations are as follows. For one thing, previous studies were based on data from cancer registries 8 , 10 , 11 , 19 , 23 , 46 in which the incidence of MBC might be underreported. 48 Comparative studies about identifying cancer cases have found that insurance claims data can be used as an important implement in many countries, including China, 48 Republic of Korea, 49 the US, 50 and Germany. 51 Our study combined claim data and cancer registry data, thus reflecting the incidence more accurately. For another, population genetic susceptibility may play a role. 52 BRCA1/2 (e.g. 5296del4 in BRCA1 and 6174delT in BRCA2 ) mutation was considered as the crucial risk factor in breast cancer, and BRCA2 mutations were more common in MBC. 53 The BRCA2 mutation was more frequently observed in China than in other Asian countries, 54 resulting in a higher incidence in China. No significant ethnic disparities were found in incidence rates, survival rates, and prognosis patterns. It can be interpreted from the following perspectives. First, according to previous studies, a higher incidence of MBC may be associated with endocrine diseases like diabetes, 55 , 56 obesity, and disadvantaged lifestyles such as more alcohol and tobacco consumption. 57 However, there were no significant differences in the proportion of diabetes (data not shown), smoking rate, and alcohol consumption rate between Mongolian and Han MBC patients in this study, and the latter two factors were also proved to have no significant ethnic differences in other studies. 58 , 59 Second, adjusted multivariate analysis indicated ethnicity was not significantly associated with higher risks of all-cause and breast cancer-specific mortality. Third, the aforementioned population genetic susceptibility is a potential factor, but no specific gene and ethnic discrepancies have been found. In sum, no significant ethnic disparities were revealed in the disease burden. However, differences related to economic factors should receive attention. Previous studies suggested that sociodemographic factors like income and insurance could neutralize ethnic disparities, and poverty was a significant predictor of breast cancer mortality, 21 , 60 with which the results of this study were consistent, warning of potential disease burden in Mongolians. Policymakers should pay great attention to economic factors and keep eliminating underlying ethnic disparities. We observed a lower level of healthcare utilization compared with other counties and regions. According to prior studies, the per-episode cost of visits on breast cancer in China ranged from $2009.3 to $3554.9 (from 1996 to 2015), 61 with a median of approximately $3091.2 to $3864.0 (from 1996 to 2014). 62 The mean cost on MBC per inpatient episode in Sichuan was $1925.0 (from 2015 to 2018), 63 and the median cost in Jilin reached $3274.0 (2019). 64 The only study that estimated hospital costs of male breast cancer at the US population level reported an average cost of $9059 per hospital visit (from 2012 to 2016). 13 These figures were higher than those reported in this study. Possible explanations are as follows. First, different data sources may lead to bias. Most studies in China collected data from a single hospital or samples in certain areas, and few reported data at the provincial level 61 , 62 like this study. Second, the economic level affects the allocation of medical resources 65 and patients’ healthcare-seeking behaviors. Disadvantaged patients are more likely to reduce expenses or quit treatment. 66 , 67 Inner Mongolia has an economic gap compared with the aforementioned areas. Third, advances in medical technology contribute to the increase in healthcare costs, 68 and high per capita human capital loss is a factor of economic cost for high-income areas like the US, where people have better education attainment. 69 Yet, in Inner Mongolia, medical technology and the quantity and quality of human health resources need to be improved. 70 We first reported ethnic differences in healthcare utilization among MBC patients, 13 , 71 and no significant disparities were observed. It can be illustrated from four aspects. First, health insurance significantly influences the cost by affecting patients’ healthcare-seeking behaviors, which might be hindered if the patient is not being covered. 67 , 72 , 73 , 74 However, the universal medical insurance system based on the National Basic Medical Insurance (NBMI) has covered over 95 % of the population in Inner Mongolia in 2020, 27 , 75 and 630 patients included in the study were all covered. Second, a high degree of ethnic mixing co-habitation 76 , 77 and demographically equitable medical resource distribution 65 , 78 assist both ethnic groups to access healthcare equally. 79 Third, it is proven that the knowledge gaps of MBC 80 might lead to stigmatization and embarrassment, causing refusal or non-adherence to medical assistance. 81 However, few studies directly revealed the ethnic influence on knowledge access in MBC patients. 82 Educational level indirectly helps understand the result as it potentially affects people’s awareness of disease and treatments. 82 Studies have shown that educational equity in China at the ethnic level has made great progress since 1949. 83 Due to favorable policies and sufficient funds resulting from economic development, the educational gap between Mongolian and Han has gradually narrowed. 84 Lastly, favorable marital status might affect MBC patients’ healthcare-seeking behaviors by offering psychosocial and emotional support. 82 , 85 , 86 , 87 630 patients in this study displayed no significant ethnic difference in marital status, which might contribute to the understanding of our finding. Overall, this study highlighted the equity in utilization for MBC patients in Inner Mongolia and provided directions for further development of health equity. To our knowledge, this is the first study to uncover ethnic disparities in disease burden and healthcare utilization of male breast cancer based on 630 patients from 9.08 million general population in China. Our results should be considered within the context of study limitations. First, case ascertainment was limited due to the unavailability of laboratory data and imaging information by medical insurance. However, previous studies using NBMI to identify cancer cases have verified sensitivities and positive predictive values (PPVs) above 90 %. 48 , 88 Second, overseas treatments and part of patient-funded treatment programs, such as traditional Chinese medicine and medicine purchased through the Internet, are not included in the NBMI. Still, the NBMI provides a good picture of breast cancer patients’ utilization of regional health services. Third, the small sample size of Mongolian males and patients, especially those with follow-ups for more than 5 years, led to low statistical power in the analyses, calling for larger sample sizes. Moreover, tumor grade and subtype were not available, influencing further interpretation of our results. However, the results of disease burden carry substantial importance. 89 Finally, the aforementioned findings were only observed in Inner Mongolia, which requires external verification in other populations. 5 Conclusions In conclusion, we examined the ethnic differences in disease burden and healthcare utilization of MBC based on large-scale population-based data in Inner Mongolia. We reported the incidence, 5-year prevalence, prognosis and healthcare utilization in MBC, and the disparity of disease burden and medical utilization between Mongolian and Han MBC patients is not statistically significant. However, the study still calls for a greater focus on potential ethnic disparities due to the crucial influence of economic factors and different healthcare-seeking behavior patterns. Further research is needed to explore the potential mechanisms and provide policymakers with more precise recommendations.
