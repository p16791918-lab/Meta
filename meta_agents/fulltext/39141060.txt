1 Introduction Physical function and quality of life assessment in patients with advanced cancer presents unique challenges. Improvements in cancer survival rates have been documented [ 1 ]. However, patients with cancer and survivors frequently experience deleterious sequelae resulting from treatment including impairments in sleep, cognition, quality of life, and physical systems (cardiovascular, pulmonary, neurologic, musculoskeletal) [ 2 ]. These sequelae collectively compromise quality of life (QOL) and physical function and increase susceptibility to adverse health outcomes including premature mortality [ 3 , 4 ]. In advanced cancer populations, the low to moderate correlations between objective functional capacity measures and subjective function assessments (r values: 0.22 to 0.38) suggests that objective evaluation provides a potentially useful and independent endpoint [ 5 ]. Applying objective and subjective assessments in tandem with QOL and symptom assessments allows for more robust evaluation of this patient population. Patients with cancer living in rural areas face additional barriers to high‐quality cancer care when compared to urban patients with cancer. These barriers are experienced by rural people globally [ 6 ]. Barriers specifically noted in the United States include geographic isolation, limited access to specialized oncology services [ 7 ], reduced availability of physical rehabilitation resources [ 8 ], and socioeconomic constraints [ 9 ]. Despite 20% of U.S. based patients with cancer living in rural areas [ 10 ], cancer services are less prevalent. Only 3% of medical oncologists in the United States practice in rural areas [ 11 ]. Further, patients living in rural areas report traveling six to ten times farther for chemotherapy compared to patients living in urban areas [ 12 ]. There is also the added financial cost of traveling, additional time off work, and/or staying away from home which is exacerbated for the ~2 million rural households in the U.S. who do not own cars [ 13 ]. Rurality is not a monolithic construct, as rural areas encompass tremendous heterogeneity in population density, economic characteristics, geographic isolation, and healthcare infrastructure that can significantly influence cancer outcomes. Many studies compare rural versus urban areas as dichotomous variables, which masks the considerable variation of disparities across the spectrum of rurality [ 14 ]. The Rural–Urban Continuum Codes (RUCC) [ 15 ] and Rural–Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) [ 16 ] classification systems defined by the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) demonstrate this complexity by distinguishing between metropolitan counties/areas (non‐rural), micropolitan areas (large rural cities/towns), and small town/isolated rural areas with higher RUCC and RUCA scores indicating greater rurality. The 2020 census tract RUCA codes data are available for the United States and its territories [ 16 ], with the RUCA codes created using census tract data to provide flexible rural–urban classification [ 16 ]. Exploring differences across the rural continuum is essential because the RUCA measure incorporates both population density and travel distance. This will help with understanding whether tailored approaches are needed to address the needs of patients with advanced cancer in more versus less rural settings. The purpose of this secondary, post hoc, exploratory analysis is to describe objective and subjective physical function, symptoms, and QOL measures in patients with advanced cancer living in rural areas, toward the goal of developing appropriate assessment protocols, informing treatment decisions, and designing targeted interventions that address this unique patient population. 2 Materials and Methods The primary aim of the Nurse AMIE trial is to assess the effects of a technology‐based supportive care platform on overall survival among 348 patients with advanced cancer living in rural areas [ 17 ]. Baseline measurements included demographics, physical function (including objective and subjective assessments), symptoms and QOL. This paper is a secondary, post hoc, exploratory analysis of baseline data to describe objective and subjective physical function, symptoms and QOL assessments of the Nurse AMIE cohort and to conduct a cross‐sectional comparison of these values according to micropolitan RUCA codes ( 7) versus small town/rural RUCA codes (7–10). All participants provided written consent, the study was approved by the WCG IRB ( www.wcgclinical.com , IRB Tracking Number: 20216947, Approved 01/27/2022), and the clinical trial was registered with Clinical Trials.gov as NCT05221606 . Patients were identified through the electronic medical record and recruited from UPMC Hillman Cancer Center locations in Western PA, Mount Nittany Hospital in State College, PA, Penn State Cancer Institute in Hershey, PA, and West Virginia University in Morgantown, WV. Patients were approached for consent and measurements at a regularly scheduled infusion visit between May 2022 and March 2025. Eligible patients were diagnosed with advanced cancer of any tumor site, defined as stage 3 or 4 or. Patients with cancers that are either unstaged (some hematologic malignancies) or differently staged (brain tumors) were included if their oncology care team agreed that (1) they had a life expectancy of greater than 6 months and (2) they were deemed to have ‘advanced’ cancer by the care team. All enrolled patients live in a rural setting defined as living in a county with a Rural Urban Continuum Code (RUCC) of 4–9 [ 15 ] or a ZIP code associated with a Rural–Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) code of 4–10 [ 16 ]. Other eligibility criteria are detailed elsewhere [ 17 ]. 2.1 Measurements Demographics (e.g., age, sex, race, household income, marital status, education) were ascertained by self‐report. Rurality was categorized using USDA RUCA codes, which are based on population density and commuting patterns, and which range from 1 (most urban) to 10 (most rural and isolated) and are based on population density and commuting patterns. For the Nurse AMIE study, we purposively focused on patients in rural settings (RUCA ≥ 4). For the purpose of this specific analysis, we dichotomized the level of rurality as ‘micropolitan’ (RUCA code ≤ 6) and ‘small town/rural’ (RUCA code 6) [ 16 ]. The ‘micropolitan’ codes (RUCA 4–6) denote large rural towns with some urban connections. The ‘small town/rural codes (RUCA 7–10) denote a range from small rural towns and areas to very remote rural areas with minimal connection to cities. Clinical characteristics (i.e., cancer type, stage (AJCC TNM), treatment (surgery, chemotherapy, radiation)) were abstracted from the electronic medical record. Most hematologic and brain malignancies were not staged. Anthropometrics were ascertained from the medical record on the same day participants provided consent and completed all other baseline surveys. The objective assessment of physical function was accomplished by trained research staff using the Short Physical Performance Battery, which includes a total score (Range = 0–12) and three sub scores (Range 0–4): standing balance, gait speed, and repeated chair stand (time to sit and stand five times) [ 18 ]. Scores of 9 or less are associated with increased risk of disability, falls, and mortality and a difference of 1 point is considered a meaningful change in physical function [ 19 , 20 ]. Minimal Clinically Important Differences for the subscales include 1.2, 0.5, and 0.9 points for balance, gait speed, and chair stand [ 21 ]. The subjective assessment of physical function, symptoms, and QOL included sections of the PROMIS 29_2 Profile v2.1 (PROPr) (including the following subscales: anxiety, depression, sleep, and cognition) (score range = 0–100; minimal clinically important difference range from 2 to 6 for all subscales) [ 22 , 23 ] and SF‐36 (including the following subscales: physical functioning scale, physical health problems scale, pain scale, general health perceptions scale, emotional well‐being scale, emotional health problems scale, social functioning scale, and energy/fatigue scale), with minimal clinically important differences ranging from 2 to 4 points for each subscale [ 24 , 25 ]. 2.2 Statistical Analysis The sample size for the Nurse AMIE study was determined based on the primary outcome for the trial (overall survival at 2 years). Continuous measures were summarized with means and standard deviations, and categorical measures were summarized with frequencies and percentages. Health outcomes were compared between groups using both univariate and multivariable methods. The unadjusted, univariable approach used independent samples t ‐tests, and the multivariable approach utilized linear regression controlling for demographic and clinical characteristics that showed between‐group differences with a p ‐value of 0.15 or less. Statistical significance was set at 0.05, and all tests were two‐sided and performed in SAS version 9.4. No corrections were made for multiple comparisons. 3 Results 3.1 Study Sample Characteristics The study screened 2192 patients in the electronic medical records to identify 716 preliminarily eligible patients. Of these, 692 were approached for consent and 50.4% of them enrolled and were randomized ( N = 348) (Figure 1 ). There were 343 patients approached who were not interested in participating. Comparisons of approached patients who were randomized versus not randomized revealed no significant differences regarding sex, race, stage, or receipt of curative versus palliative care. There was a difference in age, with the average age of randomized patients being 65, versus 69 for those not interested ( p 0.0001). Of the randomized patients, 263 (76%) reside in micropolitan areas (RUCA 7) and 85 (24%) in small town/rural areas (RUCA 7–10). Most participants were married or living with a partner (68%), with this proportion being higher in micropolitan areas (70%) compared to small town/rural areas (61%). Approximately half of the sample (51%) was retired, with similar distributions across rurality groups. Educational attainment showed that 43% were high school graduates, 28% had some college education, and 21% had completed a 4‐year college degree or higher. Household income distribution revealed that 46% of participants earned less than $50,000 annually, with 22% earning $50,000–$74,999. 3.3 Clinical Characteristics The most common cancer types were colorectal (18%, n = 61), lung (17%, n = 58), hematologic (14%, n = 49), prostate (10%, n = 35), and breast (10%, n = 35), ovarian (4%, n = 13), esophageal (3%, n = 11), and pancreatic cancers (4%, n = 15). Other cancer types including renal, head and neck, and melanoma each comprised 3% of the sample, with 6% classified as other cancer types. Cancer staging showed 1 patient entered the trial with stage 2 cancer (in error), 29% with Stage 3 disease, 55% with Stage 4, and 16% with unstaged cancer. The majority of patients (88%) received chemotherapy as their primary treatment, with smaller proportions receiving radiation only (3%) or combined chemo‐radiation therapy (7%). A total of 109 patients were receiving treatment for curative intent (31%); the remainder ( N = 229, 66%) were receiving treatment for palliative purposes. Care was being provided prior to surgery for 6 patients (2%), after surgery for 138 patients (40%), and surgery was not part of treatment for 192 patients (55%). There were notable differences in surgical status by rurality: 38% of micropolitan patients were post‐surgery, compared with 44% of small town/rural patients. Similarly, surgery was not a part of treatment for 59% of micropolitan patients, compared with 45% of small town/rural patients ( p = 0.006). However, the repeated chair stands component showed better performance in micropolitan areas compared to small town/rural areas (adjusted mean difference, 0.46; 95% CI, 0.12, 0.81; p = 0.009). Standing balance and gait speed components showed no differences between groups. 3.4.3 SF ‐36 Health‐Related Quality of Life Multiple SF‐36 domains showed worse outcomes among small town/rural residents as compared to micropolitan residents. Physical functioning scale scores were lower in small town/rural areas compared to micropolitan areas (adjusted mean difference, 7.69; 95% CI, 0.54, 14.84; p = 0.035. Pain scale scores were lower indicating more pain) in small town/rural areas versus micropolitan areas (adjusted mean difference, 7.37; 95% CI, 0.61, 14.13; p = 0.0337). General health perceptions were worse among small town/rural residents compared to micropolitan residents (adjusted mean difference, 6.78; 95% CI, 1.22, 12.34; p = 0.017). Social functioning scores were also worse in small town/rural areas versus micropolitan areas (adjusted mean difference, 7.50; 95% CI, 0.39, 14.61; p = 0.039). Finally, energy/fatigue scale scores indicated more fatigue among small town/rural residents compared to micropolitan residents (adjusted mean difference, 6.77; 95% CI, 0.53, 13.00; p = 0.034). 4 Discussion 4.1 Overview of Findings Taken together, the results of this post hoc exploratory analysis suggest that the patients with advanced cancer in this cohort who live in more remote areas have less favorable physical function, symptoms, and QOL. Prior research indicates that these participants live in a setting where less supportive care is available [ 7 , 8 ]. It could be hypothesized that a technology‐based supportive care intervention could be more useful in the small town/rural settings because there may be few local options to support this population. Interventions are needed to address the needs of these patients living in remote rural areas to ensure symptom, function, and QOL outcomes will be comparable to patients in less rural areas. There are fewer nursing homes and home health care or palliative care options in the RUCA 7–10 areas [ 26 , 27 ]. Because of this, it is crucial for these patients to maintain their physical function for as long as possible. Interventions specifically designed to address physical function in the small town/rural advanced cancer population may benefit from the incorporation of technology to address the challenges of distance and cost of travel, as well as lack of facilities and other resources. This study demonstrates intra‐rural health disparities between patients with cancer residing in more (small town/rural) versus less (micropolitan) rural areas. These findings add to the growing body of literature documenting rural health disparities and extend our understanding of how the extent of rurality affects cancer‐related health outcomes. 4.2 Sleep and Pain Disparities The worse sleep disturbance and pain scores among small town/rural residents represent meaningful differences that may have profound implications for cancer treatment and recovery. Sleep disruption is particularly concerning as it is associated with compromised immune function, delayed wound healing, and increased inflammation, which may negatively impact treatment tolerance [ 28 , 29 ]. The higher pain interference scores suggest that rural residents experience greater functional limitations due to pain, which may contribute to the observed differences in physical functioning and quality of life. These disparities may reflect differential access to specialized pain management services, sleep medicine specialists, physical function interventions, and other supportive care resources that are more readily available in larger metropolitan areas. Patients with cancer living in rural areas often face substantial travel burdens to access comprehensive cancer centers [ 30 ], potentially limiting their ability to receive multidisciplinary supportive care that addresses these symptoms effectively. 4.3 Quality of Life The pattern of worse SF‐36 outcomes across multiple domains—including physical functioning, pain, general health perceptions, social functioning, and energy/fatigue—suggests that rural residence affects broad aspects of health‐related quality of life. The social functioning differences are particularly concerning, as social support is crucial for cancer outcomes, and rural communities may have fewer formal support resources despite potentially strong informal networks. 4.4 Physical Performance Considerations While overall SPPB scores showed no statistically significant differences between groups, small town/rural residents performed worse on the chair stands component of the test. The difference between groups was statistically significant, but did not rise to the level of a meaningful difference. It is perhaps a more salient observation that the mean overall SPPB score for this cohort was 8.4. An SPPB score below 9 is commonly associated with increased risk of disability, falls, and mortality. Regardless of the extent of rurality, this cohort of patients with advanced cancer would benefit from interventions to assist with maintaining functional independence [ 19 ]. 4.5 Potential Mechanisms Several interconnected mechanisms likely contribute to these disparities. Healthcare access represents a primary driver, as rural areas typically have fewer oncology specialists, supportive care services, and physical therapists [ 8 , 11 ]. The travel burden associated with accessing care may lead to delayed symptom management, missed appointments, and reduced engagement with comprehensive supportive services. Socioeconomic factors also play a crucial role. While both groups showed similar income distributions in lower brackets, rural residents may face additional economic burdens related to travel for care, time away from work, and limited local support services. The higher proportion of rural residents with lower educational attainment may also impact health literacy and self‐advocacy skills needed to navigate complex cancer care systems. Environmental and lifestyle factors unique to rural settings may also contribute to these outcomes. Rural occupations often involve greater physical demands, potentially exacerbating cancer‐related fatigue and functional limitations. Additionally, rural communities may have cultural norms around stoicism and self‐reliance that discourage symptom reporting or help‐seeking behaviors [ 31 ]. 4.6 Clinical Implications These findings have implications for cancer care delivery and supportive care interventions. The disparities in sleep, pain, physical function, and QOL suggest that patients living in remote rural areas may benefit from enhanced symptom management protocols and potentially different treatment approaches. Technology‐based interventions targeting these specific symptoms could help bridge geographic gaps in care access. Healthcare systems serving rural populations should consider implementing systematic screening for sleep disturbance, pain interference, and physical function, with protocols for remote or regional management of these issues. The development of community‐based supportive care programs, potentially leveraging local healthcare providers with specialist consultation, could help address some of these disparities. 4.7 Study Strengths and Limitations Among the strengths of this study is the rare look at intra‐rural effects on physical function, symptoms and QOL. Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. The cross‐sectional design precludes causal inferences about the relationship between rurality and health outcomes. The sample was predominantly white, limiting generalizability to more diverse rural populations. The classification of rurality into only two categories may not capture the full spectrum of rural–urban differences, and the specific geographic regions studied may not represent all rural areas. Another limitation of this study is the small sample of remote rural participants. The observational nature of the study means that unmeasured confounders, such as disease duration, treatment history, or individual coping styles, may influence the observed associations. Finally, there were many variables compared across rurality. However, they spanned different domains of health and well‐being. There was no overall conclusion about health that was sought but rather we sought to identify areas of worse health domains, so we did not correct for multiplicity. Instead, we have deemphasized p ‐values, included group differences and 95% confidence intervals. Even so, the results should be interpreted with caution and in the context of clinical meaningfulness [ 32 ]. 4.8 Future Directions Future research should examine longitudinal patterns of these disparities and evaluate interventions specifically designed to address the unique needs of cancer patients living in rural areas. Studies investigating the effectiveness of technology‐based supportive care interventions and community health worker programs would be valuable. Additionally, qualitative research exploring the perspectives of patients with cancer living in rural areas on barriers to optimal symptom management could inform intervention development. Research examining the economic impact of these disparities, both for patients and healthcare systems, could support policy initiatives aimed at improving rural cancer care. Finally, studies should investigate whether certain rural characteristics (such as community cohesion or environmental factors) might serve as protective factors that could help identify assets that could be leveraged in interventions [ 33 , 34 ]. 5 Conclusion This study demonstrates health disparities between patients with cancer living in small town/rural and micropolitan areas across multiple important domains. The consistent pattern of worse outcomes among more rural residents, particularly in sleep, pain, cognitive function, physical function, and quality of life measures, highlights the urgent need for targeted interventions to address these disparities. Healthcare systems, policymakers, and researchers must work collaboratively to develop and implement strategies that ensure equitable cancer care outcomes regardless of geographic residence. The findings underscore that place of residence is not merely a demographic characteristic but a meaningful determinant of health outcomes that requires systematic attention in cancer care delivery.
