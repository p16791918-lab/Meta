INTRODUCTION With an increasingly aging population, prostate cancer (PCa) has consistently been reported to be the most frequent cancer in men in the 2020s in developed countries in Asia [ 1 ], such as South Korea [ 2 ], Japan [ 3 ], and Singapore [ 4 ], as it is in Western countries. PCa does not cause symptoms other than lower urinary tract symptoms until it metastasizes, and prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing plays a key role in its early diagnosis. Although PSA serves as a screening test and histological examination functions as a confirmatory test, the public implementation of PSA testing for middle-aged men became controversial in the 2010s, with both proponents and opponents [ 5 6 7 ]. However, the discontinuation of widespread PSA testing during the 2010s, prompted by several prohibitive guidelines, has been associated with an increase in metastatic PCa in the US since the late 2010s [ 8 9 ] and a rapid increase in mortality in major Western European countries [ 10 ]. Consequently, PSA testing was reintroduced as a baseline measure in the most recent guidelines published in the 2020s. According to official national cancer registration data, PCa was ranked as the 10th most common cancer in men in South Korea, with 1,454 cases registered in 1999. However, in the following year of 2000, 1,872 cases were registered, ranking seventh; in 2002, 2,148 people were registered, ranking sixth; in 2008, 6,666 people were registered, ranking fifth; in 2019, 16,819 people were registered, ranking fourth; in 2021, 19,058 people were registered, ranking third; and in the most recent result, in 2022, 20,754 people were registered, becoming the second most common malignant disease. Despite this rapid increase, the cost-effectiveness of PSA testing as a screening tool for PCa has been examined in only one study, which yielded negative results and was published in 2014 based on national data from 2000 to 2010 [ 11 ]. Although the primary factor contributing to the negative PSA test results at the time was the low incidence of cancer, this contrasts with the epidemiological growth observed in the 2010s and the fact that more than half of the PCa cases in Korea were high-risk [ 12 ]. In this context, we aimed to estimate the impact of repeated PSA testing strategies on PCa care in terms of treatment patterns and medical expenses. We used 10 years of health insurance data commencing in 2010, a period when modern treatment modalities, including robot-assisted radical prostatectomy (RARP), upfront chemotherapy with docetaxel, and androgen receptor-targeted agents, became widely available in South Korea. MATERIALS AND METHODS 1. Data source The data used in this study were procured from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) in South Korea, which encompasses nearly 98% of the nation’s population and offers comprehensive health coverage. The Korean NHIS database contains almost all medical records regarding diagnostic codes, procedures, medications, and outcomes, and also offers details regarding the expenses for each treatment, date of treatment, and admission and discharge dates for hospital stays. All personal identification numbers were secured through encryption before data processing to meet the privacy rules set by the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act. Following the approval of the Institutional Review Board (approval number: YUMC 2022-11-017) of Yeungnam University Medical Center, a custom database was created for all patients newly registered in the NHIS with an International Classification of Diseases-10 code of C61 from 2008 to 2020. 2. Study design and endpoints The current version of the NHIS data in South Korea does not provide specific information on disease severity, such as staging, grading, or PSA level. Therefore, risk stratification, which is essential for determining the treatment method for PCa, as recommended by contemporary guidelines, is not possible. Instead, this study aimed to investigate the impact of the repeated PSA testing strategy on the macroscopic choice of initial treatment modalities between localized and systemic disease, and manifestation of differences in subsequent treatment patterns according to disease progression after initial treatment; localized treatments included operation (radical prostatectomy, including RARP, and open and laparoscopic approaches), radiation therapy (primary and secondary radiation including adjuvant and salvage radiation after surgery), and focal therapy (cryoablation and brachytherapy), and systemic treatment included hormone therapy (gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonists and antagonists), chemotherapy, and androgen receptor-targeted agents. The second purpose of this study was to investigate the median individual medical cost for each treatment modality, which was defined as the sum of all costs incurred by the patient, regardless of whether the patient had health insurance. For localized therapies, the total medical costs were estimated based on reimbursement records corresponding to the timing and type of treatment, including one-time procedures such as surgery or focal therapy. For systemic therapies, the costs were calculated according to the cumulative reimbursement associated with repeated treatments over time. The cost of each treatment could only be determined if it was available in the NHIS database. In the case of radiation therapy, the cost was defined as the sum of the prices given for all individual treatment periods during the period examined for radiation therapy. For surgery, the costs incurred during hospitalization required for the surgery were settled. In South Korea, the government determines the price of medical procedures, and there was a sharp increase in the price of open radical prostatectomy in 2017 and 2018 compared to that in 2009. For RARP, the cost of hospitalization, including anesthesia, can be tracked, but not the individual price of the surgery. Similar to surgery, government-determined prices for radiation therapy increased gradually in 2011, 2014, 2017, and 2018. For brachytherapy, it was determined whether the procedure was performed using the codes assigned in the NHIS. However, we were unable to obtain clear information on the number of permanent implants used, making it impossible to determine the total cost. In this study, hospitalization costs were investigated for alternatives to brachytherapy. 3. Operational definition of each group of interest For therapeutic interventions excluded from the national healthcare framework or those that lack an unequivocal demographic coding system, it is imperative to employ operational definitions to delineate target populations based on PSA testing status. Regarding PSA testing preceding registration for the NHIS as a patient with PCa, the cohort who underwent repeated PSA testing was operationally defined as those who had undergone a minimum of three PSA tests over a period of at least two years before their registration. Based on this definition, patients from 2008 to 2010 were investigated in advance to distinguish whether they had undergone PSA testing, and subsequently, the treatment patterns and medical costs from 2010 to 2020 were analyzed. Conversely, the PSA non-tested population was classified based on the occurrence of the first PSA test conducted within 3 months leading up to registration, whereas all other individuals not fitting into these categories were designated as PSA-undetermined. PSA-undetermined was defined as individuals who did not meet the prespecified criteria for either repeated PSA testing (≥3 tests over ≥2 years before registration) or PSA non-testing (first PSA test within 3 months prior to registration). These individuals were retained as a separate category for descriptive analyses, but were not included in the head-to-head comparative analyses. Open and laparoscopic radical prostatectomies were identified using the reimbursement codes R3950 and R3960, respectively. RARP, which the NHIS did not reimburse, was operationally defined as the absence of a surgery code despite the presence of general anesthesia code (‘L1211’) and postoperative pathologic examination codes (‘C5500,’ C5504’,‘C5505’, C5508,’ ‘C5918,’ or ‘C5919’), among the patients with a previous code C61, as well as V193/194 [ 9 ]. Radiotherapy includes all related modalities, including conformal and intensity-modulated radiation therapies. Docetaxel and cabazitaxel were the approved agents during the study period and were selectively investigated. Regarding androgen receptor-targeted agents, enzalutamide and apalutamide were the only agents available on the market before 2020. 4. Statistical analysis For analyses focusing on comparative medical utilization and cost outcomes, statistical comparisons were restricted to repeated PSA-tested and PSA non-tested groups, representing clearly defined screening-exposure categories. A Sankey diagram was constructed to follow the treatment trends in the PSA-tested and PSA non-tested groups. Student’s t-test was used for continuous data, whereas the chi-square test was used for binary and categorical data to compare the features and outcomes of each group. The Cochran–Armitage Trend test was employed to examine trends between the PSA screening and non-screening groups and to evaluate the relationship between the variables in the two categories. p-values 0.01 were considered to be significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute). RESULTS 1. Macroscopic choice of initial and subsequent treatment modalities between localized and systemic disease Of the 166,848 men diagnosed with PCa nationwide during the study period, 26.7% met the criteria for the PSA-tested, 42.2% were classified as PSA non-tested, and the remaining 31.1% were classified as PSA-undetermined. The frequencies of each treatment modality are summarized in Table 1 . Localized treatments were more frequent in the PSA-tested population than in the PSA non-tested population, including surgery (45.6% vs. 33.8%, p 0.001, including all surgical modalities), radiotherapy (17.0% vs. 14.9%, p 0.001, including primary, adjuvant, and salvage radiation), and focal therapy (0.8% vs. 0.3%, p 0.001, including brachytherapy and cryoablation). In comparison, systemic treatments, including hormone therapy (42.3% vs. 59.7%, p 0.001), chemotherapy (1.0% vs. 2.7%, p 0.001), and androgen receptortargeted agents (0.5% vs. 1.4%, p 0.001) were more common in the non-tested group. Surgery and radiotherapy, which account for the majority of localized treatments, were more common in the PSA non-tested population in the first five years between 2010 and 2015, but more common in the PSA-tested population in the later five years between 2016 and 2020. Hormone therapy, which accounts for the majority of systemic treatments, was used for ≥3 years more frequently in the PSA non-tested group (36.4%) than in the PSA-tested group (21.5%). After the first treatment, the Sankey diagram for the two groups provides a summary of the entire PCa treatment course during the study period in South Korea ( Fig. 1 ). Hormone therapy as a secondary treatment after surgery was less common in the PSA-tested group, and hormone therapy initiated after the initial diagnosis and subsequent surgery was also less common in the PSA-tested group than in the PSA non-tested group. 2. The comparison of median individual medical expenses for each treatment modality between groups The analysis for individual medical expenses include patients who received at least one recorded treatment modality (PSA non-tested: 56,891; PSA-tested: 35,337), excluding those with no documented treatment. The medical costs did not differ between the PSA-tested and non-tested groups for non-robotic surgery and primary and secondary radiation therapy performed between 2016 and 2020, when government-determined medical prices increased to a level similar to that of the present, and the same was valid for focal therapy. However, medical spending for hormone therapy and androgen receptor-targeted agents was significantly higher in the PSA non-tested group, and the difference was particularly significant in enzalutamide users. This increase in expenses was related to the extension of the treatment period; in the case of hormone treatment, there were significantly more cases of use for ≥3 years in the PSA non-tested group, which was equally replicated in terms of cost. PSA status did not significantly affect the cost of chemotherapy, regardless of the type or period of each agent ( Table 2 , Fig. 2 ). DISCUSSION With the accelerating aging trend in society, the incidence of malignant diseases in South Korea has changed rapidly. As PCa mainly affects the elderly [ 13 14 ] and exhibits age-dependent increases in incidence rates [ 15 16 ], it is expected to become an increasingly important cancer in countries with increased life expectancy. Although PCa was ranked among the top ten cancers in men until the end of the 20th century, it is expected to become the most common cancer by the mid-2020s. In the last two decades, the crude incidence of PCa in Korea has increased 10-fold (2.9 in 2000 to 32.7 in 2020), and mortality has increased 4-fold (1.2 in 2000 to 4.3 in 2020). As the average age at which PCa is registered in Korea has remained almost unchanged at 71 years over the past 20 years, according to data from Statistics Korea, the increase in PCa-led cancer is likely to continue, given the current median age of 80.6 years in 2023. Nevertheless, awareness of PCa among the general public, particularly of the PSA test for screening purposes, was 10% in a general population survey conducted in 2020 [ 17 ]. Because the national cancer screening program does not include PSA testing, testing is performed opportunistically rather than in an organized manner. In the 2016 census, the testing rates were 4.9% for those in their 50s, 12.0% for those in their 60s, 19.2% for those in their 70s, and 21.6% for those aged ≥80 [ 8 ]. In PCa, which does not cause any unusual symptoms until the size of the metastatic cancer increases, low PSA testing rates are likely to negatively impact its management. However, the justification for screening for specific diseases should be measured by an increase in the survival rate of the same population through the collective performance of screening tests. In particular, PCa, which is common in the elderly and progresses relatively slowly, is difficult to verify the increase in survival through screening tests, even in a randomized clinical trial. In 2020, the final year of the study period, 44.7% of the registered 120,423 men with PCa were alive for ≥5 years after the diagnosis of PCa, suggesting that the impact of repeated PSA testing on cancer-specific mortality was difficult to estimate. Moreover, since the NHIS does not provide the most important indicators of the biological characteristics of PCa, including the Gleason grade or serum PSA level at the time of diagnosis, this study sought to investigate its cost-effectiveness by examining treatment patterns and median individual medical expenses as an alternative. Despite the relative advantages of organized PSA testing over opportunistic PSA testing, given the low PSA test rate and opportunistic nature of test exposure, it has not yet been implemented in South Korea. To replicate a hypothetical situation, this study used an operational definition to distinguish 166,848 registered patients into two groups: those who underwent repeated PSA tests and those who did not. Since the PSA non-tested group was defined as the group in which the first PSA test was performed within 3 months prior to PCa registration, this is a much simpler and more effective classification method compared to opponents where opportunities for PSA testing are difficult to clearly present; therefore, there is a high possibility that reality was reflected. On the other hand, although an increase in survival rate has been reported using the same method [ 18 ], the PSA-tested group, which is defined as testing three or more times over a minimum of two years, can be considered heterogeneous in terms of clinical situations for screening purposes, ranging from three times in 10 years to three times in two years, given the period of this study, which covers 2008 to 2020. Despite these limitations, it is important to note that the simply defined PSA non-tested group had the highest proportion of all systemic treatment modalities, including hormone therapy, chemotherapy, and androgen receptor-targeted agents, compared with those exposed to repeated PSA testing. In contrast, the repeated PSA-tested group had the highest proportion of all localized treatment modalities, including surgery, radiotherapy, and focal therapy. Previous Western trials, including the ERSPC and PLCO, primarily assessed the cost-effectiveness of PSA screening in relation to PCa-specific mortality using predefined screening protocols. These studies demonstrated that the economic value of PSA screening is closely linked to downstream changes in disease stage distribution and treatment intensity. In contrast, our study focused on real-world PSA testing exposure and subsequent medical utilization within a nationwide claims-based cohort, reflecting routine clinical practice rather than trial-driven screening strategies. Despite these methodological differences, our findings are consistent with Western evidence, suggesting that earlier detection through PSA testing is associated with less intensive downstream management and lower cumulative medical costs than later detection. Owing to the linkages and complexity of PCa care over the 10-year period depicted in the Sankey diagram in Fig. 2 , this study could not capture all medical expenses associated with PCa in each group. As information about PSA testing becomes more widely available in the 2020s, more patients are likely to undergo recent PSA tests and be categorized into the PSA-tested group. This would naturally increase the cost of treatment in the PSA non-tested group, which will have a longer follow-up and, therefore, appear to have higher costs. In assessing the cost of PCa treatment, it is essential to consider that the governmentally determined prices for treatment modalities for localized disease, particularly those that are predominantly one-time procedures, with the exception of some radiation treatments or focal therapy, became prevalent in the 2010s. For example, the price assigned to open prostatectomy, which has been highly volatile, nearly doubled between 2009 and 2018. Therefore, we split the analysis into the early and late 2010s to reflect this realization and found no difference in the price of localized disease staging between repeated PSA-tested and PSA-untested patients in the last five years. Although the reimbursement levels changed over the study period, the absence of an inflation adjustment is unlikely to affect our findings, as cost comparisons were based on the relative differences between groups treated under the same pricing conditions within each period. In contrast, for systemic therapy, where there has been relatively little price variation over the past decade, it became necessary to include drugs used repeatedly in PCa treatment to accurately track healthcare expenses. The high prevalence of hormonal therapy used for ≥3 years in the PSA non-tested group suggests that they had more advanced disease at the time of diagnosis, which in turn suggests that exposure to PSA testing could reduce staging and medical costs. As PSA testing is recommended to be performed at regular intervals in men aged 40 to 50 years in the 2020s as the most basic test for PCa through revisions to various medical guidelines [ 19 20 21 22 ], additional research related to the social costs of PSA testing is necessary. CONCLUSIONS In this nationwide claims-based analysis, differences in medical utilization and cumulative costs were observed according to PSA testing exposure, reflecting distinct diagnostic and treatment pathways in real-world clinical conditions. Our findings suggest that structured, repeated PSA testing may improve the efficiency of national PCa care systems by promoting early-stage detection and reducing long-term systemic therapy costs.
