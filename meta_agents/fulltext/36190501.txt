Introduction Breast cancer remains the most common malignancy among women worldwide and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality[ 1 ]. While surgical resection, with or without adjuvant radiation, remains the standard definitive approach for early-stage disease, minimally invasive techniques are increasingly being explored as alternatives, particularly for patients who are poor surgical candidates or require local palliation. One such technique is cryoablation, a percutaneous procedure that induces tumor necrosis via cold-induced cellular injury. HIGHLIGHTS First reported clinical series evaluating radiotherapy after breast cryoablation. Nine inoperable breast cancer patients safely completed radiotherapy post-ablation. No grade ≥2 skin necrosis, pain, fibrosis, or treatment-related interruptions were observed. Cryoablation plus radiotherapy was feasible and well-tolerated across all cases. Findings provide preliminary evidence supporting this combined approach for patients lacking surgical options. Cryoablation offers several potential advantages, including preservation of normal tissue architecture, favorable cosmetic outcomes, and the ability to perform the procedure under local anesthesia in an outpatient setting. Preclinical studies suggest that cryoablation can stimulate systemic antitumor immunity by releasing tumor-associated antigens in situ, raising the possibility of synergy with immunotherapeutic strategies [ 2 – 4 ] . Early prospective trials have demonstrated promising results in highly selected patient populations. The American College of Surgeons Oncology Group (ACOSOG) Z1072 study[ 5 ] and the ICE3 trial [ 6 , 7 ] reported favorable local control and cosmetic outcomes with cryoablation alone in small (≤2 cm in Z1072 and ≤1.5 cm in ICE3), low-risk invasive ductal carcinomas, with durable efficacy at early follow-up. These findings established cryoablation as a potentially promising minimally invasive treatment option for selected early-stage breast cancers in carefully chosen patients. For patients with medically inoperable early-stage breast cancer, current National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines recommend definitive radiotherapy as an alternative to surgery when resection is not feasible[ 8 ]. However, the role of cryoablation in this population, whether alone or in combination with radiotherapy, is not addressed in current guidelines. However, significant knowledge gaps remain. Notably, patients in these major trials did not receive radiotherapy following cryoablation, and no data exist to guide adjuvant radiotherapy with regard to dose, fractionation, technique, safety, or outcomes in this setting. Furthermore, long-term outcome data following cryoablation remain limited, and the applicability of cryoablation in patients with advanced or inoperable disease, where complete tumor eradication may be less feasible, remains unexplored. Here, we present a case series of patients with inoperable breast cancer who underwent cryoablation followed by radiotherapy. To our knowledge, this represents the first reported experience combining these modalities and provides preliminary data on the feasibility, safety, and tolerability of this treatment approach in a population lacking curative surgical options. A targeted literature search identified no prior publications reporting outcomes of radiotherapy administered following cryoablation in any breast cancer population. This case series has been reported in line with TITAN and PROCESS guidelines [ 6 , 9 ] . Methods All consecutive patients at our institution who underwent cryoablation followed by radiotherapy from 2018 to 2023 were included in this analysis. Clinicopathologic and treatment characteristics were extracted from the electronic medical record, and this retrospective analysis was approved by the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center Institutional Review Board. This work is reported in line with TITAN and PROCESS criteria [ 6 , 9 ] . Background literature was identified through targeted searches of PubMed and Google Scholar using the terms “cryoablation,” “breast cancer,” “radiotherapy,” and “thermal ablation.” This was not a systematic review; rather, relevant studies were identified to contextualize the present findings. Treatment-related toxicities were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0. Acute toxicities were defined as those occurring during or within 90 days of radiotherapy completion; late toxicities were defined as those occurring beyond 90 days. Cryoablation procedure Prior to cryoablation, all patients underwent mammography and ultrasound imaging. Cryoablation procedures were performed under local anesthesia with consistent guidance from ultrasound. The largest diameter of each lesion was measured before cryoprobe insertion. Cryoprobes were placed percutaneously under real-time ultrasound, and multiple probes were used for lesions 1.4 cm. For all cases, an argon-based cryoablation system (Visual Ice Cryoablation System, Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA) was used. To protect the skin, hydro-dissection with room-temperature normal saline was applied. For tumors abutting or involving the skin, continuous hydro-dissection using smaller syringes (3–10 mL) was used to maximize separation between the tumor and skin. At least two freeze–thaw cycles were performed: typically 10 min of freezing followed by 5 min of thawing, with the goal of achieving a 1-cm margin. In instances where the ice ball approached the skin or skin injury was observed, treatment duration was shortened as guided by real-time ultrasound monitoring. Hemostasis was ensured at probe withdrawal, and patients were monitored post-procedure before discharge. Radiation therapy Following cryoablation, patients underwent a CT-simulation for radiation planning. A computed tomography scan was obtained with a 3-mm slice thickness, covering the area from the mandible to below the diaphragm to ensure adequate coverage of the supraclavicular and internal mammary nodes when indicated. Treatment planning was performed on a dedicated three-dimensional (3D) treatment planning system. The ablation zone and identifiable tumor volume were contoured as the gross tumor volume, and an isotropic expansion of 5 mm was used as the clinical target volume expansion. Whole-breast radiotherapy was typically administered. Thereafter, planning was conducted for either 3D-conformal radiotherapy or intensity-modulated radiotherapy based on patient anatomy and dose selection as reported. Patients underwent partial breast irradiation, whole-breast radiotherapy, or regional nodal irradiation to include the full axilla, internal mammary nodal basin, and supraclavicular fossa at the discretion of the treating physician. This case series has been reported in line with the PROCESS guidelines[ 6 ]. Results Patient cohort We identified nine consecutive patients who underwent radiotherapy following cryoablation, seven of whom had inoperable early-stage disease and two who underwent palliative cryoablation in the setting of advanced (stage IV) disease (Tables 1 and 2 ). The median age at diagnosis was 67 years (range: 51–90 years). The median tumor size was 2.3 cm (range: 0.6–4.8 cm). Five patients had estrogen receptor (ER)+/progesterone receptor (PR)+/human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)− disease, three had triple-negative breast cancer, and one had ER−/PR−/HER2+ disease. Five patients had node-positive disease. Eight tumors were invasive ductal carcinoma, and one was invasive lobular carcinoma. The median time from cryoablation to radiotherapy was 2.8 months (range: 1.1–6.8 months), and the median dose was 37.5 Gy (range: 26–50 Gy). Six patients received whole-breast radiotherapy, and two received regional nodal irradiation. A sequential boost to the ablation cavity (median: 10 Gy; range: 5.2–14 Gy) was administered in four patients. Dose–volume histogram analysis confirmed that all plans met institutional and Radiation Therapy Oncology Group constraints for organs at risk, including the heart (mean dose 4 Gy for left-sided lesions) and the ipsilateral lung (V20 30%). Tolerability and efficacy of radiotherapy following cryoablation Cryoablation was performed in all cases without any intraprocedural complications. No major complications (e.g., skin necrosis, hemorrhage, infection) were reported. Of the nine patients who underwent radiotherapy following cryoablation, no acute toxicities of grade 2 or higher were observed during or within 90 days of treatment completion. One patient developed grade 2 lymphedema as a late toxicity, requiring physical therapy; no other late toxicities of grade 2 or higher were observed. At a median follow-up of 30.6 months, no patients reported breast pain, adverse cosmesis, or required conservative or operative intervention to manage the sequelae of radiotherapy following cryoablation. Importantly, no cases of skin necrosis, pain, or fibrosis were reported. Six patients maintained local control by the end of the follow-up period, while three exhibited local progression. Of these, two had triple-negative breast cancer, and one had ER−/HER2+ breast cancer; all patients with ER+ breast cancer maintained local control. Tumor sizes of the three progressive/recurrent lesions were 0.6, 1.3, and 3.5 cm, and all underwent whole-breast radiotherapy with a boost. Two patients had distant disease at the last follow-up, both of whom had presented with stage IV disease or node-positive locally advanced cancer. Discussion In this case series, we evaluated the feasibility and safety of combining external beam radiation therapy with cryoablation in patients with inoperable breast cancer. To our knowledge, this represents the first reported clinical experience of radiotherapy administered following cryoablation. Despite limited existing literature on this treatment combination, our findings demonstrate that radiotherapy can be safely delivered after cryoablation without significant toxicity, providing important preliminary evidence for this approach. Cryoablation has been extensively studied in small, early-stage breast cancer, with trials such as ACOSOG Z1072[ 5 ] and ICE3[ 6 ] demonstrating favorable local control and cosmetic outcomes in highly selected operable patients. Importantly, these trials enrolled patients with small tumors (up to 2 cm in Z1072, up to 1.5 cm in ICE3), low-risk, operable tumors and specifically excluded radiotherapy from their treatment protocols. Our cohort, by contrast, consisted of patients with inoperable or metastatic disease, larger tumors (median: 2.3 cm, range: 0.6–4.8 cm), and a higher proportion of aggressive subtypes, including triple-negative breast cancer. Consequently, substantial uncertainty remains in the clinical community regarding whether and how radiotherapy should be administered following cryoablation. The existing cryoablation literature thus provides no guidance for the delivery of radiotherapy in this clinical context. Definitive radiotherapy alone has an established role in medically inoperable early-stage breast cancer. NCCN guidelines recommend radiotherapy as an alternative to surgery when resection is not feasible, and retrospective series have reported reasonable local control rates with this approach[ 8 ]. The addition of cryoablation to radiotherapy is a conceptually appealing strategy that may improve local tumor control while maintaining the minimally invasive nature of the approach. However, whether the combination offers benefits beyond radiotherapy alone remains an open question that warrants investigation in comparative studies. Several biologic mechanisms may underlie the potential synergy between cryoablation and radiotherapy. Cryoablation induces immunogenic cell death, releasing tumor-associated antigens and damage-associated molecular patterns that stimulate antitumor immune responses [2–4] . Radiotherapy similarly activates immunogenic pathways and may complement cryoablation-induced immunity through the abscopal effect. Additionally, cryoablation and radiotherapy cause tissue injury through distinct and potentially complementary mechanisms: cryoablation through direct cold-induced cytotoxicity and vascular disruption, and radiotherapy through DNA damage and the generation of reactive oxygen species. The combination may therefore achieve more comprehensive tumor cell kill than either modality alone, though this hypothesis requires formal preclinical and clinical evaluation. Our findings address this critical knowledge gap by demonstrating that cryoablation followed by radiotherapy was technically achievable and well-tolerated, with no major procedural complications. Radiotherapy is an essential component of local control in breast cancer and is typically delivered after surgery or, in select cases, as definitive therapy for patients unable to undergo resection[ 8 ]. The integration of cryoablation into the treatment paradigm raises theoretical concerns about potentially overlapping toxicities from combined thermal and radiation injury, including risks of tissue necrosis, impaired wound healing, and enhanced fibrosis. However, no acute or late toxicities of grade ≥2 were observed in our cohort, aside from one case of grade 2 lymphedema. All patients completed the prescribed radiotherapy without treatment breaks or dose modifications, consistent with the favorable safety profile established in earlier cryoablation studies. These findings provide reassurance that the combination of cryoablation and radiation can be delivered safely without excessive toxicity, though validation in larger cohorts with extended follow-up remains essential. The local control observed in our study must be interpreted cautiously, given the small sample size, short follow-up period, and advanced disease stage at presentation, in addition to the palliative intent for some lesions that were larger than the target cryoablation zone. Although most patients maintained control in the ablation field at the last follow-up, recurrences occurred in several patients, reflecting both the advanced nature of their disease and the inherent limitations of localized therapy in this population[ 10 ]. These patients required cryoablation specifically because they were not surgical candidates due to medical comorbidities, locally advanced disease, or metastatic presentations. As such, the primary objective of this report is to establish safety and feasibility rather than to demonstrate oncologic efficacy. Whether outcomes are superior to radiotherapy alone in this setting remains unknown. Comparative studies with appropriate control groups are needed to determine the additive benefit, if any, of cryoablation combined with radiotherapy. Our study has several important limitations. The retrospective design, small sample size of nine patients, and heterogeneity of patient characteristics limit generalizability. The absence of a control group precludes definitive conclusions about comparative efficacy or optimal patient selection. Additionally, the relatively short follow-up period limits the assessment of late toxicities and long-term oncologic outcomes. Despite these constraints, this case series addresses a significant gap in the literature by providing the first clinical evidence that radiotherapy following cryoablation is feasible and safe. Future prospective studies with standardized treatment protocols, larger cohorts, extended follow-up, and ideally comparative arms are necessary to validate these findings and establish evidence-based guidelines for the integration of radiotherapy with cryoablation in breast cancer management. This case series demonstrates that radiotherapy can be safely delivered following cryoablation in patients with inoperable breast cancer. All nine patients completed the planned radiotherapy course without treatment interruptions, dose reductions, or major procedural complications. No patients developed grade ≥2 acute toxicities, and at a median follow-up of 32 months, no cases of skin necrosis, significant pain, or clinically meaningful fibrosis were observed. While disease progression occurred in some patients due to advanced baseline disease burden, treatment-related toxicity remained minimal throughout. These findings provide the first clinical evidence that cryoablation followed by radiotherapy is feasible and well-tolerated, establishing a foundation for further investigation. Although hypothesis-generating and limited by a small sample size, this report offers preliminary support for this combined approach as a treatment option for patients lacking curative surgical pathways. Prospective studies with larger cohorts and longer follow-up are needed to validate these safety findings and evaluate oncologic outcomes. In conclusion, this case series demonstrates that radiotherapy can be safely delivered following cryoablation in patients with inoperable breast cancer. All nine patients completed the planned radiotherapy course without treatment interruptions, dose reductions, or major complications, and treatment-related toxicity was minimal at a median follow-up of 30.6 months. These findings establish a foundation for further investigation of this combined approach in patients lacking curative surgical pathways.
