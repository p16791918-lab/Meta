Introduction Absolute breast cancer risk prediction models estimate a woman’s risk of developing breast cancer over a given period (e.g. 5 years) in the presence of competing risks and are useful for recommending screening strategies and risk-reduction approaches [ 1 ]. However, many primary care providers do not use risk prediction calculators due to time constraints during primary care visits; thus, adding a new calculator for a specific population requires evidence of its relative benefit. To this end, we compared performance metrics of the BWHS calculator [ 2 , 3 ], developed specifically for breast cancer risk assessment in Black women, with metrics from the two calculators most often used in U.S. clinical practice, the National Cancer Institute’s Breast Cancer Risk Assessment Tool, BCRAT or “Gail model” [ 4 – 6 ] and the IBIS or “Tyrer-Cuzick” model [ 7 , 8 ]. Methods Validation study population The Black Women’s Health Study is a prospective cohort study of 59,000 self-identified Black women from all regions of the U.S. [ 9 ]. Participants, aged 21–69 in 1995, completed a lengthy baseline questionnaire at enrollment and have updated their data on exposures and outcomes in biennial questionnaires since then. Follow-up has been successful for 85% of potential person-years through 14 follow-up cycles. Incident breast cancers are identified by self-report and by linkage to state cancer registries and the National Death Index. The study protocol was approved by the Boston University Institutional Review Board. BWHS model The BWHS model [ 2 ] was developed using data from three large case-control studies and age-and race-specific breast cancer incidence and competing mortality rates from the NCI’s Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program. It was validated in prospective data from the Black Women’s Health Study. Model predictors are first-degree family history of breast and prostate cancer, breast biopsy, age at menarche, ever parous, breastfeeding, body mass index (BMI) (current and at age 18), menopausal status, and bilateral oophorectomy. Age interactions with family history of breast cancer, breast biopsy, and age at menarche, as well as an interaction between menopausal status and current BMI, are included in the model. BCRAT model In 2007, Gail et al. modified their original BCRAT model using data from Black women in the Women’s CARE study and population-based age- and race-specific incidence rates from SEER [ 5 ]. Covariates in the BCRAT “CARE” model include number of breast biopsies, atypical hyperplasia, age at menarche, and number of first-degree relatives with breast cancer. An interaction between age and the number of breast biopsies is also included. The online version of the original BCRAT model includes an option for race; if Black is selected, the calculator uses the BCRAT “CARE” model. IBIS model The IBIS model (TC 8) [ 7 ] includes age at menarche, number of live births, age at first birth, menopausal status, age at menopause, height, weight, history of hyperplasia (usual and/or atypical), history of and age at diagnosis of ovarian cancer, Ashkenazi Jewish heritage, hormone replacement therapy use, BRCA1 2 test results, extensive family history of breast and ovarian cancer, breast density, and a polygenic single nucleotide polymorphism score (PRS). The model incorporates population-based incidence and mortality rates from the general UK female population. We used a version of the model without mammographic density or PRS because these are not available in many clinical situations. Statistical methods To assess model performance for 5 year breast cancer risk estimates, we constructed four 5 year follow-up intervals: 2001–2005, 2006–2010, 2011–2015, and 2016–2020. For each interval, validation cohort participants aged 30 through 70 were included if they had not been diagnosed with invasive breast cancer at the start of that interval. Age- and time-dependent predictors were updated at the start of each interval. For each model, we computed separate 5 year predictions for each interval to which a woman contributed. To handle variables that are in specific models but not available in validation data, we used instructions provided for each model. For the BCRAT model, number of biopsies was set to one if the participant reported biopsy and hyperplasia was set to none for all participants. For the IBIS model, the following variables were set to none: history of hyperplasia or atypical hyperplasia, Ashkenazi Jewish heritage, genetic testing, family history of bilateral breast cancer. The IBIS model included exact age of relative’s breast cancer diagnosis, whereas the validation cohort provided data only on whether the diagnosis was before or after age 50. For relatives diagnosed before age 50, we assigned an age from 20 to 49 using a shifted beta distribution with α = 5 and β = 1. For relatives diagnosed at age 50 or older, we assigned age at diagnosis using a uniform distribution. To evaluate calibration, we estimated the ratio of expected to observed numbers of invasive breast cancer cases (E/O), where E was calculated by summing the predicted 5 year risks over the four time periods. We estimated E/O ratios overall and within strata of age, breast cancer family history, and history of biopsy. We assessed discriminatory accuracy by computing the area under the curve (AUC) within 5 year age groups and used inverse-variance weighting to calculate the overall age-adjusted AUC. We used a bootstrap procedure wherein we resampled women with replacement and used the quantiles of the bootstrap distribution to obtain 95% confidence intervals for AUCs and two-sided bootstrap p -values for the differences between AUCs for different models. We created cross-classification tables between each pair of models to compare assignments of cases and non-cases as “high risk” (≥ 1.67% risk over 5 years). Results Over 20 years of follow-up, 2041 incident invasive breast cancers developed among 50,235 Black participants. The expected number of breast cancers relative to observed (E/O) was 0.99 (95% confidence interval 0.95–1.04) from the BWHS model, 0.97 (0.93–1.02) from BCRAT, and 1.13 (1.08–1.18) from IBIS (Table 1 ). Among non-cases, 17.8% (BWHS), 16.2% (BCRAT), and 23.3 (IBIS) were classified as high risk. Concordance among the models for individual women was poor: 15.5–25.6% of cases that were classified as high-risk in one model were not classified that way in another. Discussion This performance comparison of three absolute breast cancer risk prediction models in data from 50,235 Black women followed for 20 years indicated that both calibration and discriminatory accuracy metrics were better for the BWHS model than either the BCRAT or IBIS models. The differences in discriminatory accuracy were modest but statistically significant, with AUCs of 0.58 from BWHS versus 0.56 in each of BCRAT and IBIS. Strengths of this research include the updating of exposures every 5 years and the large validation cohort sample size. The primary limitation is that several predictors used in the IBIS model and two used in the BCRAT model were not available in the validation study. This is a limitation in most validations of risk calculators because no validation cohort has data on predictors from all models [ 10 – 12 ]. Those variables are probably only minor contributors to absolute breast cancer risk due to their low prevalence (e.g. family history of ovarian cancer, BRCA1/2 gene mutations, Ashkenazi Jewish heritage). We set these predictors to their lowest risk level, which could explain the slight underestimation for BCRAT, but cannot explain the overestimation for IBIS. Of note, more detailed data on family history of breast cancer would likely have led to greater overestimation of risk in the IBIS model. Paige et al. recently tested the BCRAT, IBIS, and a third model (Breast Cancer Surveillance Consortium) [ 13 , 14 ] in data from women age 40–74 presenting for screening mammography in a multisite California health system [ 15 ]. Overall, the proportions of women with estimated 5 year risk ≥ 1.67% were similar across the models, but risk estimates for individual women varied, with 21–26% discordance in pairwise comparisons of the models. Similarly, in the present study, classification according to the 1.67% risk cut point was discordant for 15.5–25.6% of breast cancer cases in pairwise model comparisons. Breast cancer risk prediction models using only questionnaire-based predictors have been shown to have limited discriminatory accuracy, with age-adjusted AUCs ranging from 0.54 to 0.61 [ 10 – 12 , 15 , 16 ]. Adding a PRS to such models markedly improves discrimination [ 3 , 16 – 18 ]. We previously demonstrated that adding a PRS derived and externally validated in data from Black women improved discriminatory accuracy of the BWHS model [ 3 ]. PRS are not currently available for clinical use at the primary care level but could be incorporated when preventive medications are being considered for women already identified as high risk. Prediction models incorporating mammographic features have shown great promise, with additional work underway [ 19 – 22 ]. Such models may become more important, given that U.S. women are now recommended to begin mammographic screening as early as age 40 [ 23 ]. However, breast cancer risk calculators using only clinical information are still very useful for women under age 40 to identify those who would most benefit from initiating screening early and among previously unscreened older women and those for whom mammography data are not available. Based on the present findings, we suggest that the BWHS calculator be used for Black patients. Supplementary Information Below is the link to the electronic supplementary material.
