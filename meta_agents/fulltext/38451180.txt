Introduction The National Cancer Institute (NCI) requires that NCI-Designated Cancer Centers (NCI-DCCs) define and describe their catchment area in terms of cancer epidemiology, burden, and risk factors. 1 The NCI subsequently expanded this mandate to include Community Outreach and Engagement (COE) activities designed to equitably reduce the burden of cancer within NCI-DCC catchment areas. 2 Although these requirements only apply to NCI-DCCs, extending catchment area approaches to community cancer centers can magnify efforts to reduce the burden of cancer nationwide. Community cancer centers provide an estimated 85% of cancer care in the United States, serve those who lack access to NCI-DCCs, and have the capacity to improve catchment area cancer outcomes. 3-5 As a first step toward implementing this approach for a community cancer center, we previously characterized the spatial distribution of advanced breast cancer (BC) cases for the Helen F. Graham Cancer Center Research Institute (HFGCCRI) catchment area in Delaware. 6 , 7 We focused on Delaware because it does not have an NCI-DCC and much of the state falls outside NCI-DCC catchment areas. 8 In addition, Delaware leads the United States in the incidence of late-stage BC among women under age 50. 9 This is likely, at least in part, because Delaware leads the United States in the incidence of triple-negative BC (TNBC). 10 , 11 Triple-negative BC is the most aggressive molecular subtype of BC, often presenting at an advanced stage and earlier age, and disproportionately affects Black women. 12-14 Our prior catchment area analysis identified 2 BC hotspots, geographic areas with significantly elevated rates of BC, one for advanced BC and another for advanced BC diagnosed before age 50. 6 In both hotspots, Black women were even more overrepresented than was observed for the full catchment area. These findings suggest that prioritizing hotspots for tailored COE interventions could make an outsized impact on the burden of BC and cancer health disparities. As a next step toward developing tailored COE interventions for these hotspots, we focused on BC screening given the 25% reduction in the incidence of advanced BC for women who participate in screening regularly. 15 , 16 Where inconsistent screening is found to account for higher rates of advanced BC, more intensive COE interventions can be deployed to these hotspots to improve screening uptake. However, it has been argued that the benefits of BC screening have been overestimated because mammography-detected tumors are more likely to be slow-growing and identified at an early-stage. 17-19 Aggressive BC tumors, by contrast, develop more rapidly and are more likely to present clinically/symptomatically during the interval between screenings, even among women who screen annually. 20 Thus, method of detection is a function of screening interval and tumor biology. We therefore adjusted for tumor characteristics related to aggressiveness and sensitivity on screening, including grade, molecular subtype, and histology. 21-25 Better understanding these characteristics may also lead to the identification of novel COE targets given the emerging link between adverse neighborhood exposures and BC tumor biology. 26-28 The objective of this study was to identify factors that help explain the advanced BC hotspots. Methods Study design and population This single-site retrospective study was conducted with data from a cohort of BC patients who were treated at the HFGCCRI and were residents in the catchment area at the time of diagnosis. The HFGCCRI catchment area was defined as New Castle County, the most populated of Delaware’s three counties. The HFGCCRI is a part of the ChristianaCare Health System and provides BC treatment to approximately 85% of all BC cases in the county, which were found to be representative of the full population of BC cases in the county along demographic and clinical characteristics. 7 This study was approved by the ChristianaCare Institutional Review Board (CCC#43040). Patient records for 3492 women diagnosed with invasive BC between 2012 and 2020 came from the HFGCCRI cancer registry. The HFGCCRI cancer registry identifies cases via an ongoing review of records from pathology and treatment areas. At the end of each reporting year, a disease index review of International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes is conducted to identify potentially missed cancer cases. Breast cancer cases are identified with a code of C50 (malignant neoplasm of breast). The 2012-2020 period was selected to maximize the study population. Before 2012, not all BC characteristics necessary for staging purposes were consistently recorded in the registry. Given the disruptions caused by the coronavirus disease (2019) beginning in 2020, 29 we examined the distribution of BC cases by age and stage in 2020 to prior years and did not observe a significant difference, in contrast to subsequent years. Measures Demographic measures included age and self-reported race/ethnicity, which were abstracted from the ChristianaCare electronic health record (EHR). Breast cancer cases were classified by age as 50 or ≥50; race as Black, White, and other/unknown; and ethnicity as Hispanic or non-Hispanic. As previously described, Hispanic/Latina patients are often misclassified as non-Hispanic in United States health-care databases, including the ChristianaCare EHR. 30 , 31 Unfortunately, we were not able to address this issue with a validated approach and did not include ethnicity in the regression models. Clinical measures included method of detection, screening interval, stage, grade, molecular subtype, and histology. Method of detection and screening interval were obtained from the ChristianaCare EHR. Consistent with prior approaches, method of detection was abstracted from the breast surgeon’s initial consult note and categorized as “screening” when detected during routine screening mammography in an otherwise asymptomatic patient; “clinical/symptomatic” when the patient or a provider observed physical changes (eg, new mass, swelling, discharge); or “unknown.” 32 As method of detection is a function of screening interval and tumor biology, this variable was only presented for descriptive purposes and not included in regression models. 33 Screening interval was based on the most recent record of screening mammogram (records of mammograms conducted outside ChristianaCare populate into the EHR via a regional health information exchange). Screening interval was categorized based on the time interval since the last known screening (plus a buffer) into regular/annual screening (≤15 months), inconsistent screening ( 36 months/no prior screening), or the interval between (15-36 months). Cases with missing data were categorized into “prior screening but unknown date” and “unknown screening.” The remaining clinical measures were obtained from the cancer registry. As recommended, BC cases were categorized as advanced if the American Joint Committee on Cancer prognostic pathologic stage was IIA-IV. 6 , 34 Grade was categorized as 1, 2, 3, or unknown. Histology was categorized into lobular or nonlobular. Nonlobular cases included ductal carcinoma, ductal adenocarcinoma, ductal carcinoma mixed with other types of carcinoma, or as a specific nonlobular special type. 21 As previously described, 7 molecular subtypes were dichotomized into TNBC, when ER, PR, and HER2 were all known negative, and non-TNBC for all other cases. ER and PR status were classified as negative when 1% of cells had nuclear positivity. HER2 was classified as negative when 10% cells had incomplete, faint/barely perceptible membrane staining (score 0) or 10% of cells had incomplete, faint/barely perceptible membrane staining (score 1+). Patients were categorized geographically based on whether they resided in 1 of 2 previously described advanced BC hotspots. 6 Briefly, these hotspots were identified after geocoding patient residential addresses at the time of diagnosis and employing the method of spatial intensity. Spatial intensity is a spatial statistical function defined to represent the expected number of events (ie, advanced BC cases) per unit area at a location, which is estimated using the kernel density approach. 35 , 36 One hotspot defined by a significantly greater ratio of spatial intensities of advanced to nonadvanced BC cases was observed in Wilmington, Delaware (advanced). A second hotspot defined by a significantly greater ratio of advanced BC cases among women under age 50 relative to all other BC cases was observed in Middletown, Delaware ( 50/advanced). Statistical analysis Descriptive statistics were used to characterize BC patients by demographic and clinical measures. Descriptive statistics were stratified by geographic area and by “cases” vs “controls” (ie, advanced vs nonadvanced; 50/advanced vs other). Two logistic regression models were used to estimate the odds of advanced BC (vs nonadvanced) and 50/advanced (vs other) based on screening interval. Both models adjusted for race, grade, molecular subtype, and histology. To minimize excluding cases for missing data, categories for unknown screening and grade were included. Next, to evaluate how well the models explained the hotspots, the expected numbers of BC cases were generated for each hotspot area by summing the predicted probabilities of a case within each hotspot using the respective logistic regressions. The ratio of the observed to expected (OE) number of BC cases for each hotspot was estimated for the null (ie, no covariates) and final models. To visualize spatial variation in screening interval, similar to the approach used to identify advanced BC hotspots, 6 the ratio of spatial intensities was estimated for patients with inconsistent screening ( 36 months/no prior screening) relative to patients who completed regular/annual screening ( 15 months). A fine mesh of generic locations was placed over the study region, with spatial intensity estimated at each location, and the results mapped to display a continuous spatial variation in the concentration of the ratio of inconsistent relative to regular screening events. The spatial intensity estimates were edge-corrected to improve estimation near the catchment area boundary, 37 and an adaptive bandwidth for smoothing accommodated the differences in population density ranging from urban to rural areas. 38 , 39 Tolerance regions denoting areas with significantly higher risk of inconsistent BC screening (“hotspots”) were identified using asymptotic methods. 38 , 40 The advanced BC and TNBC hotspots documented in our prior reports were superimposed on this map to illustrate the spatial relationships between screening, tumor biology, and advanced BC. 6 , 7 Finally, a bar graph presented the percentage of the “worst-case” levels for histology (lobular), molecular subtype (TNBC), grade (3), and screening ( 36 months/no screening) observed for each of the hotspots and the full catchment area. Results Table 1 reports the descriptive statistics for the demographic and clinical characteristics stratified by geographic area and case type for the 3492 invasive BC cases. Within the Wilmington advanced BC hotspot, Black women made up more than 95% of the cases (vs 23% for the catchment area), reflecting residential segregation in this area. Relative to nonadvanced cases, advanced cases were younger, more likely to be TNBC, less likely to be grade 3, more likely to have a lobular history, less likely to engage in regular screening, and more likely to have a clinical/symptomatic method of detection. Table 2 presents the logistic regression model results. Univariate analyses showed that Black race, TNBC, inconsistent screening ( 36 months/no prior screening), and higher grade (2 or 3) were all significantly associated with greater odds of advanced BC. In addition, unknown screening interval and both types of unknown grade were positively associated with advanced BC odds. Unknown grade in the context of neoadjuvant chemotherapy had a particularly strong association with advanced BC, consistent with the indications for this treatment. By contrast, other/unknown race, histology, and screening within 15-36 months or at an unknown interval were not associated with advanced BC odds. In the multivariate model, the relationships between the covariates and the outcome were qualitatively unchanged with 2 exceptions: lobular histology became significantly associated with advanced BC ( P .001) and unknown screening interval attenuated to nonsignificance ( P = .146). Discussion This study builds on a prior report that identified BC hotspots in a community cancer center catchment area to identify factors that contribute to explaining these hotspots. As hypothesized, inconsistent BC screening was associated with greater odds of advanced BC overall and among women under age 50 across the catchment area. These associations remained even after adjusting for tumor characteristics related to aggressiveness and screening sensitivity. In the adjusted models, we observed that patients with inconsistent BC screening had more than twice the odds of advanced BC as patients who screened regularly. The final multivariate model largely explained the Wilmington hotspot, defined by an elevated ratio of advanced to nonadvanced BC cases. Both inconsistent screening and tumor characteristics (molecular subtype, histology, and grade) contributed to the higher number of observed advanced BC cases than would be expected based on the underlying population. When visualized, the advanced BC hotspot was located at the intersection of TNBC and inconsistent screening hotspots. Although both more aggressive tumor biology and inconsistent BC screening each contributed to higher odds of advanced BC across the catchment area, the advanced BC hotspot was situated in a geographic area where both factors were more prevalent. The Wilmington hotspot also had the highest percentage of cases that were TNBC, grade 3, and inconsistent screening relative to the catchment area and other hotspots. The reduced uptake of BC screening is likely secondary to health-care access and other upstream factors in this region. 41-43 Triple-negative BC risk has been linked with adverse socioeconomic and built environment exposures, 44-46 which we previously documented as being elevated within the Wilmington hotspot. 7 , 47 , 48 The final multivariate model partly explained the Middletown hotspot, defined by an elevated ratio of 50/advanced to other BC cases. Inconsistent screening and tumor characteristics (molecular subtype and grade) were factors in the Middletown hotspot, and more prevalent relative to the catchment area, but only accounted for approximately half of the excess cases. Other unmeasured factors may be operating in this hotspot. In our prior report, we observed a larger hotspot of BC diagnosed before age 50 regardless of stage that subsumed the 50/advanced hotspot. 6 In a recent report issued by the Delaware Department of Health and Social Services, an even larger geographic area surrounding the Middletown hotspots was identified as having a significantly elevated all-cancer incidence rate. 49 It remains unclear whether these more broadly elevated rates of BC incidence among younger women and all-cancer incidence are associated with the 50/advanced BC hotspot, and it is an area of active investigation. This study has several strengths and limitations. The use of spatial intensity methods made possible by “point-level” residential address data offers advantages over aggregated approaches. When cases are aggregated to an area unit (eg, census tracts), information on spatial variation within those area units is lost and hotspots that cut across census tracts may not be detectable. In addition, this study is novel for its use of both screening and tumor characteristics data for a cancer center catchment area population. The single-site retrospective design of this study limits causal inference and the generalizability of model findings to other geographic regions. Nevertheless, other cancer centers can employ similar methods to inform COE efforts within their catchment areas. Second, the method of detection was determined retrospectively from the surgical note because prospective determinations by the radiologist were not available, raising concerns about misclassification. 50 Finally, this study did not include residential history for the BC cases, key to understanding compositional and contextual effects. For example, Middletown has experienced rapid population growth in recent decades. 51 If a large proportion of the in-migrating population shared a common risk factor based on prior exposures, then the hotspot may have more to do with the composition of residents than the Middletown context. Regardless, it remains important to prioritize this geographic area for early detection. The COE implications of this study’s results are clearer for the Wilmington hotspot. We are now piloting an embedded community health worker (CHW) initiative in Wilmington. A systematic review found that CHWs can effectively address barriers to BC screening and increase uptake. 52 Building on this approach, we aim to systematically identify hotspot residents who are nonadherent to BC screening guidelines, administer validated risk assessments, and navigate residents to screening (and high-risk prevention programs, where applicable). 53 We are also strengthening partnerships with local community-based organizations to address upstream neighborhood exposures and health-care access. The long-term objective is to replicate this approach across hotspots statewide in partnership with the Delaware Cancer Consortium. 54 More broadly, this study illustrates the potential benefits of extending catchment area approaches to community cancer centers. Approximately 25 million Americans across 15% of US counties live outside of an NCI-DCC catchment area. 55 When accounting for population density and cancer burden, 50% of US counties may receive inadequate NCI-DCC coverage. 55 With efforts underway to improve COE frameworks, 56 adopting more comprehensive geographic approaches can reach segments of the US population not served by NCI-DCCs.
