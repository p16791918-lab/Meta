Introduction According to estimates, the United States will see 287,850 newly diagnosed cases of invasive breast cancer and 51,400 cases of ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) in the year 2022 [ 1 ]. It is also projected that 43,250 fatalities will be linked to breast cancer in that particular year [ 1 ]. Breast cancer is the most commonly occurring type of cancer in women globally [ 1 ]. It is of utmost importance to promptly detect breast cancer as it can significantly influence the available treatments and improve the likelihood of surviving the disease. Discovering the presence of cancer in its initial phase’s results in a 90% chance of surviving for five years, however, if the diagnosis occurs at a more advanced stage, the survival rate declines to 25% within the same time frame [ 2 ]. Current studies are actively investigating the roots and fundamental reasons behind this extensively varied sickness. The chest region of a mammal consists of a mixture of fatty tissue and fibrous components, as well as specific glands and ducts responsible for the production and delivery of milk to the tips of the mammary glands. Breast cancer can originate in the lobules or ducts and is classified as metastatic if it has spread to other parts of the body, otherwise, it is considered non-metastatic. A non-invasive diagnostic procedure is crucial for identifying breast cancer in its early stages, often detecting conditions such as ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS), where the cancer is confined to the breast ducts. When the growth begins in the lobules or ducts and progresses, it is known as either invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) or invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC) [ 3 , 4 ]. Scientists have identified different molecular types of breast cancer through analysis of hormone receptor detection and cell proliferation markers. This method of classification takes into account the distinct molecular characteristics of the tumor, such as the presence of estrogen receptor (ER) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), the degree of proliferation indicated by the Ki67 marker, as well as any mutations in the progesterone receptor (PR) gene, which can influence tumor growth and response to hormone therapy. These groupings are officially classified as basal/triple negative breast cancer (TNBC), Luminal A, HER2-enriched, and Luminal B. Tumors with pronounced levels of HER2 protein are categorized as “HER2 enriched,” whereas TNBCs lack any markers for ER, HER2, or PR [ 5 – 7 ]. Luminal A tumors are characterized by lower levels of Ki 67 and are associated with a more positive prognosis as compared to Luminal B tumors. Luminal B tumors, on the other hand, have higher levels of Ki 67 and are often diagnosed at a later stage. Compared to other types of breast cancer, TNBCs have a particularly poor prognosis and display remarkable aggressiveness in their ability to spread to other parts of the body [ 8 , 9 ] (Fig. 1 C). Based on estimations, roughly 10% of people who are diagnosed with breast cancer have a genetic predisposition or history of the disease within their family. These mutations are more common in people of Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry, but can also occur in individuals of other ethnicities [ 10 , 11 ]. In addition, alterations in these particular genes are the underlying causes of approximately 20% of TNBC cases, while TNBC itself accounts for 12% of all breast cancer diagnoses in the United States. The five-year survival rate for TNBC shows a range of 8–16% [ 12 , 13 ]. Studies have shown that in the early stages of breast cancer, there is often an increase in the activity of certain genes, namely p53, GATA 3, Myc, ERBB2, PTEN, FGFR1, PIK3CA, and CCND1 [ 14 ]. Alterations in other genes, including PTEN, BRCA1, TP53, and BRCA2, can also increase the likelihood of developing breast cancer [ 15 ]. The initial stages and progress of breast cancer are heavily influenced by the immediate environment of the cancer, particularly the composition of immune cells [ 16 ]. It has also been suggested that estrogen can stimulate the growth of tumors in the breast tissue. Evidence supports the notion that regular and long-term exposure to estrogen can have harmful effects on breast health and increase the likelihood of breast cancer [ 17 ]. Other factors that increase the chances of developing breast cancer include being overweight, growing older, drinking excessive amounts of alcohol, receiving hormone replacement therapy, having a high density of tissue in the breasts, smoking, and certain events during pregnancy, such as experiencing a late-age first pregnancy, never having a full-term pregnancy, or having complications during pregnancy, which can affect long-term hormonal exposure and breast tissue changes [ 18 – 23 ]. Some factors that have been linked to a higher risk include starting menstruation at a young age, experiencing menopause later in life, taking hormone replacement therapy, using oral contraceptives, being exposed to radiation, and having benign tumors [ 24 – 27 ]. Certain risk factors may be controlled by taking prevention measures, such as incorporating more physical activity and altering one’s lifestyle, which can significantly decrease the overall effects of the disease [ 28 ]. In modern society, there is an increasing prevalence of working night shifts, which is particularly notable in developed countries. The rising popularity of 24-hour work and leisure activities has led to a significant increase in the amount of artificial light exposure for a large segment of the population, particularly women. This is the reason behind it [ 29 , 30 ]. Although night shifts play a crucial role in offering uninterrupted, top-notch assistance to patients within the nursing profession, they also contribute to increased levels of anxiety and strain, potentially causing both physical and psychological ailments and resulting in approximately 60% of absences from work [ 31 ]. Furthermore, working night shifts increases the likelihood of errors, which can negatively impact the quality of care provided [ 31 ]. Many studies have been carried out regarding the correlation between breast cancer and night shift work in nurses, and it has been found that there is a possible association based on epidemiological evidence. It has been observed that a significant number of nurses (50–100%) have developed breast cancer after working on night shifts [ 32 ]. There are suggestions that cancers may be more prevalent due to the presence of artificial light during nighttime and the disturbance of sleep patterns resulting from working night shifts [ 1 , 2 ]. Various physiological mechanisms could potentially be involved in this phenomenon, such as suppressing the production of melatonin, detrimental impacts on metabolic mechanisms, alterations in hormone levels, and adjustments in the functioning of circadian genes. Studies have demonstrated a link between disruptions in the methylation of DNA, which is responsible for regulating the expression of genes, and the onset of breast cancer. These changes in DNA methylation patterns involve circadian genes [ 3 – 5 ]. Up until now, there has been insufficient investigation into how working night shifts affects the methylation of circadian genes. Furthermore, the results have been inconclusive, as the affected genes and the direction of the effects have varied among studies [ 6 – 8 ]. The objective of this study is to explore the potential link between differences in epigenetic and genetic factors, specifically in individuals who work night shifts, and their increased susceptibility to breast cancer. One of the first illnesses connected to these mutations was cancer [ 9 ]. Cancer development is linked to changes in the field of epigenetics, specifically in terms of adjustments to DNA methylation, histones, microRNA amounts, and long non-coding RNA [ 10 , 11 ]. Changes can be influenced by various factors within and outside of one’s body, such as genetic makeup, surroundings, diet, level of stress, and exposure to potentially cancer-causing substances. The development of breast cancer is a complicated sequence of events that can be affected by a range of modifications in epigenetic and genetic elements. To better understand the mechanism of epigenetics, scientists have studied DNA methylation which involves adding a methyl group to the DNA sequence between the cytosine and guanine base pairs. The phenomenon takes place in groups of molecules known as CpG islands, where cytosine and guanine molecules are present. This occurrence has the potential to greatly impact gene expression and the progression of cancer [ 12 , 13 ]. Methylation is capable of impeding the binding of transcription factors to DNA, or attracting proteins that alter histones, which ultimately leads to the suppression of gene function. Mutations in DNA have been detected in breast cancer cases and they are thought to be affected by differences in DNA methylation, which is controlled by specific enzymes referred to as DNMTs and TETs. It has been demonstrated that these disparities contribute to the growth and advancement of breast cancer [ 14 – 18 ]. Additional proteins, including GADD45, AID, and APOBEC, have been linked to breast cancer development due to their role in altering gene expression. An instance of a substance that plays a significant role in repairing DNA and regulating gene expression by means of epigenetic processes is GADD45 [ 19 , 20 ]. As per studies, there may be a correlation between the GADD45 and BRCA1 genes in the development of breast cancer as they may play a role in repairing damaged nucleotides. AID proteins have the ability to actively eliminate DNA methylation, which may contribute to the formation of cancerous tumors [ 22 , 23 ]. In addition, the advancement of breast cancer has been linked to the procedure of APOBEC mutagenesis. An intriguing point to note is that it can also trigger immune responses that hinder the development of cancerous growths [ 24 – 27 ]. A correlation has been discovered by researchers between breast cancer and unusual DNA methylation, specifically in the regulatory regions of specific genes. This may lead to the suppression of genes that help prevent tumor growth, such as HOXA5, TMS1, p16, RASSF1A, and BRCA1 [ 28 , 29 ]. These genetic elements play vital roles in physiological processes like sensing estrogen, cellular death and reproduction, regulating cellular proliferation, and overseeing the mending of DNA. One example is the abnormal methylation that occurs in the promoter region of the cell cycle checkpoint gene p16INK4a, which is responsible for the absence of the gene in the early stages of non-inherited breast cancer [ 32 ]. The act of altering histone proteins, which are involved in organizing chromatin, can be achieved through either the addition or removal of chemical groups. Histone acetylation is a process that can disturb the interaction between histones and DNA, resulting in a looser chromatin structure and an increase in gene expression. The management of this process is supervised by specialized proteins called histone acetyltransferases (HATs) and histone deacetylases (HDACs). Modifications to histone levels, particularly P300, have been associated with the progression and growth of breast cancer [ 33 , 34 ]. The equilibrium may additionally be impacted by a specific category of proteins called sirtuins, which have been noted to have different impacts on different subtypes of breast cancer [ 35 , 36 ]. Histone methylation is a procedure that modifies the presence of methyl molecules on specific lysine and arginine amino acids, with the ability to increase or decrease the activity of genes. The formation of breast cancer has been linked to fluctuations in histone methylation, a process regulated by enzymes like methyltransferases (KMTs) and demethylases (KDMs). Research has also indicated a correlation between specific alterations to histones and the level of malignancy displayed by tumors, as well as the presence of malignant cells in lymph nodes, for instances of breast cancer [ 37 , 38 ]. Breast cancer is greatly influenced by epigenetic modifications, which play a crucial role in controlling key genes associated with multiple biological processes. These alterations contribute significantly to the initiation and progression of the condition. Having a thorough understanding of these protocols provides various possibilities for accurate approaches in treating and controlling breast cancer. A significant portion of the genetic makeup of humans consists of RNA which does not play a role in the creation of proteins. This encompasses a range of different classifications, including lengthy non-coding RNAs, tiny microRNAs, circular RNAs, small nucleolar RNAs, and PIWI-interacting RNAs. This is in contrast to the small number of genes found in the genome that are responsible for making proteins, making up only 2% of the total. Numerous studies have demonstrated that a significant amount of non-coding RNA molecules are not functioning effectively in various types of cancer, including breast cancer. The non-coding RNAs play a crucial role in important biological processes that affect breast cancer, such as cellular locomotion, growth and differentiation, angiogenesis, apoptosis, invasiveness, and response to treatment. Extensive research has been conducted on the functions of non-coding RNAs, with many efforts being made to use them as markers for detecting, anticipating, and treating breast cancer [ 39 – 41 ]. In a particular type of cancer, research has revealed that microRNAs play a significant role in regulating multiple cellular processes, such as intercellular communication, apoptosis, estrogen receptor alpha expression, cellular morphology, metastasis, nuclear receptor activation, cellular flexibility, and the tumor microenvironment. Primarily, it must be recognized that these specific microRNAs have the ability to move or discharge via exosomes from malignant cells to nearby benign fibroblasts, resulting in the formation of cancer-promoting fibroblasts [ 42 ]. The Cancer Genome Atlas Network has utilized a comprehensive analysis of genetic information, specifically DNA copy numbers, exon sequencing, mRNA arrays, DNA methylation, reverse-phase protein arrays, and microRNA sequencing, to identify three highly mutated genes (TP53, PIK3CA, and GATA3) that are commonly found across various forms of breast cancer [ 43 ]. Moreover, there have been recent discoveries of important mutated genes such as PTPN22, TBX3, AFF2, RUNX1, SF3B1, PTPRD, CBFB, PIK3R1, NF1, and CCND3, which had not previously been linked to breast cancer. Moreover, the research has once again confirmed the existence of commonly recognized breast cancer genes like MLL3, GATA3, PIK3CA, TP53, RB1, PTEN, AKT1, CDH1 and CDKN1B. The study also revealed variations in the frequency and kinds of genetic changes in the different intrinsic mRNA groups of breast cancer, which include luminal A, basal-like, luminal B, and HER2-enriched subtypes. For instance, the basal-like group exhibited a greater occurrence of TP53 mutations that resulted in non-functioning or altered proteins, whereas the luminal A and B groups had a higher proportion of TP53 mutations that caused slight changes in the protein structure. In addition, discrepancies were identified in the changes in the number of copies and amounts of proteins expressed for the various types of mRNA. It is notable that individuals undergoing breast cancer therapy with aromatase inhibitors (AIs) had a higher percentage of mutations in the ESR1 gene, which is responsible for regulating estrogen levels. These genetic changes can result in decreased sensitivity of the receptor to popular therapies such as GDC-0810, fulvestrant, RU-58,688, and AZD9496 due to their ability to stimulate transcription without the presence of a ligand. In particular, it was discovered that two mutations, D538G and ESR1 Y537S, are linked to a decreased chance of recovery and a more advanced course of the illness. Recent trials in the medical field have proven the success of utilizing both palbociclib, a CDK4/6 inhibitor, and letrozole, as a potential treatment for more serious cases of breast cancer. Moreover, the PAM50 analysis, which evaluates the activity of 50 specific genes in initial cases of breast cancer, has yielded significant information about the genetic traits and potential treatment options for personalized care. However, the involvement of these genes in the development of breast cancer is intricate and includes a combination of different molecular mechanisms. Research has shown that several genes, including TBX3, CASP8, MAP3K1, TET2, NOTCH2, MYC, and CCND1, have been linked to breast cancer. However, this study suggests that many other genes may also be important factors in the development of this disease [ 44 ]. More investigation is needed to determine a clear cause-and-effect connection between these particular genes and the development of breast cancer. Relationship between night shift and breast cancer The natural pattern of the body’s cycle can be disturbed by working night shifts and prolonged exposure to light during normal sleeping periods. This can cause alterations in the release of essential hormones such as melatonin, prolactin, and serotonin, which are crucial components of the endocrine system. Melatonin, which is primarily created by the pineal gland, is greatly affected by light and its synthesis and dissemination can have significant consequences on the systems related to immunity, hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal, pituitary-thyroid, and pituitary-gonadal [ 45 , 46 ]. Along with brightness, the quantity of tryptophan consumed can impact the production of melatonin, but the circadian rhythm has little impact [ 47 ]. Melatonin levels exhibit a daily cycle, reaching their highest point at night and declining throughout the day [ 48 ]. When it attaches to certain receptors such as MT1, MT3, and MT2, as well as RZR/ROR receptors, this hormone is activated and released. The strength of light, daily patterns, and the existing levels of melatonin all contribute to this mechanism [ 49 , 50 ]. The National Cancer Institute in the United States has put forth the idea that insufficient melatonin production from the pineal gland could potentially heighten the likelihood of developing breast cancer [ 51 ]. In 1987, Stevens proposed the idea that an individual’s occupation during night-time hours, combined with prolonged exposure to light, could potentially increase their risk of developing breast cancer. The proposed idea states that working at night and being exposed to light may decrease the body’s production of melatonin, leading to elevated levels of estrogen and a potential increase in the risk of breast cancer [ 52 ]. According to data gathered from animal studies and certain epidemiological research, the International Agency for Research on Cancer determined in 2007 that working night shifts may have the potential to cause cancer in humans [ 53 ]. However, the results of subsequent studies have been inconclusive [ 54 ]. Breast cancer and genetic alterations in nurses with night shift A fixed timetable and the interconnections among sleep cycles, the body’s immune response, and the secretion of melatonin are significantly involved in regulating the excessive growth of cells in cancerous masses [ 55 ]. Operating in different time periods, which has the potential to alter our natural body clock, disrupts this process and may potentially play a role in the proliferation and advancement of cancer. Studies have shown that changes in the expression of clock genes are linked to increased cancer activity and growth in laboratory settings [ 56 , 57 ]. Additionally, research on the study of disease prevalence has shown a link between breast cancer and variations in the genetic components that control the body’s natural rhythm [ 58 , 59 ]. More research is needed to fully comprehend the impact that changes in clock gene expression may have on the initiation and progression of cancer. Furthermore, it is crucial to consider the influence of irregular or nighttime shifts on these variations and their potential effects on the gene expression related to cancer. A proposed idea states that people who work on a schedule that rotates may be at a greater risk of developing breast cancer because their body’s functions, such as melatonin, 17-β-estradiol, and clock gene activity, can be affected [ 60 ]. As an illustration, a research was conducted to examine the differences between individuals who have rotating daytime (DT) shifts and those who have shift work (SW) schedules. Through this study, significant variances were observed in a number of clock genes including BMAL1, PER1, CLOCK, NPAS2, PER2, REVERB, PER3, CRY2, and PER1 among the two groups [ 60 ]. Additionally, it was found that shift working nurses had higher levels of 17-β-estradiol, emphasizing the impact of shift work on hormone levels and its potential connection to breast cancer vulnerability. The study also emphasized the importance of chronotype in relation to the levels of 17-β-estradiol and the activation of clock genes. Researchers have noticed a connection between midwives and nurses who rotate night shifts, changes in their clock genes, and a heightened likelihood of developing breast cancer [ 61 ]. The study revealed that rotating night shift workers had a significantly higher prevalence of the less common CRY1 TT genetic variant compared to day workers. A study also revealed that this particular genetic makeup was associated with prolonged periods of working in shifts. Based on these results, being employed during the nighttime hours may increase the chances of experiencing breast cancer as a result of its effects on hormones and circadian rhythms. Additional research is crucial for a thorough comprehension of the connection between working night shifts and different forms of breast cancer. This complex illness is influenced by a range of factors including genetics and external influences [ 62 – 64 ]. Furthermore, Lie et al. [ 65 ] found that longer periods of rotating shift work were linked to a higher incidence of ER + breast cancers, especially among premenopausal women. Similarly, their findings suggest that progesterone may have the ability to reduce the negative impacts of regularly working night shifts [ 66 ]. Engaging in night shifts can heighten the risk of developing breast cancer as a result of reduced levels of melatonin caused by exposure to artificial light-at-night (LAN) [ 67 ]. The pineal gland plays a crucial role in the production and secretion of melatonin in the human body. However, being exposed to too much light at night can reduce its production, putting its ability to protect against cancer at risk [ 68 ]. Usually, melatonin is released at around 9 PM and reaches its peak concentration at 3:30 AM, before gradually declining until sunrise. In addition, melatonin plays a crucial role in regulating sleep and wake cycles, and it also possesses characteristics that can aid in combatting cancer. This entails inhibiting tumor growth and decreasing the levels of reproductive hormones associated with the onset of cancer [ 69 , 70 ]. Research has shown that the normal production of melatonin during the night has been linked to inhibiting the growth of breast cancer. However, exposure to light at night (LAN) can disrupt this potential benefit [ 69 , 70 ]. In a recent investigation, scientists utilized images taken from satellites to examine the degree of illumination from artificial sources during nighttime, known as LAN, and its correlation with the occurrences of breast cancer in a sample of 147 participants. The findings indicated a clear correlation between the incidence of breast cancer and the level of exposure to LAN. People with greater exposure to LAN have a significantly higher likelihood, by 73%, of developing breast cancer in comparison to those with lower levels of exposure [ 71 ]. Melatonin has another role besides regulating sleep, which is controlling how estrogen is active in the body. The desired outcome is attained through influencing the functions of cells, resulting in an indirect effect on the system of hormones [ 72 ]. Melatonin levels have been found to correlate with elevated estrogen levels, potentially resulting in higher lifetime exposure to estrogen. There is a potential for increased susceptibility to breast cancer associated with this factor [ 73 ]. Multiple researches have confirmed that the level of estrogen an individual is exposed to during their lifetime is a crucial factor in determining their chances of developing breast cancer [ 73 ]. Peplonska et al. investigated the relationship between rotating night shift work and levels of estradiol, testosterone, and dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate, as well as the influence of non-occupational factors [ 74 ]. For this research, participants were required to provide blood and urine samples every morning. Afterward, they utilized a technique called multiple linear regression in order to analyze the connection between various factors such as lifestyle choices, personal characteristics, reproductive behavior, and physical traits, and the concentrations of hormones, which were considered the outcome variables. They also investigated the influence of an individual’s chronotype, which refers to their inherent sleep schedule, on this correlation. The results revealed a significant link between working night shifts for more than 15 years and higher levels of estradiol in menopausal women, especially those with a preference for waking up early. Additionally, significant connections were found between hormone concentrations and important variables like body mass index and age. To sum up, it can be concluded that prolonged nighttime work schedules may result in increased estradiol levels among menopausal females, particularly those who have a preference for morning hours [ 74 ]. The aim of their research was to examine the potential connection between these changes and important factors such as tumor characteristics and subgroups. These individuals were inquired about a range of topics relating to their sleeping patterns and potential influences that could affect their sleep quality. Scientific investigations were conducted using DNA extracted from colorless blood cells to establish the degree of methylation in specific genes responsible for impeding the development of tumors. The results revealed that there was no significant connection noted among the entire group, as well as the two separate subgroups distinguished by their employment schedules. This discovery implies a potential association between decreased sleep and heightened methylation levels in the CDKN2A gene for individuals with daytime occupations. Numerous studies have shown that individuals who work non-traditional shift schedules may have a higher likelihood of developing breast cancer compared to those with regular work schedules.
