Regulatory approval and access to safe, effective medicines rely on clinical trials. Clinical trial participation should ideally reflect the demographics of the affected population; appropriate representation, access, and inclusion are foundational to promoting health and well-being. Clinical trials, however, serve as both a gateway and a gatekeeper and are particularly challenging for populations that have been historically excluded from biomedical research. From women and racial minorities to pediatric and rural populations, purposeful inclusion in clinical trials is gaining attention. The gap between intent and participation persists, threatening the equitable evaluation of safety and efficacy and, ultimately, the broad impact of therapeutic advances. This perspective summarizes insights from the Patient Forum at the 2025 Annual Meeting of the American Society for Clinical Pharmacology and Therapeutics. The forum featured a diverse panel of United States experts—patients, clinicians, researchers, and advocates—whose lived experiences and professional expertise converged into actionable strategies for increasing clinical trial representation. THE IMPERATIVE FOR REPRESENTATION “Change Clinical Trial from a Dirty Word to a Halo.” Latoya Bolds-Johnson Clinical trials are the cornerstone of evidence-based medicine. When trial populations fail to reflect the demographics of those most affected by a disease, the resulting therapies risk being less effective—or even harmful—for underrepresented groups. Case example—Triple Negative Breast Cancer (TNBC) Black women are 2.7 times more likely to develop TNBC, have a 28% higher breast cancer mortality rate, and suffer a twofold higher recurrence rate than white women, yet Black women comprise 5% of cancer trial participants. 1 – 4 These disparities are not solely attributable to social determinants of health. 5 Even among Black women with the same level of insurance and those living in high socioeconomic neighborhoods, mortality rates remain disproportionately high. 1 The lack of representation of Black women in clinical trials has profound implications: without adequate inclusion, physiological differences in disease and drug response remain unstudied and unaddressed in treatment protocols. Lived experience as evidence To understand the human impact of trial underrepresentation, panelists shared their lived experience. Ricki Fairley, a TNBC survivor, spoke of her journey from diagnosis to advocacy and how a clinical trial saved her life. In response to learning that Black women are disproportionately affected by TNBC but rarely included in trials, she launched the “When We Tri(al)” campaign. When We Tri(al) is a multimedia initiative designed to drive awareness, provide education, and give access to clinical trials. Over 65,000 people have visited whenw etrial. org, and over 30,000 have taken the next step to search for clinical trials that are right for them. Latoya Bolds-J ohnson, a clinician diagnosed with TNBC at age 36, shared her journey of self-advocacy. Initially dismissed by her oncologist, Johnson discovered, through her own review, that an FDA-approved immunotherapy existed for her condition—approved on the very day she was diagnosed. Johnson discovered a clinical trial that not only altered the course of her disease but also transformed her into an advocate for patient empowerment. These stories illustrate a critical point: many patients and healthcare providers are unaware of clinical trials or misinformed about their purpose. Mistrust, rooted in historical abuses, continues to shape perceptions. Not only do some patients lack trust in research, but providers may fail to initiate what they fear will be an uncomfortable conversation. Johnson’s message is clear: “We are worthy. Our bodies should be studied just like anyone else’s.” Current clinical trial representation While regulatory efforts and growing awareness have led to improvements in representation, clinical trials worldwide continue to underrepresent key demographic groups. Of 339 drugs/biologics approved by the FDA (2015–2021), 25% of pivotal trials included ≤1% Black participants, and 21% included ≤1% Asian/Asian-American participants. 6 From 2022 to 2024, multinational clinical trials supported the approval of 147 new drugs/biologics 7 ( Figure 1 ). In those trials, women comprised 21–68% across drug programs and older adults ( 65) represented 35% of participants in age-related conditions like oncology and cardiovascular disease. 7 Although demographics across clinical trials are informative, these data do not tell the full story. Benchmarking enrollment against the incidence and severity of disease, which can vary significantly within a therapeutic area and/or geographic region, is not commonly reported. The Representation Quotient (RQ) has been proposed as a metric of clinical trial representation, with an RQ of 1 indicating a match between the enrollment demographics and the geographic reference population. 8 The RQ could be adapted by defining the reference population by burden of disease within the geographic region to provide a clinically meaningful, patient-centric metric that could be tracked across sites and programs. A matter of science For 20% of new drugs/biologics approved by FDA, differences in drug exposure and/or response resulted in modified prescribing recommendations for certain populations, often long after regulatory approval. 9 In clinical trials, analyzing group differences, such as race, ethnicity, age, gender identity, and common comorbidities, may reveal biologically important factors. Subgroup representation in the data enables insights into safety, efficacy, and disease complexity. A matter of health equity Lack of representation affects the distribution and fairness of the long-term benefits of new therapies. Clinically relevant subgroup differences can be delayed for years until sufficient postmarketing data are collected and analyzed. New approaches include pharmacokinetic-pharmacodynamic studies in relevant subpopulations during phase II to inform labeling and prescribing (e.g., dose adjustments by sex to reduce adverse drug reactions 10 ). THE ROLE OF TRUST, COMMITMENT, AND SUSTAINABILITY Trust is crucial to the success of any clinical trial initiative and is built on sustained engagement, transparency, and reciprocity. Hope Krebill, executive director of a Midwestern National Cancer Institute (NCI)-designated comprehensive cancer center, described how the Masonic Cancer Alliance earned the trust of community oncologists across the state through listening tours, co-branding initiatives, and offering support beyond clinical trials. Dr. Barbara Bierer, faculty director of the Multi-Regional Clinical Trials Center (MRCT Center) and physician at Brigham and Women’s Hospital, cautioned against “parachute research,” where investigators engage communities only for the duration of a trial. Instead, institutions must commit to long-term partnerships that extend before, during, and after research activities. Ms. Fairley’s vision of a clinical trial experience is one infused with love—“a grandmother’s hug, a warm blanket, Luther Vandross playing in the background.” Patients are asking for more than access; the experience must be humane, dignified, and affirming. “Put a hug around science. Bring love to the clinical trial experience.” Ricki Fairley BARRIERS TO PARTICIPATION Beyond mistrust, the forum highlighted several barriers to participation: Lack of invitation: Patients are simply not asked to participate in trials. Implicit bias, systemic racism, and assumptions about compliance contribute to this exclusion. Limited understanding: Many patients and some providers lack a clear understanding of what clinical trials entail. Terms like “standard of care,” “placebo,” and “randomization” are not clearly defined, obfuscating value-concordant decision making. Timing of information: Patients typically learn about trials during crises—after diagnosis, when fear and urgency limit the amount of information one can process, and the concept of a clinical trial can be overwhelming. GLOBAL INITIATIVES DRIVING SYSTEMIC CHANGE Significant efforts are underway to shift clinical research from a mindset of exclusion to one of global inclusion. Clinical trial sponsors often balance many factors in the deliberation of study design and target population, including participant heterogeneity influencing sample size and power and estimated recruitment cadence influencing timeline for regulatory approval. Resources exist to inspire innovative problem-solving aligned with program goals (see Supplemental Material S1 ). The Clinical Trials Transformation Initiative, a public–private partnership co-founded by the FDA, promotes patient engagement in medical product development. The World Health Organization’s Global Action Plan for Clinical Trial Ecosystem Strengthening aims to embed trials into national health systems and support capacity-building in low- and middle-income countries. The MRCT Center has developed a Global Representation Roadmap to improve the rigor, representativeness, and ethics of clinical trials worldwide. The roadmap discusses country-specific regulatory alignment, capacity-building initiatives, ethics checkpoints, and includes a model Diversity Action Plan. In the United States, these plans are mandated by the Food and Drug Omnibus Reform Act of 2022. ACTIONABLE RECOMMENDATIONS As systemic change is a culmination of intentional efforts, panelists emphasized key next steps: Just ask. Asking about interest in clinical trials is more important than asking perfectly. Educate early and often. Integrate clinical trial education into (1) clinical care (e.g., anticipatory guidance) BEFORE patients are in crisis and (2) general and medical education. Engage trusted messengers: Ms. Fairley encourages outreach “where people live, work, play, pray, and slay.” Partner with survivors, pastors, teachers, barbers, and family members to disseminate information. Design for inclusion: Involve patients in study design, justify exclusion criteria, simplify language, tailor materials to cultural contexts, and provide financial and other resources to support participation. Decentralize trials: Use telemedicine and home-based procedures to reduce participant burden. Track and share metrics: Use data to evaluate and improve outreach effectiveness, enrollment rates, and retention. Fund sustainable community engagement: Allocate resources for sustained partnerships, including compensation for patient advocates and a commitment to sharing trial results. CONCLUSION Through stories of survival, advocacy, and innovation, the panelists provided actionable guidance to improve representation in clinical trials and ensure enrollment reflects the burden of disease. Representation, access, and inclusion are not endpoints; they are commitments that require collaboration, communication, compassion, and humility. While a complex interplay of social, economic, and scientific factors will always be present, and barriers will never be fully eliminated, each of us can help ensure forward momentum. As Fairley reminded the audience, “Treat others as you want to be treated.” When we center our science on the patient, listen to their stories, and act on their insights, we move closer to a world where therapeutic innovation truly serves all.
