1 Introduction Cancer remains a leading cause of death in the United States (US) [ 1 ]. Recent advances in screening and treatment have significantly improved survival rates, with more patients now living beyond 5 years after diagnosis [ 2 , 3 ]. By 2040, the number of cancer survivors in the US is projected to exceed 26 million, driven largely by the improved survival and population aging and growth [ 4 , 5 ]. As a result, cancer survivorship has become an increasingly important focus in both clinical care and public health. Despite improved survival, cancer survivors face elevated risks of mortality due to cancer recurrence, long‐term treatment effects, and overall poorer health outcomes [ 4 , 6 , 7 , 8 ]. Racial and ethnic disparities in cancer survival are well documented [ 9 , 10 , 11 ]. Certain racial/ethnic populations, such as Black, American Indian/Alaska Native (AI/AN), and Native Hawaiian and Other Pacific Islander (NHPI) individuals, consistently experience worse outcomes compared to non‐Hispanic white individuals [ 9 , 10 , 11 ] due to multiple factors, including later stage at diagnosis [ 12 ], lower insurance coverage [ 11 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 ], lower access to guideline‐concordant care [ 18 ], lower participation in genetic testing and clinical trials [ 19 ], and greater barriers to care due to social determinants of health factors [ 20 ]. While 5‐year survival is a common benchmark in oncology research [ 10 , 12 , 21 , 22 ], they may not fully reflect long‐term outcome patterns. By 2045, the U.S. population is projected to become racially and ethnically diverse, with non‐Hispanic White individuals comprising less than half of the population [ 23 ]. To date, few studies have examined survival beyond 5 years, particularly across racial and ethnic groups. Addressing this gap is increasingly urgent, as cancer incidence is projected to rise an estimated 99% among racial and ethnic minority populations, compared to 31% among non‐Hispanic White individuals [ 4 ]. Furthermore, the most pronounced growth is expected among younger individuals of color [ 13 , 24 , 25 , 26 ]. Existing research on racial and ethnic disparities in cancer survival remains limited for Hispanic and Asian groups [ 3 , 10 , 11 , 14 , 17 ]. Even less is known about AI/AN and NHPI populations, despite emerging evidence that these groups experience some of the lowest survival [ 27 , 28 , 29 ]. Moreover, despite having distinct cultures, data on cancer outcomes for NHPI is often aggregated with Asian populations, which can conceal important differences in health outcomes between the two groups [ 30 , 31 ]. To address these gaps, we examined survival outcomes up to 15 years post‐diagnosis across six racial/ethnic groups among men and women using data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program, a large, population‐based registry in the US. Because cancer stage and cancer site are two of the strongest predictors of survival [ 12 ], and both vary across racial and ethnic groups, we conducted stratified analyses by stage at diagnosis and by the five most common cancer sites among men and women. To further capture potential age variation, socioeconomic context, and temporal changes in survivorship, we examined racial and ethnic differences in 5‐year survival by age at diagnosis, county‐level income, and calendar year. 2 Methods 2.1 Study Design and Data Sources This retrospective cohort study utilized data from 22 registries in the SEER Program, the most comprehensive, population‐based cancer data source in the US. The November 2023 SEER data release included cancers diagnosed from 2000 to 2021, with follow‐up through 2021, within the SEER 22 registries, which represent approximately 48% of the U.S. population [ 32 ]. SEER provides enhanced coverage of geographic areas with relatively large racial/ethnic minority subgroups, ideal for studying racial/ethnic differences [ 33 ]. 2.2 Outcomes and Measures The primary outcome was time from the first primary cancer diagnosis to death or censoring. Causes of death was based on International Classification of Disease, Tenth Edition. Other variables included sex assigned at birth (hereafter referred to as sex); age at diagnosis; county‐level cost‐of‐living adjusted medium income, and cancer characteristics such as cancer type and stage of cancer at diagnosis. In this study, ethnicity refers to Hispanic origin (Hispanic vs. non‐Hispanic), and race refers to the racial categories reported in SEER (White, Black, Asian, American Indian/Alaska Native, and Native Hawaiian/Other Pacific Islander). Hispanic ethnicity was prioritized over race; individuals identifying as Hispanic were classified as Hispanic regardless of their reported race. Non‐Hispanic multiracial individuals were assigned to a single race category based on their primary race, with self‐report taking precedence over information from medical records or death certificates [ 34 ]. Accordingly, we classified cancer survivors into six mutually exclusive race/ethnicity groups: Hispanic, non‐Hispanic White, Black, Asian, NHPI, and AI/AN. We use “Hispanic” rather than “Latine/x” to align with the standard labelling in SEER. For ease of discussion, we refer to the 5 non‐Hispanic groups as White, Black, Asian, NHPI, AI/AN hereinafter. Cost‐of‐living‐adjusted county‐level median household income, which represents the median household income of the county where the individual resides, were from the American Community Survey [ 35 ]. County‐level median household income was grouped into eight $5000 categories, ranging from $30,000 to ≥ $60,000. The cutoffs were selected to provide sufficient granularity for potential linear or non‐linear associations while ensuring adequate sample sizes within each stratum. SEER stage at diagnosis data (classified as in situ, localized, regional or distant) was available for all cases diagnosed in 2004 and after [ 36 ]. Of note, in situ cancers in SEER are almost always urinary bladder cancers because SEER recodes in situ bladder cancers as invasive due to the lack of a reliable method to distinguish in situ from invasive disease for these cancers, while stage remains coded as in situ [ 34 , 36 ]. In situ cancers accounted for 2% of all malignancies and 41% of urinary bladder cancers in this study. 2.3 Analysis We estimated several complementary survival measures. All‐cause cumulative survival was calculated using standard life‐table methods. Cancer‐specific survival was assessed using both relative survival (using the Ederer II method) and cancer‐cause survival. Relative survival compares the observed survival among cancer survivors to the survival of a comparable cancer‐free population (matched on age, sex, race/ethnicity, calendar year, and geography) [ 37 ]. A relative survival value below one indicates that cancer survivors experience lower survival than their matched cancer‐free counterparts and vice versa. This approach does not rely on cause‐of‐death information, which can be incomplete or misclassified, and therefore served as our primary measure of cancer survival [ 37 , 38 , 39 ]. Cancer‐cause survival was the probability of surviving without death attributed to any cancer based on the cause of death documented on death certificates. For Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander (NHPI) individuals, we used cancer‐cause survival (instead of relative survival) for cancer‐specific survival because the SEER relative survival algorithm does not provide population‐specific life tables for this group. We first estimated all‐cause and cancer‐specific survival from diagnosis through 15 years of follow‐up by race/ethnicity and calculated survival ratios using White individuals as the reference group. A survival ratio below one indicates worse survival compared to White cancer survivors and vice versa. We then estimated cancer‐specific survival and corresponding survival ratios stratified by cancer stage and primary cancer site. Given the large number of cancer sites, we focused on the five most common sites among men (prostate, lung and bronchus, colon and rectum, melanoma of the skin, and urinary bladder) and women (breast, lung and bronchus, colon and rectum, uterine corpus, and melanoma of the skin), which collectively account for more than 60% of all cancers in men and women. In subsequent analyses, we estimated 5‐year relative survival by county‐level income, age at diagnosis, and calendar year. We focused on 5‐year survival for these analyses to maintain comparability with prior literature and to ensure adequate case counts, as the number of individuals at risk declines with increasing time since diagnosis. To assess the potential influence of county‐level socioeconomic context on survival, we also estimated ratios of relative survival by the percentage of residents identifying with racial and ethnic groups other than White. All analyses were stratified by sex. Estimates were age‐standardized (except for the analyses by age). SEER*Stat [ 33 ], HD*Calc [ 40 ], and Stata MP18.0 were used for analysis and visualization. 3 Results The study sample consisted of 10,894,683 individuals diagnosed with cancer from 2000 to 2021. The cohort was 13.1% Hispanic, 69.8% White, 10.9% Black, 5.5% Asian, 0.4% NHPI, and 0.3% AI/AN; 49% of the cohort was female and 51% was male. Stratified analysis by stage at diagnosis was restricted to the 8,365,162 cases diagnosed since 2004. Compared to White cancer survivors, all other racial/ethnic groups were more likely to be diagnosed at younger ages and later stages (see Table 1 for details and Supplemental Table S1 ). Column percentages may not sum to 100% due to rounding. 3.1 All‐Cause Survival Racial/ethnic differences were seen in all‐cause survival up to 15 years post‐diagnosis (Figure 1 ). Among males, AI/AN and NHPI men had the lowest survival throughout the entire follow‐up, widening over time (survival ratio, SR = 0.72 [95% CI = 0.70, 0.75] for AI/AN and 0.76 [95% CI = 0.73, 0.78] for NHPI at 10 years post‐diagnosis), with survival also lower for Black and Hispanic men as compared to White males (SR = 0.85 [95% CI = 0.84, 0.85] for Black and 0.92 [95% CI = 0.92, 0.93] for Hispanic at 10 years post‐diagnosis). Compared to White men, Asian men had worse all‐cause survival in the first 10 years post‐diagnosis (e.g., SR = 0.96 [95% CI = 0.95, 0.96] at 5 years post‐diagnosis), followed by slightly better survival beyond 10 years (e.g., SR = 1.02 [95% CI = 1.02, 1.03] at 15 years post‐diagnosis). Among females, all but Asian women exhibited lower survival than White females, with the poorest survival among Black and AI/AN women (SR = 0.77 [95% CI = 0.76, 0.77] for Black; 0.79 [95% CI = 0.77, 0.81] for AI/AN; 0.89 [95% CI = 0.87, 0.91] for NHPI; 0.95 [95% CI = 0.94, 0.95] for Hispanic; 1.08 [95% CI = 1.08, 1.09] for Asian at 10 years post‐diagnosis). By 15 years post‐diagnosis, Black and AI/AN women had approximately 25% lower survival than White females (SR = 0.74 [95% CI = 0.73, 0.74] for Black and 0.74 [95% CI = 0.71, 0.78] for AI/AN). These gaps persisted and widened throughout the observation period (Figure 1 ). Among men, AI/AN and NHPI cancer survivors had the lowest cancer survival (SR = 0.78 [95% CI = 0.75, 0.82] for AI/AN; 0.82 [95% CI = 0.80, 0.84] for NHPI at 10 years post‐diagnosis). Among women, Black and AI/AN cancer survivors had the lowest survival (SR = 0.79 [95% CI = 0.79, 0.89] for Black; 0.83 [95% CI = 0.80, 0.86] for AI/AN at 10 years post‐diagnosis). Despite the better all‐cause survival observed among Asian women, significant gaps in cancer survival emerged for this population 2 years after cancer diagnosis and persisted throughout the follow‐up (SR = 0.94 [95% CI = 0.93, 0.94] for Asian at 15 years post‐diagnosis). In addition to poorer cancer survival, Black, NHPI and AI/AN populations also exhibited poorer survival from non‐cancer causes, further exacerbating survival gaps among these groups (Figure S1 ). 3.3 Cancer Survival by Cancer Stage Racial/ethnic gaps in cancer survival widened with advancing cancer stage at diagnosis and increased over time post‐diagnosis (Figure 2 ). The largest gaps were observed among Black, AI/AN and NHPI individuals with distant cancers (among male: SR = 0.71 [95% CI = 0.68, 0.73] for Black, 0.67 [95% CI = 0.56, 0.78] for AI/AN, 0.53 [95% CI = 0.42, 0.64] for NHPI at 10 years post‐diagnosis; among female: SR = 0.75 [95% CI = 0.73, 0.77] for Black, 0.65 [95% CI = 0.53, 0.76] for AI/AN, 0.59 [95% CI = 0.49, 0.69] for NHPI at 10 years post‐diagnosis). For example, by year 15, Black men with localized cancers and Black men with distant cancers were 5% (SR = 0.95, 95% CI = 0.93, 0.98) and 35% (SR = 0.65, 95% CI = 0.60, 0.70) more likely to die from cancer than White males, respectively. For Asian women with distant cancers, a nonlinear pattern was seen. Asian women with distant cancers had higher survival than their White counterparts in the first 2 years (SR = 1.05 [95% CI = 1.04, 1.06] at 1 year post‐diagnosis), but poorer survival after 3 years, which continued to widening over time (SR = 0.90 [95% CI = 0.88, 0.92] at 10 years post‐diagnosis). Bars around survival ratio represent 95% confidence intervals. 4 Discussion Our analysis of survival up to 15 years post‐diagnosis of a large contemporary cohort of cancer survivors revealed significant and persistent gaps in survival outcomes, extending previous studies documenting racial/ethnic gaps in 5‐year survival [ 9 , 10 , 11 , 16 , 41 , 42 , 43 ]. White cancer survivors had better survival outcomes than Black, Asian, AI/AN, and NHPI cancer survivors, and these differences not only continued beyond the 5‐year mark but widened over time. These differences also persisted even after controlling for commonly recognized mediating factors such as income and stage at diagnosis [ 13 , 14 , 44 , 45 ]. The poorest survival was observed among AI/AN men and women, NHPI men, and Black women. By year 15, cancer survivors in these groups were at least 25% more likely to die compared to their White counterparts. Although largely driven by cancer‐related survival, lower non‐cancer survival further exacerbated gaps in AI/AN, NHPI and Black populations. Unlike other research documenting lower cancer mortality (which is affected by both incidence and survival) among Asian cancer survivors [ 10 , 12 , 27 ], we observed higher cancer death rate in Asian cancer survivors compared to White cancer survivors. This pattern aligns with evidence showing several disadvantages among Asian cancer survivors along the cancer continuum, including barriers to screening and timely diagnosis, later stage at diagnosis, and access to quality cancer care, all of which can contribute to the higher cancer death rate observed in this study [ 46 , 47 ]. The disproportionate cancer burden in Asian populations is further worsened by the rapidly rising cancer incidence among younger Asian women [ 45 ], outpacing other racial/ethnic groups. Methodologically, our finding suggests that the generally lower cancer incidence among Asian Americans can mask poorer cancer survival outcomes when examining mortality at the population level. Furthermore, lower death rates from major non‐cancer causes such as cardiovascular disease and chronic respiratory diseases among Asian Americans (vs. non‐Hispanic Whites) [ 48 , 49 ] can offset poorer cancer‐specific outcomes when studying all‐cause survival. Our analysis also revealed large survival gaps for NHPI individuals, underscoring the importance of studying NHPI separately. Of note, we used cause‐specific survival for NHPI survivors, whereas relative survival was used for all other racial/ethnic groups because NHPI‐specific life tables are not available. Existing literature suggests that NHPI populations experience higher non‐cancer mortality than the general population [ 42 , 50 , 51 ], which would lead to lower relative survival for NHPI survivors than their cause‐specific survival. Consequently, using cause specific survival may overestimate NHPI net survival, thereby underestimating the true extent of the survival disparities when comparing NHPI survivors with other groups. The differences observed between the relative survival and cause specific survival curves in our Figures S1–S5 are consistent with this expected pattern. This highlights the potentially even larger disparity in cancer survival experienced by NHPI cancer survivors. Future work incorporating NHPI specific life tables would improve methodological consistency and strengthen cross‐group comparability. Both Asian and NHPI populations are quite diverse, with many subgroups experiencing poorer access to cancer care, worse outcomes, and lower survival [ 28 , 37 , 52 ], often masked by data aggregation [ 31 , 32 ]. These findings highlight the need for further research and tailored interventions to address the distinct risks in Asian and NHPI populations at high risk to improve survival outcomes. Our findings of persistent and widening survival gaps among Black, NHPI and AI/AN populations, as compared to their White counterparts, are consistent with the cancer health literature [ 9 , 10 , 11 , 22 , 26 , 28 , 29 , 43 , 44 , 53 ]. Importantly, both cancer‐ and non‐cancer‐cause death was higher in these populations, contributing to the reduced life expectancy among Black, NHPI and AI/AN populations [ 54 , 55 , 56 ]. This highlights the need for a holistic approach to survivorship care that extends beyond the evaluation for cancer recurrence and new primary cancers [ 57 ] to include the evaluation and management of competing causes of death such as cardiovascular disease and diabetes in these groups [ 58 , 59 ]. In addition, greater emphasis is needed on wellness including lifestyle interventions. Treatment‐related complications can significantly burden individuals with cancer, affecting their ability to work and overall quality of life. Therefore, both surveillance for and management of long‐term side effects are crucial to adequate survivorship care delivery [ 8 , 60 ]. Survival gaps are not fully explained by stage at cancer diagnosis [ 9 , 22 ]. In fact, we observed larger racial/ethnic gaps in more advanced cancer suggesting lower access to high quality or advanced treatment and clinical trials [ 18 ]. Therefore, clinical trial participation by diverse individuals is crucial towards the understanding of the most effective treatments of cancer [ 19 ]. In this study, we found that racial and ethnic differences in cancer survival varied by cancer site. Extending prior research documenting disproportionate mortality burdens among Black patients in breast cancer (among women) and prostate cancer (among men) [ 12 , 61 ], our results show that these survival gaps continued to widen well beyond 5 years post‐diagnosis and throughout long‐term survivorship. Prior studies indicate that these disparities are not fully explained by tumor subtype or stage at diagnosis [ 12 , 61 ]. This pattern underscores the need for interventions that address both acute treatment outcomes and long‐term survival care, including timely cancer screening, early detection, access to high‐quality treatment, financial support, and ongoing surveillance and lifestyle intervention [ 14 , 62 , 63 , 64 ]. For colorectal cancer, uterine corpus cancer among women, and urinary bladder cancer among men, we also observed pronounced disparities among Black survivors. For these cancers, the plateau in survival differences after approximately 5 years suggests that early detection and timely treatment may be particularly impactful. Despite lower precisions in estimates due to relatively small numbers of AI/AN cancer survivors when stratified by cancer site, the point estimates suggested poor survival after lung and urinary bladder cancer in this population. Given the central role of cigarette smoking in the etiology and prognosis of lung and urinary bladder cancers and the high prevalence of smoking among the AI/AN population [ 65 , 66 , 67 ], these findings underscore the need for future studies on smoking cessation and tobacco‐related risk reduction, which may help improve survival among AI/AN survivors of lung and urinary bladder cancer [ 68 ]. Across all cancer sites studied, except for lung cancers, Hispanic survivors experienced persistently widening survival gaps, pointing to ongoing barriers to timely, high‐quality treatment and long‐term survivorship care. The higher survival among Asian lung and bronchus cancer survivors during the first 2 years after diagnosis aligns with evidence of a greater prevalence of targetable driver mutations, such as EGFR, and more favorable responses to targeted therapies in this population [ 60 , 69 , 70 ]. In contrast, the emergence of a survival deficit only after 5 years post‐diagnosis among Asian women diagnosed with breast cancer highlights the need for continued surveillance beyond active treatment. Taken together, these results reinforce that racial and ethnic disparities in cancer survival are neither uniform across sites nor constant over time, highlighting the need for tailored, site‐specific strategies that extend into long‐term survivorship. Though residence in higher income counties was associated with improved survival in White cancer survivors, these gains were less pronounced, nonlinear, or non‐existent among Black, Asian, AI/AN, and NHPI cancer survivors. These findings suggests that traditionally protective factors (e.g., higher socioeconomic status) may not confer the same benefits to all populations. The finding that AI/AN men in wealthier counties had worse survival than AI/AN men in lower income counties aligns with recent research showing disparate health impacts of processes such as gentrification that raise median incomes at the community level, with health indicators improving for White residents and staying the same or worsening for residents of color [ 71 ]. This highlights the potential role for policy level interventions to improve health outcomes and survival [ 14 , 17 , 71 , 72 , 73 ]. Despite significant advances in cancer care during the 21‐year study period, racial/ethnic gaps in survival persisted and, in some cases, worsened, pointing to the need for deeper investigation into the drivers and underlying mechanisms of these survival gaps. While a slight narrowing of the survival gap between White and Black cancer survivors was observed in the early 2000s, progress stagnated in the last decade. In addition, cancer survival decreased for Hispanic men in the last 5 years of the study period, a finding in line with research documenting increasing cancer incidence, diminished screening and treatment access and poorer outcomes among Hispanic populations [ 74 , 75 , 76 ]. Further research is needed to explore the specific challenges, experiences, and needs of diverse cancer survivor populations, and to develop risk‐based, tailored interventions and services for various racial and ethnic groups. Potential solutions include ensuring access to responsive, comprehensive survivorship care that addresses the complex needs of each individual patient [ 4 ]. A recent survey of accredited cancer programs found that 88% reported offering some form of survivorship services; however, the scope and reach of these services varied significantly across patient populations [ 54 , 73 ]. While a comprehensive analysis of all structural drivers is beyond the scope of this paper, the higher cancer death rates observed among Black, Asian, AI/AN, and NHPI cancer survivors underscore the need for timely delivery of high‐quality, risk‐tailored, and holistic care [ 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 22 , 72 ]. This may include access to quality treatment and reducing exposure to health‐adverse factors by promoting healthy diets, increasing physical activity, and reducing stress throughout the cancer care continuum [ 9 , 15 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 72 , 74 , 77 , 78 ]. 4.1 Strengths and Limitations Strengths include the use of large, diverse population‐based cancer registry data covering over 40% of the US population, which enabled us to generate reliable survival estimates across racial/ethnic groups, including AI/AN and NHPI populations. Data submitted to SEER undergoes rigorous quality checks and validation, enhancing internal validity. There are advantages and drawbacks to both relative and cancer‐cause survival [ 50 ]. Relative survival assumes that deaths from cancer are independent of other causes and confounding factors (e.g., smoking can cause both cancer‐ and non‐cancer‐related deaths). Cancer‐cause survival relies on death records, which may be incomplete or inaccurate [ 50 ]. Despite these limitations, both measures revealed generally similar, significant racial/ethnic differences, providing confidence in the reliability of our results. Second, we use county‐level median income, which does not fully capture other individual level socioeconomic factors (e.g., education and occupation) that may influence health outcomes. Third, our analysis used 6 mutually exclusive racial/ethnic categories, but they are not socially mutually exclusive and do not capture the full complexity of racial and ethnic identity. Many of which encompass diverse subpopulations with diverse experiences and health needs. Data were not disaggregated by sub‐group, which may miss within‐group differences. Likewise, the unique experiences of multiracial individuals were not represented within this data. Future research that includes multiracial populations and disaggregates data within these broader racial/ethnic categories will be important to better understand potential within‐group variations to develop more tailored interventions. Fourth, in situ bladder cancers were included in the analysis because they could not be reliably distinguished from invasive disease in SEER. Our sensitivity analysis showed similar racial/ethnic patterns when these cases were excluded. Finally, while this study focused on survival differences after cancer diagnosis, future longitudinal studies with assessments before and after a cancer diagnosis, and/or among those who do not develop cancer will shed more light on the potential differential impact of cancer on survival among various racial/ethnic groups. 5 Conclusion This analysis of SEER data demonstrated substantial racial/ethnic differences in survival after cancer diagnosis. These gaps persisted up to 15 years post‐diagnosis, and widened over time, even when controlled for income and stage at diagnosis. The significant differences in survival warrants further research to address causes and mechanisms that reduce survival. Effective, tailored interventions to improve survival for all individuals are needed at all stages of the cancer continuum. As the number of cancer survivors, especially cancer survivors of color, are expected to increase, risk‐stratified efforts are needed to prevent further widening of these gaps.
