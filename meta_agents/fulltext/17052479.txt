Takeaways Question: What are the epidemiological and clinicopathologic characteristics of patients with primary skin cancer treated at a tertiary hospital in Ethiopia? Findings: In this retrospective study, squamous cell carcinoma was the most common primary skin cancer (54.2%), predominantly involving the lower limbs. Basal cell carcinoma most frequently affected the head and neck, whereas dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans commonly involved the trunk. Most patients presented with advanced-stage disease. Meaning: Primary cutaneous malignancies in Ethiopia commonly present late, with squamous cell carcinoma being the predominant tumor type. INTRODUCTION The skin, a dynamic and complex organ comprising approximately 8% of total body weight with a surface area of 1.2–2.2 m 2 , serves as a protective barrier against environmental hazards, pathogens, ultraviolet (UV) radiation, and harmful substances, while also providing sensory input, immune function, and vitamin synthesis. 1 – 3 It influences physical appearance through pigmentation and aging, supports wound healing through its regenerative capacity, and is structurally organized into 3 main layers: epidermis, dermis, and hypodermis. 1 – 3 The epidermis, a renewing outer layer, contains keratinocytes, melanocytes, Langerhans cells, and Merkel cells and gives rise to skin appendages. The dermis, a fibrous layer rich in collagen, glycosaminoglycans, and elastin, imparts strength and elasticity. The innermost hypodermis insulates and houses major blood vessels and nerves. 1 – 3 Primary cutaneous malignancies arise from various skin cell types and are the most common malignant neoplasms worldwide. Their incidence doubles every 15–20 years, with a markedly higher prevalence among individuals with lighter Fitzpatrick skin types (I–III) than among those with darker types (IV–VI). 4 , 5 Although the incidence and prevalence of skin cancer have increased in recent decades, the exact number remains unknown. In contrast, the mortality rate from cutaneous malignancies has shown a stable or decreasing trend. 6 , 7 According to the GLOBOCAN 2018 database, the estimated incidence of nonmelanoma skin cancers was about 1 million (6%), with around 100,000 deaths worldwide. 8 According to statistics from the World Health Organization and the Skin Cancer Foundation, an estimated 2–3 million nonmelanoma skin cancers and new cases of 132,000 malignant melanoma (MM) occur globally each year. One in 3 cancers diagnosed is a skin cancer, and 1 in every 5 Americans develops skin cancer in their lifetime. 9 Traditionally, primary cutaneous malignancy is considered a rare occurrence among Black populations, and its exact magnitude remains poorly defined. 10 , 11 Nonmelanoma skin cancers constitute the majority of cutaneous malignancies worldwide. 12 The Fitzpatrick skin phototype classification has historically been used to categorize skin types based on their response to UV exposure. However, more recent genetic insights have led to the proposal of a complementary system: the genetic classification for skin types proposed by Helmy, 13 which emphasizes the evolutionary and genomic basis of pigmentation. In this model, darker skin tones (types I–III) represent the ancestral human phenotype, whereas lighter tones (types IV–VI) result from later genetic adaptations to lower UV environments. 13 This paradigm provides a biologically grounded context for understanding ethnic and geographic variations in skin cancer epidemiology, complementing the classical phototype-based framework. In Ethiopia, information on the burden, histopathologic types, and clinical stages of presentation remains limited. This study therefore aims to describe the epidemiological and clinicopathologic features of primary cutaneous malignancies diagnosed at the All-African Leprosy Rehabilitation and Training Center (ALERT) Hospital in Addis Ababa, providing baseline data for future multicenter research and policy planning. PATIENTS AND METHODS This was a single-institution retrospective chart review conducted at the ALERT Hospital, a tertiary referral center in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, from January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2018. The study included all patients with histologically confirmed primary cutaneous malignancies diagnosed during the study period. Patients with secondary or metastatic skin lesions, incomplete medical records, or uncertain histopathologic diagnoses were excluded. A structured data abstraction form was used to collect demographic and clinical data, including age, sex, anatomical site, histological type, duration of illness, and clinical stage. Fitzpatrick skin type was not routinely documented in patient records and therefore could not be reliably analyzed. The study population primarily consisted of native Ethiopian patients, who are predominantly dark-skinned. Data completeness was verified by 2 independent reviewers, and discrepancies were resolved through consensus to minimize bias. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 27. Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and disease characteristics. Associations between categorical variables were tested using χ 2 tests, with a P value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. Because this was a descriptive study, no power calculation was performed. Institutional review board approval was obtained from Addis Ababa University before the chart review. Patient identifiers were omitted to ensure confidentiality. RESULTS A total of 310 patients with primary skin cancer were identified during the study period. DISCUSSION This study shows that SCC is the predominant primary cutaneous malignancy in Ethiopia, followed by BCC and MM. The predominance of SCC contrasts with patterns observed in lighter-skinned populations, where BCC is more common. 9 , 11 This difference may reflect variations in melanin-mediated photoprotection, environmental exposures, and healthcare-seeking behaviors. The predominance of lower limb involvement and the late clinical stage at presentation highlight delayed recognition and limited access to early diagnostic services. Increased UV exposure in equatorial regions has long been recognized as a major risk factor for skin cancer. 14 – 16 Nevertheless, many SCCs in African populations arise in non–sun-exposed sites and are associated with chronic inflammation, scars, or longstanding wounds rather than UV exposure alone. The high proportion of advanced-stage disease in this cohort (70%) likely reflects both diagnostic delay and restricted access to dermatologic and oncological care. 10 , 11 , 17 BCC, in particular, is frequently diagnosed late because of its indolent growth and minimal early symptoms. 18 These findings are consistent with previous reports from sub-Saharan Africa, where late presentation remains a major challenge. 10 From a biological standpoint, genetic factors related to skin pigmentation and photoprotection may also influence disease distribution. The Helmy 13 genetic classification of skin types proposes a genotype-based framework that complements the classical Fitzpatrick model. In this classification, darker skin tones (Helmy types I–III) represent the ancestral human phenotype, characterized by higher melanin content and robust UV protection. Although this protection reduces the incidence of UV-induced DNA mutations, it may also obscure the early clinical detection of malignant lesions due to pigmentation masking. Incorporating both the Fitzpatrick and Helmy frameworks provides a broader understanding of the interplay between genetic, environmental, and behavioral factors shaping the skin cancer burden in African populations. The finding that DFSP accounted for nearly half of recurrent tumors aligns with its well-known locally aggressive behavior and high recurrence risk after inadequate excision. 19 , 20 Recurrence within 2 years of primary treatment underscores the need for wide local excision or Mohs micrographic surgery and multidisciplinary follow-up. 19 – 21 Inadequate surgical margins remain a major contributor to local recurrence. 22 Patients with DFSP also report substantial diagnostic delays and psychosocial burden related to repeated surgical interventions. 23 Improving local access to specialized surgical and pathology services could significantly reduce recurrence and associated morbidity. In this study, margin status and details of re-excision were not consistently documented, limiting our ability to assess their contribution to recurrence rates. STUDY STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS This study is one of the few comprehensive analyses of histologically confirmed primary cutaneous malignancies in Ethiopia. The relatively large cohort size and detailed clinicopathologic data provide valuable insight into disease patterns. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective single-center design introduces potential selection bias, as ALERT Hospital functions as a tertiary referral center that likely receives more advanced and complex cases. Second, incomplete documentation and limited dermatopathology infrastructure may have affected data completeness and diagnostic precision. Third, margin status and details of re-excision were not consistently documented, limiting the assessment of their impact on recurrence. Finally, follow-up duration varied among patients, which may have led to an underestimation of late recurrences. Despite these limitations, the study establishes an important baseline for future multicenter and prospective research. CONCLUSIONS This study shows that SCC is the most common primary cutaneous malignancy in Ethiopia, with a predominance in the lower limbs and frequent late-stage presentation. BCC and MM were less common but also presented at advanced stages. DFSP was the leading recurrent tumor, highlighting the importance of surgical margin control and long-term follow-up. These findings suggest that public awareness, early detection, and access to dermatologic and oncological care must be strengthened. The integration of genetic frameworks, such as the Helmy skin type classification, into future research could enhance understanding of risk patterns among African populations. Although limited by its retrospective, single-institution nature, this study provides essential baseline data to inform larger multicenter studies aimed at improving skin cancer outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa.
