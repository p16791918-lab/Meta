Introduction Dermatofibrosarcoma protuberans (DFSP) is a rare, locally aggressive low-grade dermal based cutaneous neoplasm with an estimated incidence of 4.2 cases per million people annually [ 1 ]. While classic DFSP (C-DFSP) typically demonstrates excellent prognosis with complete surgical resection, local recurrence rates of 13.7%, and low metastatic potential (1.1%), the clinical landscape changes dramatically when fibrosarcomatous transformation occurs [ 2 ]. Fibrosarcomatous DFSP (FS-DFSP) represents a more aggressive variant occurring in 5–15% of classic cases, characterized by significantly elevated rates of local recurrence (29.8%), distant metastasis (14.4%), and mortality (14.7%) compared to its classic counterpart [ 2 ]. The pathogenesis of DFSP involves the characteristic COL1A1-platelet-derived growth factor B (PDGFB) fusion gene, resulting from the translocation t(17;22)(q22;q13) in approximately 90–95% of cases [ 3 ]. This chromosomal rearrangement leads to overexpression of PDGFB, which drives tumor growth through autocrine stimulation of PDGF receptors [ 4 ]. The fibrosarcomatous transformation represents a progression toward a higher-grade tumor, often accompanied by loss of CD34 expression and increased mitotic activity ( 10/10 per high power field), making accurate histopathological diagnosis crucial for appropriate treatment planning [ 3 , 4 ]. Myxoid differentiation in FS-DFSP represents an even rarer variant, with limited cases reported in the literature. This histological subtype presents unique diagnostic challenges due to altered morphological features and variable immunohistochemical staining patterns [ 5 ]. The clinical implications of myxoid differentiation in FS-DFSP remain incompletely understood, particularly regarding treatment response and long-term prognosis. This report presents a comprehensive case of giant myxoid FS-DFSP with complex anatomical involvement, including compression of the right jugular vein, requiring multidisciplinary management with surgical resection, reconstructive procedures, and adjuvant radiation therapy. We discuss the diagnostic approach, treatment challenges, and 4-year follow-up outcomes to contribute to the limited literature on this rare variant. Case Presentation A 56-year-old female presented to our dermatology clinic with a progressively enlarging chest mass that had been present for approximately 19 years. The patient’s medical history was significant for an initial diagnosis of “fibroma” made 19 years prior, which was treated with surgical excision at an outside facility. However, the lesion recurred approximately 12 years ago and demonstrated progressive enlargement over the subsequent years, prompting the current evaluation. The patient’s primary concern was the cosmetic disfigurement and functional limitations imposed by the massive chest wall lesion. On physical examination, an irregular, exophytic, partially ulcerated mass measuring approximately 15 × 12 × 6 cm was observed on the right anterior chest wall, extending from the clavicular region toward the upper chest ( Fig. 1 a). Advanced imaging studies were performed to assess the extent of local invasion and rule out distant metastases. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed an 8 × 13 × 12 cm poorly defined soft tissue mass involving the pectoralis major muscle and extending superiorly to the lower margin of the sternocleidomastoid muscles. Regional lymph node examination of the cervical, axillary, and supraclavicular regions was unremarkable, with no palpable lymphadenopathy. Fig. 1. Clinical images. a Giant mass on the right anterior clavicle and neck. b At the 4-year follow-up, no evidence of recurrence was observed. Comprehensive laboratory evaluation including complete blood count, comprehensive metabolic panel, liver function tests, and inflammatory markers was within normal limits. Autoimmune antibody screening and tumor markers, including CEA, CA 19-9, and LDH, were unremarkable, ruling out systemic inflammatory conditions and metastatic disease. Given the size of the tumor, the possibility of thoracic invasion could not be ruled out during the preoperative discussion. Therefore, ultrasound examination was first performed to dynamically display the relationship between the tumor and the pleura in real time, including assessment of changes during respiratory movement to evaluate for potential chest wall involvement. Biopsy was performed under ultrasound guidance to establish a definitive histopathological diagnosis. Histopathological examination revealed a cellular spindle cell proliferation arranged in a characteristic fascicular pattern, consistent with fibrosarcomatous transformation, with subtle patchy myxoid changes ( Fig. 2 a, hematoxylin and eosin staining). Fig. 2. Investigations of chest mass. a Hematoxylin and eosin (H E) staining showing cellular proliferation of the dermis and subcutis by spindle cells arranged in a fascicular pattern with high mitotic activity and patchy areas of myxoid changes. b Immunohistochemical CD34 staining demonstrating strong positivity. c EMA immunohistochemical staining demonstrating positivity. Comprehensive immunohistochemical analysis was performed to confirm the diagnosis and rule out other spindle cell neoplasms. The tumor cells demonstrated strong positive staining for CD34 ( Fig. 2 b), epithelial membrane antigen ( Fig. 2 c), and smooth muscle actin, arranged in a characteristic nodular pattern. Staining for desmin, S100 protein, and STAT-6 was negative, helping to differentiate this lesion from other soft tissue sarcomas, melanoma, and solitary fibrous tumors. Other neoplasms with myxoid changes have been considered, including low grade fibromyxoid sarcoma, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor, and leiomyosarcoma. The combination of morphological features and immunohistochemical profile supported the diagnosis of FS-DFSP with myxoid differentiation. Molecular analysis using COL1A1 fluorescence in situ hybridization was performed to detect the characteristic chromosomal translocation ( Fig. 3 ). Analysis of tumor cells revealed COL1A1/PDGFB fusion in 8% of examined cells, confirming the diagnosis of DFSP. Overall, 23% of cells lacked detectable fusion signals, likely reflecting tumor heterogeneity and the challenges associated with detecting translocations in myxoid variants of FS-DFSP. The final histopathological diagnosis was FS-DFSP with myxoid differentiation. Given the complex anatomical location, significant tumor burden, and involvement of critical vascular structures, a multidisciplinary team approach was employed for treatment planning. Fig. 3. COL1A1-FISH analysis demonstrating COL1A1/PDGFB fusion. Red fluorescence signals represent the COL1A1 probe, green fluorescence signals represent the PDGFB probe, and yellow/overlapping signals indicate COL1A1/PDGFB fusion events, confirming the diagnosis of DFSP. In DFSP, the COL1A1-PDGFB fusion characteristically arises through a supernumerary ring chromosome derived from t(17;22), rather than a simple balanced translocation. As a result, the FISH signal pattern may differ from that seen with conventional gene fusions and may not display classic split signals; this is an inherent feature of the ring chromosome mechanism and not a technical limitation of the assay. Preoperative MRI ( Fig. 4 a–d) demonstrated compression of the proximal right jugular vein ( Fig. 4 a, coronal plane), poorly defined tumor margins with the pectoralis major muscle ( Fig. 4 b, axial plane), and extensive tumor involvement of the sternocleidomastoid muscle ( Fig. 4 c, d, axial and sagittal planes). These anatomical features guided preoperative planning and multidisciplinary coordination to achieve optimal surgical outcomes while preserving critical neurovascular structures. Fig. 4. Preoperative MRI demonstrating surgical complexity. a Coronal plane: compression of the proximal right jugular vein (arrow). b Axial plane: poorly defined tumor margins involving the pectoralis major muscle (arrow). c Axial plane: extensive tumor involvement and compression of the sternocleidomastoid muscle (arrow). d Sagittal plane: extent of sternocleidomastoid muscle compression shown across two levels (arrows). Arrows have been enhanced for visibility. Clinically relevant adjacent structures include the pectoralis major muscle and the sternocleidomastoid muscle. Treatment and Follow-Up Preoperative planning included tumor vascular embolization performed to reduce intraoperative bleeding risk and facilitate complete resection. Surgical resection was performed. Wide local excision was achieved with surgical margins of 2.5 cm in all directions where anatomically feasible. The resection included the overlying skin, subcutaneous tissue, and involved portions of the pectoralis major muscle. Due to the complicated anatomical location and proximity to vital structures including the clavicle and great vessels, complete resection with adequate margins proved challenging in certain areas, particularly the anterior sternum region where microscopic residual tumor was suspected. Immediate reconstruction was performed using a combination of split-thickness skin grafting and local tissue rearrangement. The surgical procedure was completed without complications, and the patient tolerated the procedure well. Final histopathological examination of the surgical specimen confirmed the preoperative diagnosis of FS-DFSP with myxoid differentiation. Margin analysis revealed clear margins in most areas; however, close margins were identified in the region adjacent to the anterior sternum, where complete resection was limited by anatomical constraints. Given the presence of close surgical margins and the aggressive nature of FS-DFSP, adjuvant radiation therapy was recommended by the multidisciplinary team. Adjuvant radiation therapy was initiated postoperatively following adequate wound healing and graft incorporation. The prescribed regimen consisted of 240 cGy per fraction delivered over 25 fractions, for a total dose of 6,000 cGy. Comprehensive follow-up surveillance was implemented according to current guidelines for FS-DFSP management. European guidelines suggest follow-up every 6 months for 5 years and annual examinations for up to 10 years, with imaging in FS-DFSP cases. At 4 years post-treatment, the patient demonstrates excellent clinical outcomes with no evidence of local recurrence or distant metastasis ( Fig. 1 b). Serial MRI studies have remained normal, showing complete resolution of the original mass and no signs of recurrent disease. The patient reports excellent functional outcomes and satisfaction with cosmetic results. Discussion This case illustrates several important aspects of myxoid FS-DFSP management, including the diagnostic challenges, treatment complexity, and excellent long-term outcomes achievable with aggressive multimodal therapy. The myxoid variant of FS-DFSP represents an exceedingly rare subset of an already uncommon malignancy, with limited cases reported in the literature [ 6 ], making this case a valuable contribution to the existing knowledge base. The differential diagnosis of large chest wall masses encompasses a broad spectrum of benign and malignant entities, including atypical fibrous histiocytoma, malignant fibrous histiocytoma, synovial sarcoma, and other CD34-positive spindle cell tumors. Distinguishing FS-DFSP from these entities requires comprehensive histopathological evaluation, including specialized immunohistochemical markers such as STAT6, which can provide definitive differentiation from morphologically similar lesions [ 3 , 4 ]. In our case, the combination of characteristic morphology, immunohistochemical profile, and molecular confirmation with COL1A1-fluorescence in situ hybridization analysis provided definitive diagnosis. The preoperative MRI findings ( Fig. 4 a–d), described in the Case Report section, further illustrate the surgical complexity imposed by the tumor’s anatomical location. The histological features of myxoid FS-DFSP include infiltrative growth patterns, intermediate grade spindle cells arranged in the characteristic fascicular pattern with zones of increased cellularity, and significantly increased mitotic activity as demonstrated by elevated Ki-67 expression [ 3 , 4 ]. Presence of classic histologic features of DFSP can help with the diagnosis. CD34 expression is often reduced or absent in fibrosarcomatous and myxoid regions, despite its characteristic positivity in C-DFSP, which can complicate the diagnostic process and requires correlation with other immunohistochemical markers and molecular studies [ 3 , 4 ]. The presence of myxoid changes also highlight this unique variant of FS-DSFP and careful histologic examination with appropriate stains is needed to rule out other tumor with myxoid changes including solitary fibrous tumor, low-grade fibromyxoid sarcoma, malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor, and leiomyosarcoma. Adequate sampling of the identification of more typical areas of DFSP is crucial for diagnosis (new reference of Myxoid Dermatofibrosarcoma Protuberans: A Rare Variant Analyzed in a Series of 23 Cases). The treatment approach for FS-DFSP requires aggressive surgical management with wide local excision and margins greater than 2 cm when anatomically feasible [ 4 ]. However, in anatomically challenging locations such as the head, neck, and chest wall, achieving adequate surgical margins may be limited by the proximity to vital structures, as demonstrated in our case. In situations where complete resection with adequate margins is not achievable, adjuvant radiation therapy is strongly recommended to reduce the risk of local recurrence [ 4 ]. Long-term surveillance protocols for FS-DFSP patients require more intensive monitoring compared to C-DFSP due to the elevated risk of local recurrence and distant metastasis. Current European guidelines recommend clinical examinations every 6 months for the first 5 years, followed by annual examinations for up to 10 years, with routine imaging surveillance for FS-DFSP cases [ 5 ]. A 4-year follow-up without evidence of recurrence in our patient is encouraging but requires continued vigilance given the potential for late recurrences in FS-DFSP. This case emphasizes the critical importance of thorough tissue sampling to capture areas of C-DFSP along with myxoid and fibrosarcomatous changes, comprehensive immunohistochemical evaluation, and molecular characterization for accurate diagnosis of myxoid FS-DFSP variants. The successful management achieved through multidisciplinary collaboration, aggressive surgical resection, appropriate reconstruction, and adjuvant radiation therapy demonstrates the potential for excellent long-term outcomes even in complex anatomical locations. Future research should focus on identifying prognostic factors specific to myxoid variants and optimizing treatment protocols for this rare subset of FS-DFSP patients.
