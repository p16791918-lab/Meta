Introduction In 2022, breast cancer emerged as the second most frequently diagnosed malignant cancer worldwide and the fourth leading cause of cancer mortality across all genders. [ 1 ] Notably, it holds the highest rank for both incidence and mortality among women. The disease claimed the lives of 666,103 individuals, accounting for 6.8% of all cancer-related deaths and 15.4% of deaths among female patients with cancer. [ 1 ] This statistic demonstrates that approximately three out of every 20 women with cancer succumbed to breast cancer, highlighting its profound impact on female oncology. [ 2 ] This has imposed a significant burden on healthcare systems globally. Numerous lifestyle and reproductive factors have been identified as risk factors for breast cancer. Among these, modifiable risk factors are as follows: Obesity, alcohol intake, tobacco use, a high-protein diet, and a short duration of breastfeeding, whereas an early age at menarche is classified as a non-modifiable risk factor. [ 3 – 8 ] However, a further minor proportion of breast cancer incidence and progression can be attributed to genetic mutations, such as the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes. [ 9 ] There is a correlation between the prevalence and fatality rates of breast cancer and the level of Human Development Index (HDI) in the respective countries. Nations characterized by a low to medium HDI tend to exhibit elevated rates of both incidence and mortality in relation to breast cancer. [ 10 ] The World Health Organization (WHO) has introduced the Global Breast Cancer Initiative (GBCI), established in 2021, to address the increasing prevalence of breast tumors. The primary objective of this initiative is to decrease mortality rates, particularly in transitioning countries, by ensuring widespread availability of early detection services. [ 11 ] It would be advantageous to comprehend the present worldwide burden of breast cancer to facilitate the advancement and execution of this initiative. To enhance comprehension of breast cancer prevalence on a global, national, and regional scale, we have conducted an analysis of data sourced from the Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) 2022. This study examines the incidence and mortality rates of breast cancer in 185 countries and regions worldwide in 2022, explores the correlation between incidence and mortality rates and the economic development status of these areas, and projects the future trends of breast cancer incidence and mortality up to 2050 based on the global population projections. The primary objective of this research is to provide valuable insights for governments and policymakers to formulate evidence-based strategies for breast cancer prevention and management. Methods Ethics approval This study does not involve any animal or human experimental models. Hence, ethical approval is not required. Data sources Data on new cases and deaths from breast cancer (International Classification of Diseases, tenth revision [ICD-10] code C50) were sourced from the GLOBOCAN 2022 database, managed by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC). GLOBOCAN provides comprehensive estimates for 36 types of cancers, including incidence, mortality, and prevalence (1-, 3-, and 5-years) across 185 countries or territories, segmented by sex and 18 distinct age groups (0–4, 5–9, …, 80–84, 85 years and over). In our analysis, we aggregated the age data into two broad categories: 0–49 and ≥50–85 years, aligning these groups with premenopausal and postmenopausal stages, respectively. This stratification facilitates targeted insights into breast cancer dynamics across different life stages. In addition, demographic data for 2022 were accessed through the United Nations (UN) website. The methodologies and hierarchical processes for compiling these cancer estimates have been extensively detailed in earlier publications. [ 12 – 14 ] The data of breast cancer obtained from GLOBOCAN were estimated; however, the validity of this estimate depended on the degree of representativeness and quality of the source in the national cancer registries. Thus, 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) for the number of incidences and mortality were provided. The results of this article are presented in a variety of formats, disaggregated by 185 countries and 21 subregions (defined by the UN). HDI, established by the UN Development Programme, serves as a comprehensive metric for assessing national development. This composite index integrates indicators such as life expectancy, mean years of schooling, expected years of schooling, and gross national income per capita. [ 15 ] HDI was stratified into four tiers ranging from a minimum of 0 to a maximum of 1: Low HDI ( 0.550), medium HDI (0.550– 0.700), high HDI (0.700–≤0.800), and very high HDI ( 0.800). For clarity, we used “transitioning”, “emerging”, and “lower HDI” to denote countries with low or medium HDI, whereas “transitioned” and “higher HDI” referred to countries with high or very high HDI. Our analyses employed tabular data and graphical visualizations to represent new cases and mortality rates, alongside age-standardized incidence rates (ASIRs) and age-standardized mortality rates (ASMRs) per 100,000 individuals. The world standard population used for these calculations followed the Segi population standard, with modifications from Doll and Cook. [ 16 ] Age-standardization was applied to adjust for differences in age distribution across populations, facilitating accurate comparisons of morbidity and mortality rates globally. [ 16 , 17 ] Menopausal status was inferred based on age at diagnosis using established thresholds: Women aged 50 years were classified as premenopausal, and those aged ≥50 years as postmenopausal. This approach aligns with the GLOBOCAN age groupings for breast cancer epidemiology, where premenopausal incidence and mortality are defined in women aged under 50 years, and postmenopausal in those aged 50 years and above. The classification accounts for the global average age of natural menopause (45–55 years) and addresses the lack of direct menopausal status documentation in cancer registries. [ 12 ] The 5-year breast cancer survival rate was estimated using the country-level mortality-to-incidence ratio (MIR), defined as the ratio of the total number of breast cancer deaths to the total number of incident cases across all ages. [ 18 ] The period incidence ratio (PIR) was defined as the ratio of the 5-year prevalence of breast cancer to the number of new cases across all ages, whereas the period mortality ratio (PMR) was defined as the ratio of the 5-year prevalence to the total number of deaths across all ages. These epidemiological metrics such as PIR, PMR, and MIR offer insights into the efficacy of health systems and the quality of oncological care across different countries. [ 19 ] Statistical analysis For the countries or territories with available HDI data, we analyzed the relationships between age-standardized rates (ASRs), MIR, PIR, and PMR for breast cancer and the HDI using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (Spearman’s ρ). This approach assesses the strength of association by utilizing the ranks of variables, thereby avoiding reliance on linearity assumptions. It is appropriate for examining monotonic relationships and demonstrates robustness against the influence of outliers. All correlation analyses were conducted as two-tailed tests, with the significance threshold established at P 0.05. The magnitude of the Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was employed to determine the association strength, categorized into five levels: no correlation (0– 0.20), weak correlation (0.20– 0.40), moderate correlation (0.40– 0.60), strong correlation (0.60–0.79), and very strong correlation (0.80–1.00). Identical interpretive thresholds were applied to both positive and negative coefficients. Using demographic projections, we examined the anticipated number of new breast cancer cases and deaths from 2022 to 2050. By using 2022 as the baseline year, we analyzed potential increases or decreases in case and death rates to visually illustrate the impact of various interventions on future epidemiological trends in breast cancer. Results Breast cancer incidence, mortality, and ASR in 2022 worldwide The GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates reveal that breast cancer will account for approximately 2.30 million new cases and 666,103 deaths globally, representing 11.5% of all new malignancies and 6.8% of cancer-related deaths across all genders [Table 1 ]. Among women, these proportions are predominantly higher, with breast cancer constituting 23.8% (2,296,840/9,664,889) of new cases and 15.4% (666,103/4,313,548) of deaths. Out of the 185 countries and territories assessed, breast cancer ranks as the most prevalent malignant tumor in 82 of these locations for all sexes, and its prominence increased to 158 countries and territories when considering only females. In addition, it is the leading cause of cancer mortality in 28 countries and territories across all sexes and in 111 countries and territories specifically among women. These statistics elucidate the widespread impact of breast cancer globally, highlighting its significant burden on both incidence and mortality. Mortality rates followed a similar pattern, with South Central Asia experiencing the highest number of deaths at 135,348 (95% CI: 133,994–136,715), and Micronesia the lowest, with only 53 (95% CI: 31–92) deaths. ASIRs were highest in the Australia–New Zealand region at 100.3 per 100,000 individuals, followed by Northern America (95.1/100,000) and Northern Europe (90.8/100,000). Conversely, South Central Asia had the lowest ASIR at 26.7 per 100,000 individuals, followed by Middle Africa (30.0/100,000) and Eastern Africa (31.9/100,000). Notably, the ranking of ASIR across different HDI subgroups did not directly align with the HDI categories as very high, high, low, and medium. ASMRs were the highest in Melanesia at 26.8 per 100,000 individuals, followed by Polynesia (25.3/100,000) and Western Africa (22.3/100,000), whereas Eastern Asia exhibited the lowest ASMR at 6.5 per 100,000 individuals, with Central America (10.1/100,000) and Northern America (12.3/100,000) also reporting low rates. MIR globally stood at 0.29, with the highest values recorded in Middle Africa and Eastern Africa, both at 0.54, and the lowest in Northern America and Australia–New Zealand at 0.16. The MIR ranking followed the HDI order, which was consistent from low to high HDI [Table 1 ]. Eastern Asia had the largest number of women living with breast cancer within 5 years, with approximately 1.68 million affected. In contrast, Western Europe, characterized by a very high HDI, exhibited the highest 5-year prevalence rate, at 761.3 per 100,000 individuals, whereas Middle Africa reported the lowest rate at 39.7 per 100,000 individuals [Table 1 ]. PIR across regions varied nearly threefold, with Australia–New Zealand having the highest and Micronesia the lowest values. The PIR and PMR rankings were consistent with HDI, maintaining the order from the highest to the lowest HDI. Projections of breast cancer burden by 2050 Assuming current trends persist and in the absence of effective interventions, the global number of new breast cancer cases is projected to increase from approximately 2.30 million in 2022 to 3.55 million by 2050 (1.55-fold increase), while deaths are expected to rise from approximately 0.66 million to 1.14 million (1.71-fold increase) [Figure 5 ]. The most significant rise is anticipated in low HDI countries, with an estimated increase of 327.28%, although very high HDI countries are projected to see a doubling of cases [Figure 6 ]. Low HDI countries will experience the most substantial increase in deaths, estimated at 335.17%. Countries with high and medium HDI levels are expected to reach similar absolute numbers of deaths by 2050 (0.539 vs . 0.542 million), with medium HDI countries experiencing a notable percentage rise of 252.60%. Maintaining current mortality levels would necessitate an approximate 2% annual decrease in mortality rates. The majority of future cases and deaths are projected to occur in very high HDI countries, with an increase from about 1.09 million cases and 245,043 deaths in 2022 to 2.28 million cases and 598,602 deaths in 2050 [Figure 6 ]. Discussion According to the latest GLOBOCAN 2022 data analysis, the number of breast cancer deaths, ASIR, ASMR, and PIR indicators show significant regional disparities among countries and territories. The ordering of ASIR and ASMR exhibited considerable variation across regions, ranging from developed regions, including Australia–New Zealand, Northern America, and Northern Europe to developing regions, such as Western Africa, Middle Africa, and Eastern Africa. The three African regions referenced above are in the lowest quartile of ASIR’s regional rankings, but appear among the highest ASMR rankings. The considerable discrepancies observed in the rankings of Australia–New Zealand, Northern America and Northern Europe are due to the opposite pattern seen in these regions: They have high ASIR but low ASMR, in contrast to the three African regions. Although they rank within the top four regions in ASIR, they fall into the lowest group of regions for ASMR. It is notable that all these regions encompass countries and territories with very high HDI levels. The prevalence of Western dietary patterns and the ideology of late marriage and childbearing, which results in prolonged estrogenic hormone stimulation of the mammary glands, also contributes to the increase in morbidity. There is also a pronounced focus on postmenopausal quality of life, leading to widespread use of menopausal hormone replacement therapy (HRT) aimed at mitigating menopausal symptoms and reducing cardiovascular risk. [ 20 ] The increased life expectancy resulting from higher levels of healthcare has also led to increased morbidity of malignant tumors. It has been demonstrated that the probability of developing cancer increases in conjunction with rising HDI levels. [ 21 ] Individuals living in countries and territories with very high HDI have a greater life expectancy, which consequently elevates their risk of developing cancer. [ 22 ] The majority of deaths among those individuals residing in low HDI countries are attributed to causes other than cancer, such as acute infectious diseases. The discrepancy between ASIR and ASMR observed in countries with very high HDI levels can be attributed to a number of factors, including the availability of advanced treatment options, early diagnosis across a wide range of age groups, enhanced cancer prognosis care, and the implementation of mammography screening programs. [ 23 ] In the United States, approximately 14% of women aged 18–39 years, who are considered at an average risk for breast cancer, participated in mammography screening programs between 2011 and 2015. [ 24 ] In high-income Asian countries, such as Japan and South Korea, mammography screening is recommended to begin at age of 40 years. Countries with very high HDI levels are well-positioned to implement comprehensive precancer screening programs, which are instrumental in detecting tumors at an early, more manageable stage. This early detection is crucial as it significantly improves prognosis by reducing the incidence of advanced and metastatic breast cancers, leading to lower mortality rates and improved overall survival outcomes. Advanced age is an established risk factor for cancer. [ 25 ] Countries characterized by higher HDI levels typically exhibit more pronounced aging populations, which contributes to the elevated cancer incidence rates observed in these high HDI countries. An elevated level of education is associated with an increased probability of developing breast cancer. [ 26 ] Nonetheless, education itself does not directly elevate breast cancer risk; instead, it affects intermediary variables that mediate this relationship. For instance, women with higher educational attainment often experience improved socioeconomic status but also greater social and occupational stress, which may contribute to increased alcohol consumption. In addition, highly educated women tend to delay childbirth and have prolonged menstrual cycles, contributing to a later onset of menopause. Furthermore, this demographic is more likely to use HRT during menopause. These factors collectively serve as mediators through which education indirectly influences breast cancer risk. [ 26 ] The strong positive correlations observed between PIR, PMR, and HDI describe the relationship between economic development and cancer care quality. High HDI countries are equipped with advanced healthcare infrastructure and resources, which enable more effective cancer treatment and management. This correlation reflects not only the availability of early detection technologies and treatments but also the ability of these regions to offer supportive care that contributes to higher survival rates. Consequently, countries with higher HDI levels have lower breast cancer mortality and improved patient outcomes, reflecting their ability to provide quality healthcare and early interventions. For countries with low HDI, limited healthcare investment often leads to underdiagnosis and delayed treatment of breast cancer, resulting in low ASIRs but elevated ASMRs. On the contrary, it is related to cancer registries, and IARC elucidates that countries with low and medium HDI levels still have a lot of room to develop population-based cancer registries. [ 27 ] Sudden or prolonged sociopolitical and economic instability may prevent the establishment or maintenance of high-quality cancer registries in these areas. [ 28 ] Insufficient funding may also lead to relatively high levels of deaths from competing causes other than cancer, such as acute infectious diseases, that will kill people from noncancer diseases. [ 21 ] All of these factors contribute to a relatively low ASIR. In addition, in these areas, the insufficient supply of treatment medications, inconvenient transportation to and from healthcare service centers, limited treatment options, inability to afford treatment costs, inadequate coverage of healthcare service centers, as well as local cultural, social, and religious customs, may all become barriers for female patients in accessing healthcare services. [ 19 , 29 ] A summary of Centers for Disease Control and Prevention report on breast cancer screening indicates that women with low incomes and education levels are highly unlikely to elect to undergo a mammography screening program. [ 30 ] According to previous reports, food insecurity also affects compliance with mammography. People affected by food insecurity are not only likely to experience anxiety, depression, and psychological stress, but they are also more likely to fill up on nutritionally poor foods, thereby increasing their risk factors for breast cancer. Such individuals do not prioritize screening as a preferred option. [ 31 ] The lack of breast cancer screening means that patients seek medical attention only after experiencing significant clinical symptoms, by which time the disease is often at an advanced stage. Research shows that many women in Africa seek help from traditional healers after symptoms appear, and these traditional healers promise not to use painful surgery and drug during treatment, further delaying treatment and leading to a poor prognosis. [ 18 ] Among the 10 regions with the highest breast cancer mortality rates, five are located in Africa. Empirical studies indicate that the average prevalence of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) in Africa is approximately 27%, with West Africa exhibiting an especially high proportion, reaching up to 45.7%, nearly half of all breast cancer cases in that area. [ 32 ] TNBC is recognized as the most aggressive breast cancer subtype and is associated with the poorest prognosis among all pathological classifications. This subtype distribution provides a plausible explanation for the elevated mortality rates observed in these African regions. The various factors mentioned above can lead to a high ASMR in low HDI countries. Menopausal status constitutes a critical determinant in the therapeutic management of patients with breast cancer, significantly guiding the selection of endocrine therapies, chemotherapeutic regimens, and targeted agents. [ 33 ] Treatment strategies should incorporate a comprehensive evaluation of menopausal status, comorbid conditions such as osteoporosis, and individual patient tolerance. In the context of endocrine therapy, tamoxifen (TAM) monotherapy remains the standard of care for premenopausal patients. However, for those classified as high risk, the combination of ovarian function suppression (OFS) with aromatase inhibitors is recommended, as this approach has demonstrated superior efficacy compared to OFS combined with TAM. Among postmenopausal patients, aromatase inhibitors are the preferred therapeutic option, particularly within high-risk cohorts. This preference is supported not only by the primary indication of aromatase inhibitors in this population, but also by evidence indicating a significant reduction in recurrence risk (from 14.4% to 12.6%) without an associated increase in endometrial cancer incidence. Nonetheless, vigilant monitoring of bone mineral density is imperative, alongside supplementation with calcium and vitamin D2 to mitigate potential adverse effects. [ 34 ] Furthermore, in premenopausal patients, particular consideration should be afforded to the implementation of OFS, the preservation of fertility, and the adoption of intensified systemic treatment protocols for aggressive breast cancer subtypes, including those characterized by low hormone receptor (HR) expression (HR-low) or TNBC. [ 33 ] With economic development and changes in dietary patterns, such as the consumption of high-fat and high-sugar foods, the proportion of obese people has increased worldwide, with 15% of adults classified as obese in most countries. [ 35 ] Obesity is already a recognized risk factor for the development and progression of breast cancer. [ 36 ] Being overweight or obese has been confirmed to increase the likelihood of developing HR-positive and luminal A-like subtypes of breast cancer. Existing studies have shown that the luminal A-like subtype of breast cancer accounts for the vast majority of postmenopausal breast cancer cases. [ 37 ] Postmenopausal women experience a decline in ovarian function, leading to a decrease in estrogen secretion, which can even reach zero. This results in a slowed metabolism, making it easier for fat to accumulate in the abdominal area. The increase in abdominal obesity and body mass index after menopause raises the risk of developing breast cancer. [ 12 ] This explains from different perspective why the number of new breast cancer cases aged 50 years and above in countries with very high HDI, high HDI, and medium HDI is several times that of those aged 49 years and below. In countries and territories with low HDI levels, the ratio of new breast cancer cases among individuals under 50 years old to those aged 50 years and above is approximately 1:1. This can be attributed to two factors. Firstly, the life expectancy in low HDI countries is shorter, resulting in a reduced population base for those aged 50 years and above. If breast cancer incidence rates remain stable, we would expect a decrease in the number of new breast cancer cases within specific age groups, resulting in a diminishing ratio of new cases relative to the overall incidence. This shift suggests a near-equal distribution of new cases across age categories. Moreover, genetic predispositions play a significant role in breast cancer incidence. Research highlights that women of African descent in low HDI countries exhibit a genetic propensity to develop breast cancer at an earlier age compared with their counterparts in higher HDI countries. Similarly, African American women in the United States are diagnosed with breast cancer at younger ages compared with other American women, despite similar economic and healthcare access levels. These observations suggest that genetic factors, rather than solely socioeconomic or medical advancements, significantly influence the age of breast cancer onset. Thus, although improvements in healthcare and life expectancy contribute to lower cancer rates in developed regions, genetic predispositions remain a crucial determinant of breast cancer incidence across different populations. [ 38 ] There exist notable disparities in the distribution of breast cancer mortality across regions characterized by varying levels of the HDI. In countries with very high HDI, the highest mortality rates are observed among individuals aged 85 years and older. This phenomenon can be explained by two primary factors. First, demographic aging and the associated base effect result in a larger elderly population, thereby increasing the absolute number of deaths within this age cohort. Second, the widespread implementation of breast cancer screening programs has substantially reduced mortality rates among middle-aged and younger women, particularly those aged 50–69 years. Empirical evidence from the United States indicates that initiating screening within this age range and maintaining it for a decade prevents approximately 21.3 breast cancer deaths per 10,000 women screened (95% CI: 10.7–31.7), implying that screening roughly 469 women over 10 years averts one death. [ 39 ] This screening efficacy has markedly enhanced survival outcomes in the middle-aged and younger populations. Despite these advancements, it is important to recognize that elderly patients, especially those aged 75 years and above, tend to experience poorer prognoses irrespective of breast cancer subtype (including hormone receptor–positive/negative, Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2)-positive, and triple-negative) and stage at diagnosis. [ 40 ] This trend further intensifies the mortality burden within the oldest age groups. In countries characterized by low to medium HDI, particularly those with low HDI, the highest mortality rates are observed notably earlier, predominantly within the age range of 50–69 years. This pattern can be primarily attributed to two factors. First, there is a substantial incidence of late-stage cancer diagnoses, largely due to inadequate screening programs that result in many patients being identified at advanced stages of the disease. [ 41 ] Second, there is a deficiency in treatment resources, as the accessibility and availability of essential therapeutic modalities, including targeted pharmaceuticals and radiotherapy equipment, are limited, thereby adversely impacting treatment efficacy and patient outcomes. [ 42 ] In comparison with the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study on breast cancer, the present analysis reveals a largely consistent global pattern in incidence and mortality across various age groups. Both investigations identify the highest incidence of new breast cancer cases within the 50–59 age cohort. However, a divergence emerges regarding the age group with the peak mortality: This study reports it as 60–64 years, whereas the GBD study indicates 55–59 years. It is pertinent to acknowledge that the two data sources emphasize different metrics; the current study prioritizes absolute case numbers, although the GBD study focuses predominantly on ASR. Despite these methodological differences, both studies demonstrate an upward trajectory in breast cancer incidence and mortality, underscoring the persistent global health challenge posed by this disease. [ 43 ] Within the GBD database, breast cancer incidence rates tend to be higher in countries and regions characterized by a high sociodemographic index (SDI) relative to those with medium or low SDI levels, whereas mortality rates are comparatively lower. This pattern is consistent with findings from the GLOBOCAN for countries and regions with high or very high HDI scores. Such trends may be attributable to widespread implementation of early detection programs, enhanced access to therapeutic interventions, and overall improvements in healthcare quality. Certain discrepancies are observed in data from specific countries; for instance, some high-SDI nations exhibit marginally elevated mortality rates in GBD estimates compared with those reported by GLOBOCAN. [ 44 ] These inconsistencies may stem from differences in modeling approaches, data sources, and disease classification methodologies. [ 45 ] Our projections reveal that the burden of breast cancer is expected to remain substantial, particularly in low HDI countries and among individuals aged 50 years and older. This elucidates the urgent need for implementing proactive and effective strategies to mitigate future impacts. In terms of modifiable risk factors, interventions remain relatively limited. Key components of primary prevention include maintaining a healthy weight, increasing physical activity, reducing tobacco and alcohol consumption, and promoting extended breastfeeding. Evidence supports these strategies, as they can significantly reduce breast cancer risk. For instance, studies have shown that weight management and regular exercise lower estrogen levels, which are associated with reduced breast cancer risk. In addition, extended breastfeeding has been linked to a decreased risk of breast cancer, likely due to hormonal changes during lactation. A randomized controlled trial conducted in low- and middle-income countries has highlighted the effectiveness of clinical breast palpation for early detection. This method has been shown to enhance early diagnosis and reduce the stage at which breast cancer is detected, thereby improving prognosis and treatment outcomes. Early detection through clinical examination can lead to less aggressive treatments and better survival rates, demonstrating the importance of integrating accessible and cost-effective screening methods into public health strategies. [ 46 ] In countries and territories with low HDI levels that cannot afford mammography screening program, clinical breast palpation should be promoted by healthcare professionals to local women as a simple and cost-effective procedure. This should be done to increase awareness of self-examination, educate them about the early symptoms and signs of breast cancer, and reduce stigma through public education and awareness-raising. The aim is to detect and diagnose patients at an early stage and provide early treatment, which should result in a better prognosis. [ 47 , 48 ] A survey conducted in several high-income countries and one lower-middle-income country have shown that the incidence of estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer is increasing. If this trend could be identified on a global scale under expanded survey conditions, as this particular subtype of breast cancer has a more favourable prognosis, it would be highly beneficial in reducing the prognostic burden associated with breast cancer. [ 12 ] Our study is subject to several limitations. Our forecasts for the burden of breast cancer in 2050 do not account for potential shifts in incidence rates over time, including variations among different breast cancer subtypes. These predictions are based solely on population growth and aging trends from 2022 to 2050. Disparities in breast cancer diagnosis and detection programs across different countries and periods further complicate comparability, as variations in these practices can influence reported incidence and mortality rates. In conclusion, this study highlights the geographical disparities in breast cancer incidence and mortality rates, categorized by age and HDI, and projects these incidence and mortality metrics for 2050. The significant variability in breast cancer burden across regions describes the necessity for region-specific cancer prevention programs. Tailoring these programs to address the unique economic contexts and epidemiological trends of each region will enhance their effectiveness. Moreover, in an era of globalization, increasing awareness of socioeconomic and cultural factors that influence cancer risk is crucial. Healthcare systems must anticipate and adapt to rising breast cancer risks associated with these factors. Effective cancer control strategies should focus on comprehensive population education, risk factor management, promotion of screening programs, and ensuring both the accessibility and affordability of treatment. By implementing targeted interventions and adapting to local contexts, governments and healthcare providers can better address the evolving challenges of breast cancer.
