INTRODUCTION Racial disparities in breast cancer mortality emerged in the mid‐1980s, which coincided with advances in screening and treatment. 1 , 2 Before this time, breast cancer mortality was comparable for Black and White US women. The timing of this disparity, along with evidence of a period effect, 1 suggests that inequities in access to health resources are at play. By building off the fundamental cause theory, which posits that health disparities ultimately arise as a result of social inequities, 3 Tehranifar et al. provided empirical evidence that cancer sites with greater amenability to medical intervention (e.g., screening and effective treatments) have larger racial disparities in survival. 4 , 5 Furthermore, there are racial and socioeconomic gradients in screening uptake and utilization consistent with fundamental cause theory. 6 Per fundamental cause theory, disparities will be largest after innovation, when the most advantaged groups can access newly developed, effective therapies, whereas less advantaged groups cannot. 3 , 6 As access to screening and therapies increases, disparities may attenuate until new advances arrive and reestablish disparities. Thus, understanding the interplay between structural factors and medical advances is necessary in order to identify ways to effectively prevent disparities and lower disease burden. A nascent but growing body of research supports that historical redlining is associated with adverse breast cancer end points, including survival. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Historical redlining was a federally sponsored residential segregation policy from the 1930s, in which the Home Owners’ Loan Corporation (HOLC) appraised neighborhoods for mortgage lending explicitly on the basis of race, ethnicity, and class composition, along with land use (e.g., residential vs. mixed use). 15 Neighborhoods were graded A (“best”) to D (“hazardous”); D‐graded neighborhoods were colored red on maps, thus “redlined.” Although historical redlining was outlawed in the 1968 Fair Housing Act, it helped to create a vicious cycle that upheld and strengthened segregation. 15 Even today, neighborhood socioeconomic and environmental profiles differ according to historical redlining grade. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 As depicted by our conceptual framework (Figure 1 ), we theorize that historical redlining acts as a fundamental cause of breast cancer outcomes via the establishment of neighborhood trajectories that influenced neighborhoods’ physical environments, health resources, and economic inequalities. Although there is evidence that worse historical redlining grade is associated with lower breast cancer survival, it is unknown whether this disparity has changed over time. Given the numerous medical advances in breast cancer and the disparate access to health care, it is likely that associations with historical redlining are time dependent. 21 , 22 The importance of characterizing the temporal effects of historical redlining associations is twofold. First, although historical redlining has consistently been shown to be a significant factor for breast cancer outcomes, the point of intervention is unclear, given that this is a historical policy no longer in effect. Detailing how associations change across time may improve our understanding of the pathways via which historical redlining operates. Second, explicitly assessing a fundamental cause for survival over time may provide insight into how to anticipate and equitably deliver advances in early detection and treatment. In the present study, we evaluated associations between historical redlining and all‐cause survival among a large cohort of female breast cancer cases in New York State across five diagnostic periods, which range from 1995 to 2019. We hypothesize that the association between historical redlining and survival will differ across time periods, and expect the association to attenuate over time, although never disappear, as time since major advances in screening and drug discovery increases and accessibility generally grows. Given that breast cancer treatments and the timing of their development differ depending on the stage at diagnosis and subtype, we also assessed temporal effects according to stage and hormone receptor status. MATERIALS AND METHODS Study design and sample This population‐based cohort study identified cases from 1995 to 2019 from the New York State Cancer Registry (NYSCR). This included female invasive first primary breast cancer cases ( International Classification of Diseases for Oncology , Third Edition , code C50), an age of ≥20 years, and a residential census tract at diagnosis in a HOLC‐graded neighborhood. This resulted in a final sample of 135,827 cases. Ethical approval This study was approved by the University at Buffalo Institutional Review Board. Data are reported to the NYSCR by health care facilities and practitioners. The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act does not require written informed consent from each patient with cancer reported to public health entities. Exposure New York State has 18 geographically spread cities with HOLC maps (Figure S1 ). 23 We assigned cases a historical redlining grade via linkage to their residential census tract at diagnosis. We defined HOLC grades with the Historic Redlining Score (HRS) data set. 24 , 25 Although HOLC grades were given in 1935–1940 and do not change, census tract boundaries can change over time, and do not necessarily align with the neighborhood boundaries used in HOLC maps. 26 To address this, the HRS data set assigns contemporary census tracts an area‐weighted average HOLC score (range, 1–4), which was done for the 1990, 2000, and 2010 census tracts. 27 This approach has been identified to have the highest sensitivity for linking HOLC grades to contemporary census tracts. 26 Moreover, our sample population spans a period that also could include changing census tract boundaries (cases diagnosed in 1995–2019), and thus we used the decennial census tract boundaries corresponding to the year of diagnosis to assign cases a HOLC score. Cases diagnosed from 1995 to 1999 were assigned HOLC scores on the basis of 1990 census tracts; cases from 2000 to 2009 used 2000 census tracts; and cases from 2010 to 2019 used 2010 census tracts. All 135,827 cases were linked to a historical redlining score. Consistent with prior research, 24 , 27 we categorized scores to A–D grades as follows: A, score 1.0– 1.75; B, score 1.75– 2.5; C, score 2.5– 3.25; D, score 3.25–4.0. Outcome Five‐year all‐cause mortality was the outcome of interest. Follow‐up lasted from January 1, 1995, to December 31, 2021. We right truncated cases at 60 months after diagnosis for 5‐year survival, with death considered an event. Cases diagnosed during or after 2016 did not have 5 years of follow‐up time, and thus were censored if patients were alive at the end of the follow‐up period (December 31, 2021). Covariates Covariates included age, diagnostic period, and city of residence at diagnosis. Diagnostic periods included the following: 1995–1999, 2000–2004, 2005–2009, 2010–2014, and 2015–2019. Stage at diagnosis and hormone receptor status were used for stratification. Stage at diagnosis was categorized as local, regional, distant, or unknown. Hormone receptor status was categorized as positive, negative, or unknown on the basis of estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) status; ER/PR status was routinely collected by the NYSCR after 2004. Triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) status was defined as TNBC, non‐TNBC, or unknown via ER, PR, and HER2 data when available. HER2 status was not routinely collected by the NYSCR before 2010. We evaluated race/ethnicity and health care factors descriptively. Because race and ethnicity were factors that determined HOLC grades, and their contemporary distribution differs significantly by HOLC grade, we did not adjust for case race/ethnicity to avoid overcontrolling the models. Race/ethnicity was categorized as Hispanic, non‐Hispanic Black, non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic other, and non‐Hispanic unknown race. Non‐Hispanic American Indian/Alaskan Native cases were grouped with non‐Hispanic Asian/Pacific Islander because of small sample sizes, and labeled “non‐Hispanic other.” First‐course treatments (radiation, chemotherapy, hormone therapy, and surgery) were each categorized as yes or no/unknown. Health insurance was categorized as employer sponsored, Medicare, Medicaid, dual insurance, uninsured, or unknown. Statistical analysis Descriptive statistics were calculated according to historical redlining grade for each diagnostic period. Crude Kaplan–Meier survival curves estimated survival probability according to historical redlining grade for each diagnostic period. Hazard ratios (HRs) of 5‐year all‐cause survival associated with historical redlining grade were estimated via Cox proportional hazards models. The proportional hazards assumption was tested and satisfied by evaluation of the correlation between weighted Schoenfeld residuals and time. Cox models were adjusted for city‐level clustered standard errors via a robust sandwich covariance matrix. To formally test temporal effects, we included a multiplicative interaction term between historical redlining grade and diagnostic period. HRs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated for each period in the interaction model. We stratified time‐interacted models by stage, ER/PR status, and race/ethnicity. We ran a sensitivity analysis to adjust for health insurance and first‐course treatments, as well as with full survival time. Statistical analyses were conducted with SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina). Figures were created with R 4.2.1 and ArcGIS Pro 10.8 (Esri, Redlands, California). DISCUSSION In this population‐based cohort of 135,827 patients with breast cancer diagnosed between 1995 and 2019, we found that the association between historical redlining and breast cancer survival differs across time. Results suggest that historical redlining‐based disparities in 5‐year survival generally narrowed over time until recently, when the overall disparity may have increased. Although disparities narrowed, women in D‐graded neighborhoods faced a significantly higher mortality risk compared to those in A‐graded neighborhoods throughout all time periods. The risk of death associated with historical redlining was largest in 1995–1999 for local and regional cases, and generally attenuated over time. In contrast, we did not find that historical redlining was associated with mortality among distant‐stage tumors in 1995–1999; however, the disparity started to widen in 2000–2004 through 2010–2014. We detected temporal effects among ER/PR+ tumors, where survival disparities widened over time. Finally, we found significant temporal effects across all race/ethnicity groups. This is the first study to consider temporal effects on cancer survival disparities by historical redlining specifically but is among many to document persistent disparities according to social determinants of health. 1 , 28 , 29 , 30 Our results suggest that survival has improved over time for all groups and that survival disparities have generally narrowed; however, the highest advantage group (here, women in A‐graded neighborhoods) maintains the highest survival. Our results are consistent with work from Tehranifar et al. that shows that disparities in cancer survival are largest for cancer sites with screening and treatment options, which indicates that disparities emerge as a result of differential access to health resources. 4 , 5 In considering our results from a fundamental cause theory perspective, the largest redlining‐based disparities were likely preceded by advances in breast cancer screening and treatment. 3 The 1990s were an era for drug discovery with major advances in breast cancer treatment approaches, specifically adjuvant therapy and aromatase inhibitors for early‐stage breast cancer; neoadjuvant chemotherapy for locally advanced breast cancer; anthracyclines, taxanes, and capecitabine for metastatic breast cancer; and trastuzumab for HER2+ tumors. 21 , 31 These numerous medical breakthroughs may undergird the large survival disparity we observed in 1995–1999, particularly among local‐ and regional‐stage cases, which are more amenable to treatment. Similarly, the Food and Drug Administration’s approval of cyclin‐dependent kinase 4/6 inhibitors for hormone receptor–positive distant‐stage breast cancer in 2015 and poly(adenosine diphosphate ribose) polymerase inhibitors for HER2− distant‐stage breast cancer in 2018, along with the disparities in regional‐stage breast cancer, may help to explain the widening disparity we observed among 2015–2019 cases. 32 , 33 This inference is further supported by our results among ER/PR+ cases, for which disparities have been widening with time. These recent medical advances tailored to subtype and stage, and their inequitable access, may outweigh contemporaneous influences of the Affordable Care Act (ACA), given that 2015–2019 was the full first study period during which the ACA was fully implemented, and thus reduced the proportion of the population without insurance. It is also worth noting that cases diagnosed in 2015–2019 could have faced mortality risks related to coronavirus disease 2019, which may also differ according to historical redlining grade. 34 However, 2015–2019 is the most recent period for observation, and thus findings could be spurious. Relevant to our study population, geographic disparities in the adoption of digital mammography have been reported in New York State, with higher socioeconomic areas adopting digital mammography before lower socioeconomic areas, which may also contribute to the observed temporal effect. 35 Our results provide evidence that historical redlining acts as a fundamental cause of breast cancer survival, and consequently can lead to unintended consequences from medical innovation. 6 Although initiatives to advance breast cancer treatment are necessary, the arrival of new drugs may present financial toxicity to patients, and may further exacerbate existing socioeconomic disparities in cancer survival. 36 Results from the present study, along with those from similar work, recommend policy efforts that remove socioeconomic barriers to accessing new screening modalities and treatments. 4 , 5 , 6 , 37 , 38 This may include a more equitable insurance policy, as was the goal of the ACA, as well as increasing spatial access to health care facilities, improving education for facilities and practitioners on newly developed therapies, and increasing community outreach programs. 36 , 39 Improvements in Medicare coverage could be prudent given the high out‐of‐pocket costs of newly developed drugs and the older age of patients with breast cancer. 36 , 40 Finally, primary prevention is important, particularly given that historical redlining grade may play a role in tumor biology and the development of aggressive tumors. 11 , 41 In a prior ecologic study, we found significant clustering of behavioral and socioeconomic factors according to historical redlining status, which may mediate associations between HOLC grade and adverse breast cancer outcomes. 42 In the Northeast (which includes New York State), redlined neighborhoods have significantly lower health insurance, income, educational attainment, leisure physical activity, and mammography rates, and higher obesity rates. More broadly, historical redlining may operate as a fundamental cause because it was a form of profit‐oriented land valorization, which continues to motivate the US housing market, and thus subsequently produces spatial inequality and contributes to wealth gaps. 43 To our knowledge, this is the first study to consider the temporal effects of historical redlining grade on cancer survival. Understanding how this association changes across time is important for multiple reasons. First, it clarifies that, although HOLC grades are not themselves intervenable, the association is dynamic over time, and thus HOLC grades are proxying a neighborhood exposure (whether physical or socioeconomic) that is intervenable. Second, this work further illuminates how historical redlining may operate as a fundamental cause for breast cancer survival. 3 Although it is disconcerting that we observed a widening of survival disparities in recent years, we believe that these results suggest that delivery of medical innovations is a significant pathway by which disparities may emerge. Strategies to equitably deliver medical advancements may be key to avoid widening future survival disparities. Other strengths of the study include the sample size, the number of cities in New York State with HOLC maps, and their geographic spread, which allowed for more robust statistical models. 44 There are limitations that need to be considered. Although the NYSCR was established in 1976, data before 1995 are not available, which thus prevents us from evaluating temporal effects closer to the widespread use of mammography during the 1980s. 1 Data on first‐course treatments and health insurance were not routinely collected by the NYSCR until 2003 and 2004, respectively, and thus we are unable to evaluate these factors in earlier time periods. This is salient, given that we believe that our results suggest that differential access to treatments and health insurance may give rise to the temporal effect but we are unable to comprehensively test whether the distribution of health care factors by HOLC grade has changed over time. Furthermore, there may be issues with data quality for these variables, and sensitivity can vary by case characteristics. 45 , 46 , 47 Data on subtype were not routinely collected; ER/PR status was collected in 2004 and onward, and HER2 status was collected in 2010 and onward. Although we stratified cases by ER/PR status, we were limited to comprehensively assessing disparities by subtype. We were unable to evaluate breast cancer–specific mortality; however, prior studies have shown a similar magnitude of disparities with redlining whether for all‐cause or breast cancer–specific mortality. 8 , 9 , 13 Without the use of geocoded addresses, misclassification of HOLC grade is possible. 26 The NYSCR does not collect residential information before or after diagnosis, and thus we cannot account for residential mobility. This study also has limited generalizability, given the focus on HOLC‐graded neighborhoods. First, the urban context brings different circumstances that do not necessarily hold for rural areas. Furthermore, cities generally expanded after the creation of HOLC maps, and thus HOLC‐graded neighborhoods only represent a fraction of the residents in a given urban area. Previous research suggests that HOLC‐graded neighborhoods (including A‐ and B‐graded neighborhoods) may have more adverse exposure profiles compared to nongraded neighborhoods. 48 Finally, trends in survival statistics should be interpreted cautiously because of lead‐time bias and overdiagnosis bias. However, survival for regional‐ and distant‐stage cancers may be less prone to such biases. Our results indicate that differences in survival by historical redlining grade persist across all strata of cancer stages. In conclusion, the association between historical redlining and all‐cause survival after breast cancer differs across time. Our results suggest that survival disparities between historical redlining grades have generally narrowed over time, although there has been a recent, albeit relatively small, widening of the gap. Temporal changes in this survival disparity may reflect differential access/uptake of medical interventions.
