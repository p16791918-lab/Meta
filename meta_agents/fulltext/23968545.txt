Why was the study undertaken? This study aimed to provide a predictive tool for sentinel lymph node metastasis (SLNM) in melanoma, particularly for the Asian population, by developing a robust machine learning model integrating histopathological features and clinical data. What does this study add? Introduced the M elanoma I ndicative S corer for S entinel L ymph node E valuation (MISSLE), a hybrid model combining multiple instance deep learning and a clinical nomogram, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.950, surpassing previous studies. High‐attention patch analysis suggests histopathological features for SLNM, such as cytoplasmic paleness and melanin density, providing a novel perspective on melanoma morphology. What are the implications of this study for disease understanding and/or clinical care? MISSLE offers a powerful, objective tool for SLNM risk stratification, enhancing decision‐making for sentinel lymph node biopsy and personalized melanoma management. Identified that histological features are sensitive not only to microscopic metastases but also to molecular indications, supporting the integration of AI‐driven pathology into clinical workflows. INTRODUCTION Melanoma, which originates from melanin‐producing melanocytes in normal skin, 1 is a prevalent and life‐threatening malignant neoplasm worldwide. Various advanced therapeutic strategies, including immunotherapy and targeted therapy, 2 have been developed to address the biological disparities among these neoplasms. One of the most important prognosis‐limiting factors of melanoma is lymph node metastasis, which is the determinant for the Stage III disease, according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC). 3 Sentinel lymph node (SLN) biopsy is the standard for nodal staging and is associated with prolonged disease‐free survival. 4 Although recommended for melanomas with intermediate tumour thickness, 5 the decision to perform lymph node biopsy relies on stratifying patients into high‐ and low‐risk categories for SLN metastasis (SLNM). 6 Therefore, accurate prediction or risk stratification of SLNM is crucial for guiding the subsequent management of patients with melanoma. A nomogram incorporating age, primary site, tumour thickness, Clark level and ulceration to predict SLNM was developed by Wong et al. 7 as a stratification tool. Integrating artificial intelligence (AI) into pathology is a captivating avenue in cancer research. Deep learning (DL) as a special form of machine learning (ML) is the primary driver since 2012. Compared with human experts, AI models are often more objective and reproducible. Pathological multiple‐instance learning (MIL) is a weakly supervised approach where only bag‐level labels (e.g. metastasis status) are available. A MIL method, clustering‐constrained attention multiple‐instance learning (CLAM) 8 is a DL framework leveraging an auxiliary clustering loss to enhance instance representation within bags. Previous studies demonstrated the effectiveness of CLAM, outperforming other methods in various applications. 9 Although there was one study that aimed to predict SLNM in the European population, 10 little attention has been devoted to the use of morphological features by AI models to assess the biological behaviours and progression of melanoma, especially in Asian populations. In this study, we developed a collaborative multiple‐instance and ML model, which we called the M elanoma I ndicative S corer for S entinel L ymph node E valuation (MISSLE) to predict SLNM in patients with melanoma (Figure 1 ). MISSLE is a hybrid of multiple‐instance DL models consisting of ResNet50trunc encoder and CLAM 8 using pathological images and the Wong et al. nomogram, which has been trained and validated on a cohort comprising almost all Japanese individuals who underwent pathological and PCR testing for SLNM, thereby providing a valuable resource compared with Western countries. 11 FIGURE 1 Overview of the Melanoma Indicative Scorer for Sentinel Lymph node Evaluation (MISSLE) development. A clustering‐constrained attention multiple‐instance learning (CLAM) is trained on the tissue region of the whole‐slide images. Tiled patches were encoded in the 1024‐dimensional embeddings with ResNet50trunc. An attention network is branched from the main network and merged as the gated attention. The classification and instance losses are minimized simultaneously. The CLAM model prediction is combined with nomogram probabilities with gradient boosting to yield the final MISSLE score. MATERIALS AND METHODS Data cohort The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board of the University of Yamanashi. Informed consent was obtained from all patients through an opt‐out process. Between 2007 and 2022, 81 patients with invasive cutaneous melanoma were retrieved from the University of Yamanashi Hospital archives. These patients underwent partial excision biopsy or were wholly excised at the time of diagnosis, followed by SLN biopsy. Two patients were excluded because of inaccessible haematoxylin and eosin (H E) sections. One patient who had distinct morphological characteristics of desmoplastic melanoma was excluded. Finally, 78 patients were enrolled in this study (Figure S1 ). Pathological examination with immunohistochemistry for S‐100, SOX10, MART‐1/MelanA and/or HMB‐45 is the gold standard to determine the histological indications of melanoma (HIM). However, reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) has been reported to identify the molecular indications of melanoma (MIM) as an alternative or supplement for SLNM detection. 6 , 12 , 13 Although a meta‐analysis showed that the presence of MIM on RT‐PCR correlated with worse prognosis, 14 the role of RT‐PCR assay remains controversial. 6 , 13 The SLN samples were examined histologically and analysed by RT‐PCR using primers targeting the genes MART‐1 , gb100 and tyrosinase . A positive SLNM was defined as the presence of HIM and/or MIM in all patients, except two patients, in whom overt SLNM was detected by contrast‐enhanced computed tomography. Histological melanoma diagnosis was supported by positive staining for MART‐1, gb100 (HMB‐45) and/or S‐100. Prognostic data were obtained from the patient's medical records. The SLNM‐positive and SLNM‐negative cohorts were randomly divided into the training ( n = 60) and test ( n = 18) sets, respectively. All H E sections were prepared according to standard protocol; after reexamination by a Japanese pathologist (MK), a single representative section was selected for each case. Whole‐slide images (WSIs) were scanned using a Motic Easy Scanner (Motic, Hong Kong, China) at 20× or 0.5 microns per pixel. Reverse‐transcription polymerase chain reaction analysis Total RNA was extracted from the homogenized half SLN tissues using an RNA extraction kit (Nippon Gene, Tokyo, Japan), following the manufacturer's protocol. cDNA synthesis was performed using the ReverTra Ace PCR RT Kit (Toyobo, Tokyo, Japan), following the standard protocol. The PCR products were separated on a 2% agarose gel prepared using Tris‐borate‐EDTA buffer, stained with ethidium bromide and visualized under ultraviolet illumination using ImageQuant LAS4000 (GE Healthcare, Chicago, USA). The primer sequences are listed in Table S1 . All experiments included positive and negative controls, and the results were validated by triplicate experiments to ensure reproducibility. Clinical nomogram We used the clinical nomogram tool available at https://www.mskcc.org/nomograms/melanoma/sentinel_lymph_node_metastasis (accessed between 2024 December and 2025 January), provided by Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center, based on the work by Wong et al. 7 This tool predicts SLNM probability based on age, tumour thickness, Clark level, tumour location and ulceration. Multiple‐instance learning using the pathological sections WSIs were segmented and tiled into patches using the CLAM pipeline with the tcga.csv preset. Two versions of the regions of interest were prepared by a Vietnamese pathologist (MKL) trained in Japan: whole‐tissue and delineated tumour‐annotated regions. The tiled histopathological patches had dimensions of 224 × 224 pixels, corresponding to a physical size of 112 × 112 μ. We experimented with pretrained ResNet50 8 , 15 and UNI 16 as the image encoders to encode patches into 1024‐dimensional vectors. Patches were encoded using ResNet50trunc, which uses intermediate features from imagenet 17 ‐pretrained ResNet50. While UNI is a large‐sized vision transformer 18 that had been pretrained on pathological images. Because of the limited number of available cases and to avoid overfitting, we conducted a minimal hyperparameter search. The best‐performing model was selected after testing the learning rate at 2e−2, 2e−3 and 2e−4. Compared with the entire tissue region, the tumour‐annotated region had smaller bags. Accordingly, the number of training epochs was set to 30 in whole‐tissue regions and 50 in tumour‐annotated regions. The number of positive and negative patches to sample in the CLAM algorithm was set at 8 and 4, respectively. Using warmup cosine annealing, the learning rate was scheduled to linearly increase during the first 10% of the training iterations before cosine decay during the rest of the training. The optimizer was Adam with β values of 0.9 and 0.999 using a weight decay of 1e−5. Table S2 shows the technical specifications of the CLAM. Hybrid machine learning methods We constructed hybrid ML models using the CLAM model and clinical nomogram probabilities. We experimented with logistic regression, a support vector machine and gradient boosting. We used the scikit‐learn default hyperparameters. The calibration curve of the predicted probability against the true probability is depicted. Sharply additive explanations (SHAP) values 19 were calculated to determine the contribution of each feature. Evaluation metrics To evaluate the performance of the prediction models, we used four metrics: AUROC, area under the precision–recall curve (AUPRC), F1 score and accuracy. Among these parameters, AUROC was designated as the primary endpoint because it is a threshold‐free comprehensive metric that had been used in previous reports to enable direct comparison. The test set performance metrics were reported unless otherwise mentioned. Statistical analysis Categorical variables were compared using Fisher's exact or chi‐square test on R software version 4.2.2. The AUROCs were compared using two‐sided DeLong's test. The decision curve displays the relationship between net benefit and threshold probability. 20 Survival curves were drawn using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log‐rank test on the Python lifeline package version 0.27.8. Environment We performed multiple‐instance DL using PyTorch 2.3.0. For ML and metrics evaluation, scikit‐learn version 1.0.1 and shap version 0.44.1 packages were used. For generating histopathological patches and annotation, we used the OpenSlide 4.0.0 package and the automated slide analysis platform software (ASAP) version 1.9. Attention heatmap visualization and interpretation of high attention patches To analyse the morphological features contributing to SLNM status prediction, we used the CLAM model attention weights. Among the top four attention patches from the test set, representative tissue patches were selected by MK. The patches with the highest attention scores for SLNM‐positive prediction were found to have pale cytoplasm, plasma cell‐like morphology and mild melanin deposition (Figure 4 ). Conversely, the patches with the highest attention scores for SLNM‐negative prediction exhibited eosinophilic cytoplasm, rhabdoid and lymphocyte‐like morphology and dense melanin deposition (Figure 5 ). To evaluate the sensitivity of our approach, we conducted ablation analysis by excluding MIM and defining SLNM‐positive status based solely on the presence of HIM. As a result, six patients in the training set and one patient in the test set were reclassified as SLNM‐negative. Using these revised labels, we developed the clinical nomogram, CLAM‐R50 and hybrid gradient boosting models, while maintaining the same hyperparameters described in the previous sections. This modification degraded the CLAM‐R50 model AUROC from 0.872 to 0.843, suggesting that histological features contributed to the identification of both HIM and MIM in lymph nodes. Similarly, the hybrid gradient boosting model AUROC decreased from 0.950 to 0.792, highlighting its sensitivity to label changes. However, the clinical nomogram remained robust, with its AUROC changing from 0.868 to 0.877. These results are summarized in Table 5 . DISCUSSION In this study, we developed MISSLE, which is a hybrid ML and DL model that was found to have high performance in predicting SLNM status in a cohort comprising almost all Japanese patients. Previous studies on melanoma identified various clinicopathological factors associated with nodal metastasis. 7 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 Pathological factors, such as Clark level, melanoma subtypes, tumour thickness, mitotic index, ulceration, lymphatic invasion and tumour‐infiltrating lymphocytes are also critical in predicting nodal metastasis. 7 , 21 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 The center patch in Figure 5 suggests a lymphocytic infiltrating microenvironment for SLNM‐negative prediction. Several gene markers MELANA encoding MART‐1, a marker of melanosome biogenesis, along with several epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) markers have been associated with lymph node involvement. 22 Although the morphological correlates of these markers remain to be clarified, MLANA expression could be linked to melanin deposition, and EMT markers to plasma cell‐like or lymphocyte‐like morphology. 27 , 28 For non‐SLN metastasis, one study found no significant clinicopathological characteristics, 29 whereas another study identified thick primary tumour and large SLN size as risk factors. 30 Although the predictive value of these clinicopathological and molecular factors is crucial for risk stratification of SLN involvement, 31 , 32 , 33 several risk factors, such as melanoma subtype, mitotic index, lymphatic invasion and molecular markers, are often subjective and/or cumbersome to collect. Most AI studies on melanoma focused on diagnostic challenges, as highlighted in a systematic review. 34 These investigations primarily used clinicopathological features along with dermatoscopic and histopathological images to detect malignancies or differentiate between nevus and melanoma. 35 , 36 AI algorithms can effectively use dermatoscopic and microscopic images as inputs, 10 , 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 and efforts are underway to predict the disease course, progression and prognosis. 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 Our MISSLE model used both ML and DL algorithms and demonstrated promising performance in predicting SLNM. Predicting SLNM holds substantial relevance, particularly because routine SLN biopsy is not universally recommended. 5 , 43 Therefore, accurate prediction or risk stratification of SLNM is essential for guiding subsequent management of patients with melanoma. The nomogram by Wong et al. was designed using an American cohort, 7 and external validation on a European cohort reported an AUROC of 0.869 (95% CI 0.813–0.925). 44 Serigne et al. developed a nomogram incorporating age, mitotic rate, ulceration, tumour thickness, melanoma subtype and lymphovascular invasion to predict SLNM in an Australian cohort and validated it in an American cohort to achieve an AUROC of 0.750 (95% CI 0.733–0.767). 24 Brinker et al. developed a DL model on tumour‐annotated WSIs integrating clinical and cell features through an auxiliary network and reported an AUROC of 0.618, 10 which was comparable to our CLAM results on the tumour‐annotated region. Our results suggested that nontumor patches play a crucial role in SLNM prediction, although high‐attention patches were mostly confined to the tumour region. MISSLE offers the greatest net benefit across clinically relevant thresholds (Figure 3 ) particularly valuable for managing intermediate‐thickness (1.2–3.5 mm) and thick ( 3.5 mm) melanomas. For intermediate‐thickness cases (~17% SLNM 4 ), a 0.15 threshold yields 0.88 sensitivity and 0.80 specificity on the test set, resulting in 0.47 positive predictive value (PPV), 0.97 negative predictive value (NPV) and 68% avoidance of SLN biopsies. For thicker melanomas (~35% SLNM 4 ), a 0.40 threshold yields 0.88 sensitivity and 0.80 specificity on the test set, resulting in 0.70 PPV, 0.93 NPV and 56% avoidance of SLN biopsies. Further study is warranted for thinner melanomas, as our cohort is predominantly intermediate‐thickness to thick melanoma cases. Architectural patterns, stromal alterations, cytological features and other morphological characteristics play important roles in melanoma histopathological subtyping and are related to advanced stages. 45 Beyond melanoma subtyping, evaluating mitosis and apoptosis within subtypes proves valuable in predicting malignant potential. 46 Use of MISSLE to extract faint morphological alterations could enhance risk stratification. The additional sensitivity analysis suggested that histological features can be used to detect MIM and HIM. Several studies reported relatively worse prognosis in patients who have MIM only 14 ; therefore, MISSLE offers a valuable tool for clinical management. Our study had several limitations. First, the limited sample size may have inadequately captured the diverse spectrum of histopathological and morphological variants of melanoma. Another limitation was the almost exclusive Japanese population, which is known to have a relatively high prevalence of low cumulative sun‐damage melanoma types, such as acral. Tumorigenesis and progression of melanoma are closely linked to skin pigmentation among different racial groups. 47 , 48 However, this aspect was not necessarily a limitation, because DL models trained on specific racial groups could be more precise and accurate in supporting personalized medicine within those populations, making our data and model both unique and valuable for research purposes. CONCLUSIONS In summary, we developed MISSLE as a collaborative multiple‐instance DL and ML model that was capable of predicting SLNM with high performance. Although our results are preliminary and further external data are required for comprehensive training and validation, the MISSLE holds potential in guiding decision‐making on SLN biopsy and risk stratification.
