Introduction Breast cancer, an enduring public health challenge, continues to exert a substantial impact on communities across the United States. In 2024, an estimated 310,720 new cases of breast cancer were reported, accompanied by 56,500 deaths among women ( 1 ). Although breast cancer is the second leading cause of cancer mortality nationwide, Kentucky faces a unique burden ( 1 , 2 ). Recent data suggest that Kentucky is within the top five leading states for breast cancer mortality rate in the United States with 21.6 breast cancer–related deaths per 100,000 women ( 1 , 2 ). Moreover, it is well documented that non-Hispanic (NH) Black women have significantly higher breast cancer mortality rates than NH-White women, and the same pattern has been observed in Kentucky ( 3 , 4 ). These stark figures underscore the urgency of investigating the factors that contribute to breast cancer outcomes, particularly within the context of socioeconomic disparities and racial inequalities. Area deprivation index (ADI), a measure of socioeconomic disadvantage, from the Neighborhood Atlas is proved to be a powerful tool to provide much more nuanced information about neighborhood disadvantage ( 5 ). ADI integrates key factors such as income, education, employment, and housing quality, to inform health delivery and policy initiatives, aimed at addressing the needs of the most disadvantaged communities ( 5 ). Previous research has highlighted that women who live in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas have a 20% to 33% increased risk for breast cancer–related mortality ( 6 , 7 ). Additionally, studies have revealed a correlation between neighborhood deprivation and tumor characteristics, with women from these areas more likely to develop higher-grade, poorly differentiated breast tumors. Furthermore, higher ADI groups have been associated with elevated allostatic load and reduced global DNA methylation, suggesting possible molecular pathways contributing to these outcomes ( 8 ). Past research has shown that NH-Black women are more likely to live in more disadvantaged neighborhoods than NH-White women being affected by systemic racism ( 6 ). For example, discriminatory loan practices and redlining have contributed to NH-Black women being more likely to live in under-resourced areas ( 9 , 10 ). A survey from New York State hospitals also revealed that physicians have more negative perceptions toward African Americans and people of low or middle socioeconomic status (SES; ref. 3 ). In line with this, NH-Black women face a disproportionate risk for adverse breast cancer outcomes ( 3 ). Nevertheless, there have been limited studies to examine the role of race and neighborhood disadvantage in specific breast cancer outcomes in Kentucky. Therefore, the objective of this study is to build on findings from previous studies and conduct a retrospective cohort analysis to examine whether an association exists between the ADI, a measure of neighborhood socioeconomic disadvantage, and breast cancer outcomes. These outcomes include the receipt of surgical treatment, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, breast cancer–specific survival, and whether these associations differ by race and ethnicity. Here, we examine differences in treatment receipt, as these disparities can contribute to worse breast cancer outcomes by affecting the effectiveness of care, delaying treatment, or limiting access to optimal therapies. Ultimately, the findings of this investigation will inform targeted interventions, policies, and healthcare strategies that aim to alleviate the burden of breast cancer inequalities among the diverse patient population in Kentucky. Materials and Methods Study design and population We conducted a secondary data analysis using retrospective cohort data from the Kentucky Cancer Registry (KCR), which is part of both the NCI’s Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s National Program of Cancer Registries and is an active participant in the North American Association of Central Cancer Registries. SEER registries are considered to be among the most accurate and complete population-based cancer registries in the world, and the funding from SEER has allowed the KCR to be a comprehensive database of all cancer cases in Kentucky. Each patient record was linked to their census tract for the ADI derived from the Neighborhood Atlas project ( https://www.neighborhoodatlas.medicine.wisc.edu/ ). This analysis focused on patients with breast cancer diagnosed between 2010 and 2017, with follow-up data through December 31, 2022. We included women, ages 18 years and older, diagnosed with breast cancer between 2010 and 2017 in the analysis. Participants were excluded if they were diagnosed with breast cancer prior to 2010 (e.g., no information about HER2 status), had an unknown address (e.g., unable to match them with ADI), were missing follow-up time, had missing information about breast cancer stage at diagnosis, had an unknown age, had unknown race/ethnicity, and had a first primary cancer diagnosis other than breast cancer. Using these criteria, only three participants were excluded due to missing address, making the total number of participants included in the study 26,087. Primary exposure of interest This study utilized the ADI, sourced from the Neighborhood Atlas, to evaluate varying degrees of neighborhood disadvantage. We utilized the 2015 ADI (v5) comprised of data from 2010 to 2014—5-year estimates extracted from the US Census American Community Survey. ADI measures neighborhoods as units of census block groups. Census block groups represent the smallest geographic unit utilized by the US Census Bureau for comprehensive data tabulation. The ADI ranks neighborhoods based on their degree of socioeconomic disadvantage within a specific region of interest. It encompasses factors pertaining to income, education, employment, and housing quality. In this study, we utilized the state decile rankings at the level of block groups, ranging from 1 to 10. These percentiles are established by arranging the ADI values in ascending order across Kentucky and categorizing the block groups/neighborhoods into bins that correspond to each 10% interval of the ADI. Notably, a block group with a ranking of 1 denotes the lowest level of “disadvantage” within the state, whereas an ADI score of 10 signifies the highest level of “disadvantage.” In the current study, five-digit zip codes at the time of diagnosis were used to link ADI based on census block group data. Furthermore, we geocoded each KCR patient with breast cancer based on their first five-digit zip code. Thus, ADI in this study represents the mean state-level ADI scores aggregated to the five-digit zip codes. Not every patient had the complete nine-digit zip code. Thus, it was only feasible to use their five-digit zip codes and link with an averaged version of ADI at the five-digit zip code. We categorized state-level ADI into quartiles, with the first quartile representing the least disadvantaged and the fourth quartile representing the most disadvantaged neighborhoods. In our analyses, we examined ADI as both a continuous variable and quartiles to understand the potential linear relationship between ADI and breast cancer outcomes. Outcomes of interest There were five outcome variables of interest in this study: (i) stage at diagnosis based on the SEER summary stage variable and categorized as late stage (if regional or distant) or early stage (if in situ and localized); (ii) surgical treatment given or not; (iii) radiation treatment given or not; (iv) chemotherapy treatment given or not; and (v) breast cancer–specific death and time to death. Covariates of interest Other individual-level covariates included patient demographics such as age, tobacco use (never tobacco user or tobacco user), marital status, insurance status (uninsured, private insurance, Medicare/Medicaid, or military), and race/ethnicity (NH-White, NH-Black, NH-Asian, or Hispanic/Latina). Stage at diagnosis referred to whether tumor was localized, regionalized, or distant. Breast cancer subtype included luminal A, luminal B, HER2 + enriched, and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Patient demographics were also adjusted for residence in Appalachia and family history of breast cancer. Statistical analysis The distribution of the KCR patient characteristics between groups of ADI was compared and presented as descriptive statistics including the count and relative frequencies (percentages) for each categorical variable. We conducted consecutive logistic regression models for the binary outcomes of (i) late-stage diagnosis, (ii) no surgical treatment, (iii) no radiotherapy, and (iv) no chemotherapy. We interpreted the estimates derived from these logistic regression models as adjusted ORs (aOR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI). We examined the proportional hazards assumption for breast cancer–specific survival using Schoenfeld residuals by graphically assessing the log–log plots of survival for all patients in our sample and stratifying by race. Additionally, all data examined in the analyses were sourced from within the KCR. We conducted three sequentially adjusted models: (i) unadjusted; (ii) adjusted for age (21–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59,60–69, or 70+), race/ethnicity (not in race-stratified models), tobacco use (never used tobacco or tobacco user), pack-years (nonsmoker, 1–10 pack-years, 11–20 pack-years, 21–50 pack-years, 51–100 pack-years, 100+ pack-years, or not reported categorized as a group), marital status (single, married or domestic partner, divorced, separated, or widowed), and insurance status (uninsured, private insurance, Medicaid/Medicare, or military); and (iii) further adjusted for stage at diagnosis (not in late-stage models; in situ , localized, regionalized, or distant), breast cancer subtypes (luminal A, luminal B, HER2 + enriched, or TNBC), family history of breast cancer (yes or no), and residence in Appalachia (non-Appalachia Kentucky or Appalachia Kentucky). These factors were considered confounders based on a prior selection to be included in this study from prior research, and these factors were all associated with ADI in bivariate analysis. After confirming the proportionality of hazards assumption, we examined the hazard/risk of breast cancer death by ADI and fit Cox proportional hazards models with time to breast cancer–related death as the outcome and censored women at the time of death, or the end of follow-up (December 31, 2022). We additionally examined these hazards in race/ethnicity–stratified models. We presented estimates derived from Cox proportional hazard models as adjusted HRs and associated 95% CIs. Due to small sample sizes, we focused race-stratified analysis on NH-White and NH-Black patients. Thus, race-specific regression models were not conducted among NH-Asian, Hispanic/Latina, or women identified as other race/ethnicity. We performed all statistical analyses using SAS (version 9.4, SAS Institute, Inc.). Data availability The data generated in this study are not publicly available due to institutional review board restrictions to protect patient privacy and consent. The process for accessing KCR data is available at https://www.kcr.uky.edu/research/how.php . ADI data generated for this study are publicly available at: https://www.neighborhoodatlas.medicine.wisc.edu/ . Results Descriptive statistics Table 1 describes the KCR statistics we observed for patients with breast cancer in Kentucky who were diagnosed between 2010 and 2017, with follow-up through December 31, 2022. The average age of the participants was 60.85 years. When compared with the first quartile of ADI (least disadvantaged), most patients identified as NH-White, and patients with breast cancer living in the fourth quartile (most disadvantaged) for ADI were more likely to be NH-Black (fourth quartile: 9.01% vs. first quartile: 4.53%). Patients living in the fourth quartile were also more likely to be current tobacco users (fourth quartile: 60.66% vs. first quartile: 48.08%). Women living in the fourth quartile were more likely to be divorced, separated, or widowed when compared with women in first-quartile neighborhoods (fourth quartile: 32.25% vs. first quartile: 23.95%). When looking at insurance status, we observed that women living in the fourth quartile were more likely to receive Medicaid or Medicare compared with the women in the first quartile (fourth quartile: 60.76% vs. first quartile: 36.57%). More than 60% of the patients living in the fourth quartile were Appalachian residents. Patients living in the fourth quartile were also more likely to have invasive breast cancer diagnosis (fourth quartile: 85.1% vs. first quartile: 80.2%) and TNBC subtypes (fourth quartile: 11.3% vs. first quartile: 8.7%) compared with women living in first-quartile neighborhoods. Regression analyses: Associations between ADI and breast cancer outcomes Table 2 displays the results of the regression analyses. In the fully adjusted models, women living in the fourth quartile of ADI were 29% more likely to receive chemotherapy treatment (aOR, 1.29; 95% CI, 1.11–1.49) and had 26% increased odds of late-stage breast cancer diagnosis (aOR, 1.26; 95% CI, 1.12–1.41) when compared with women living in the first quartile of ADI neighborhoods. Race-stratified analyses In Table 3 , we examined the race-stratified risk of breast cancer death in our patient population. Among NH-White patients, after fully adjusting, women in the fourth quartile of ADI were 32% more likely to die from breast cancer compared with the women in the first quartile (HR, 1.32; 95% CI, 1.12–1.57). We observed among NH-White patients for every unit increase in ADI, patients were at 3% increased risk for breast cancer–related death (HR, 1.03; 95% CI, 1.01–1.05). For the NH-Black patients, however, the highest rates of breast cancer death were observed in the third quartile instead of the fourth quartile. In the fully adjusted model, NH-Black women in the third quartile were 53% more likely to die from breast cancer when compared with the first quartile (HR, 1.53; 95% CI, 0.92–2.54), although nonsignificant. We observed among NH-Black patients for every unit increase in ADI, patients were at 4% increased risk for breast cancer–related death (HR, 1.04; 95% CI, 0.98–1.10). Among NH-White patients, women residing in the fourth quartile of ADI were 12% less likely to receive radiotherapy when compared with those in the first quartile (aOR, 0.88; 95% CI, 0.79–0.99), 32% more likely to receive chemotherapy treatment (aOR, 1.32; 95% CI, 1.13–1.54), and 27% more likely to receive a late-stage diagnosis (aOR, 1.27; 95% CI, 1.12–1.43). Discussion In this retrospective cohort study, we assessed the association of ADI with breast cancer outcomes among Kentucky patients. We observed that there was a strong association between ADI, a measure of neighborhood SES, and higher risk of negative breast cancer health outcomes in Kentucky. Our study was consistent with previous findings, which showed that neighborhood-level deprivation was associated with poorer outcomes and lower rates of survival among patients even after accounting for socioeconomic confounders ( 11 , 12 ). We found that more than 60% of the patients living in the most disadvantaged neighborhoods (fourth quartile) were Appalachian residents. Moreover, women living in more disadvantaged areas were less likely to undergo radiation and surgical treatment but more likely to have chemotherapy when compared with women living in the least disadvantaged neighborhoods (first quartile), pointing to what could be a link between ADI and access to health care. This finding is worrisome as women living in more disadvantaged neighborhoods are more likely to have more underlying risk factors and less access to screening services. Thus, they are at a disproportionate risk for adverse breast cancer outcomes ( 13 ). Interventions to improve access to timely diagnosis and prompt treatment for these patients are crucial. The benefits of improved access to care have been demonstrated in Kentucky since the expansion of Medicaid, which has been associated with earlier diagnosis, and somewhat improved quality of breast cancer care ( 14 ). Surgery paired with radiotherapy is the standard of care for early-stage breast cancer ( 15 ). Given the results of the current study, it is conceivable that women living in the most disadvantaged areas have lower odds of receiving these combinations of treatments (e.g., radiation and surgery) but higher odds of receiving chemotherapy. We observed nearly 40% increased odds of chemotherapy among women living in disadvantaged neighborhoods, which is plausible given that patients with late-stage disease are more likely to receive this therapy. Therefore, it stands to reason those living in the most disadvantaged groups are less likely to have access to treatment, especially during the pivotal early diagnosis window. Thus, women living in the most disadvantaged areas continue to be the most vulnerable to adverse breast cancer outcomes. The disparities observed for women living in disadvantaged neighborhoods may be partially due to presentation at a later stage of diagnosis, which is consistent with an increased risk for adverse breast cancer outcomes than women who live in more socioeconomically advantaged communities. This may be especially harmful to women living in Appalachia, and they are more likely to face barriers to care such as lack of transportation and unique geographic challenges; these barriers have been shown to largely drive the negative health outcomes in Kentucky ( 13 ). Dreyer and colleagues ( 16 ) found that upon analyzing 11,368 women older than 60 years from the SEER-Medicare database with invasive breast carcinoma, women with a lower SES were less likely to undergo adjuvant radiation after breast conservation surgery than women with a higher SES. The same group also showed that women with fewer financial resources with stage II or III disease were less likely to undergo adjuvant chemotherapy. Even among women with similar SES, survival has been shown to be worse for Black women versus other races/ethnicities, suggesting that only wealth was shown to protect Black women ( 17 ). The relationship between neighborhood disadvantage and breast cancer outcomes was stratified by race as well. Given that previous studies have shown NH-Black women are more likely to live in more disadvantaged neighborhoods, it is plausible that they are at an increased risk for adverse breast health outcomes, driven in part by a lack of access to care ( 9 , 10 ). Accordingly, we found NH-Black patients had a higher likelihood of late-stage diagnosis and had decreased odds of receiving radiotherapy and surgical treatment. However, the highest rates of breast cancer mortality happened in the third quartile, rather than fourth quartile of NH-Black women. This suggests that access to care alone is not the sole contributor to adverse breast cancer outcomes among NH-Black women. For example, NH-Black patients are systematically disadvantaged in the healthcare system due to inaccessible health communication from their providers ( 18 ). Manifestations of this are exemplified in mammography behaviors. For example, NH-Black women have been shown to be more likely to receive a mammogram than NH-White women but less likely to follow up for abnormal results ( 19 , 20 ). This exemplifies the need for more culturally sensitive care, especially in the context of historical injustices with regard to NH-Black women’s bodily autonomy ( 21 ). NH-White women typically have better breast cancer–related outcomes than their NH-Black counterparts ( 3 , 4 ). This is in part because NH-White women typically have more access to health care as a result of structural inequities ( 22 ). Although it is important to identify and address racial/ethnic disparities in breast cancer healthcare access, it is also important to consider NH-White women who have less access to health care. As both NH-Black and NH-White women in the most socioeconomically disadvantaged neighborhoods have worse outcomes than those with more socioeconomic advantages, our results emphasize the need for efforts to make resources associated with better breast cancer outcomes more accessible to women living in socioeconomically disadvantaged areas while being cognizant of their lived experiences. This is especially important as individuals with lower incomes are often disenfranchised within existing systemic structures in health care. For example, evidence suggests that the financial burden associated with cancer treatment disproportionately affects individuals with higher levels of socioeconomic disadvantage, and debt related to medical expenses deters individuals from seeking care ( 23 , 24 ). Strengths and limitations A strength of our study is the utilization of patient-level data linked to area level deprivation that enabled us to ascertain granular insights with regard to breast cancer outcomes. Additionally, we completed analyses stratified by race. Thus, we were able to identify the magnitude of the impact of the relationship between neighborhood disadvantage and breast cancer outcomes. It is important to identify race/ethnicity-specific determinants of breast cancer outcomes due to racially/ethnically disparate breast cancer–related outcomes. Because we analyzed a variety of breast cancer outcome measures, we were able to identify specific areas where care is lacking for more disadvantaged communities. The study also has limitations. For instance, we only focused on the state of Kentucky. Given its unique Appalachian population, the results from this study may not be generalizable to the rest of the United States. There are potential for misclassification of exposure (ADI) based on patient’s address at diagnosis. Thus, we are unable to account for changes in address over time, and there is the potential that patients could have moved to a different area during their treatment. Additionally, our race-specific determinants of breast cancer outcomes were limited to only NH-White and NH-Black because of small sample size, so these results are not generalizable to all races/ethnicities. However, the methodology described provides a basis for future studies to explore the relationship between ADI and breast cancer outcomes in other states or at the national level. Conclusions The main finding in the current study is that women, regardless of race, living in the most disadvantaged areas are more likely to die from breast cancer when compared with women living in the least disadvantaged areas. Access to quality providers, social determinants, such as transportation barriers, and poverty are key factors in the diagnosis, treatability, and, ultimately, survivability of breast cancer. To achieve equitable health outcomes, efforts should be made to increase healthcare access in more disadvantaged areas. Higher levels of neighborhood deprivation are associated with worse breast cancer outcomes. Efforts aimed at increasing access to care among women living in areas with high levels of neighborhood disadvantage may decrease these efforts. Improving access to care in more disadvantaged neighborhoods may help ameliorate racial/ethnic disparities in breast cancer outcomes in the state of Kentucky.
