Introduction Cancer remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide [ 1 ]. In recent years, growing attention has been devoted to the impact of social determinants of health on cancer risk, access to care, and outcomes. The International Organisation for Migration defines migrants broadly as individuals who move away from their usual place of residence—within or across national borders, temporarily or permanently, and for diverse reasons [ 2 ]. This vulnerable population often faces multiple barriers along the cancer-care continuum, including limited access to prevention and screening programs, delayed diagnosis, and suboptimal adherence to treatment. These inequities may ultimately translate into poorer survival rates and reduced quality of life compared with the general population [ 3 ]. At the European level, numerous studies have reported differences in cancer incidence and outcomes among migrants, reflecting a complex interplay of genetic, environmental, lifestyle, infectious, and socioeconomic factors, as well as disparities in participation in organized screening programs. For instance, higher rates of cervical, stomach, and liver cancers have been observed among migrants from low- and middle-income countries, whereas lower participation in breast, colorectal, and cervical cancer screening is consistently reported across host countries [ 4 – 7 ]. In Italy, more than 5 million migrants currently reside in the country, accounting for approximately 8.6% of the total population. Despite the steadily growing migrant population, data on cancer epidemiology among migrants remain scarce [ 8 ]. Regional disparities in access to oncologic services and the lack of integrated monitoring systems further limit our understanding of these inequities. Only a few studies have investigated cancer incidence among migrants residing in Italy, using data from Regional Cancer Registries [ 4 , 9 - 13 ]. The Campania region, located in southern Italy, is characterized by a high population density, marked socioeconomic heterogeneity, and a substantial number of newly settled migrants. As of January 1st, 2024, there were 265,484 foreign residents in Campania, accounting for 5% of all foreign residents in Italy and 4.7% of the total regional population. Most foreign residents in Campania originate from Europe (42.0%), followed by Asia (27.5%), Africa (25.9%), and the Americas (4.6%) (ISTAT Census, 2023). To promote equity and improve cancer care, the region established the Campania Oncology Network (Rete Oncologica Campana. ROC), as a Comprehensive Cancer Care Network (CCCN). The ROC integrates cancer care pathways across the region through a unified digital platform for patient registration, multidisciplinary management, and outcome monitoring. It comprises 11 Cancer Reference Centers and 7 Local Health Authorities (ASLs). Multidisciplinary Oncology Groups (G.O.M. in the Italian context), operating within and across institutions, ensure timely and equitable care for all cancer patients, guiding them toward the most appropriate diagnostic and therapeutic pathways according to annually updated Diagnostic Therapeutic Care Pathways (PDTA) [e 14 ]. Previous evidence has shown that multidisciplinary management improves cancer outcomes by promoting coordinated, patient-centered care [ 15 ]. The ROC’s modular web-based platform enables the comprehensive tracking of each patient’s journey—from referral and multidisciplinary discussion to treatment and follow-up—ensuring standardized data collection and outcome evaluation. Within this framework, the present study aims to analyze real-world data from the ROC platform to investigate the distribution and proportion of cancers among migrant populations recorded between 2022 and 2025. Ultimately, this analysis can guide and inform health policy decisions, helping to reduce inequalities and improve outcomes for the most vulnerable patients. Methods We extracted data from the ROC digital platform regarding all solid tumor cases diagnosed in adult patients between 2022 and January 31, 2025, as part of the ONCOCAMP project. The ROC platform is integrated with the official regional population registry, ensuring that patient demographics—including vital status and residency—are continuously and accurately updated. In this study, we defined “migrants” as foreign-born individuals residing in the Campania region. This operational definition was based on the country of birth, which was retrieved from the administrative data associated with each patient’s Italian fiscal code. Specifically, the fiscal code’s alphanumeric structure (specifically positions 12–15) allows for the distinction between individuals born in Italy and those born abroad, with the latter identified by a country-specific code starting with ‘Z’. This method provides a reliable and objective proxy for identifying foreign-born status within our institutional database. For each cancer type, we calculated the proportion of new cancer cases among migrants and compared it with that of non-migrants. Proportionate morbidity ratio (PMR) for each cancer type were computed as the ratio of observed to expected cases among migrants, adjusted for sex, age group, and calendar year. Moreover, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of PMR were calculated based on the Poisson distribution of expected cases. Only cancer types with an occurrence greater than 2% in migrants were considered for the PMR analysis. The cancer types included in the analysis were classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10). A detailed mapping of each cancer type to its corresponding ICD-10 code is provided in the Supplementary Table 1. In this study, ‘hereditary cancers’ were defined as tumors arising in the context of genetically confirmed syndromes or in individuals meeting clinical criteria for high-risk hereditary predisposition. This classification is based on the formal Diagnostic-Therapeutic-Assistential Path (PDTA) implemented by the Campania Oncology Network (ROC) for specific hereditary-familial cancers: breast, ovary, endometrium, colon, prostate, pancreas, and melanoma. Within this pathway, patients are identified through a standardized clinical workflow that includes mandatory genetic counseling, the execution of high-quality germline testing, and the interpretation of pathogenic variants. Consequently, this category comprises only individuals for whom a hereditary risk was formally assessed and documented within the ROC institutional database, ensuring high diagnostic accuracy and excluding cases based solely on self-reported family history. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and is included in the ONCOCAMP research protocol, a retrospective–prospective multicenter observational study (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT06270602 , registered on February 21, 2024), aimed at improving the appropriateness, effectiveness, and safety of diagnostic and therapeutic pathways, as well as clinical strategies and procedures within the ROC (“The ONCOCAMP Study”). This study approved by the Ethics Committee of the Istituto Nazionale Tumori IRCCS “Fondazione G. Pascale” of Naples (registration number 6/23 OSS “ONCOCAMP” on 10 March 2023) and by the local Ethics Committees of all participating institutions within the Campania Oncology Network (ROC). The analyses were conducted on pseudo-anonymized data with each patient assigned a unique identifier within the ROC platform. Informed consent was obtained from all participants to be included in the ROC platform and to participate to the assessment of the ROC performance. Results Overall, 78,156 patients were registered in the ROC platform from 2022 to 2025; 37 pediatric cases were excluded, resulting in a study population of 78,119 adults, of whom 2,583 (3.3%) were migrants (Supplementary Fig. 1). Migrant patients showed a significant lower median age compared to Italian-born patients (58 [IQR: 50–65] vs. 67 [IQR: 56–76] years, respectively). Most individuals born abroad belong to the 40–64 year age group (65.9%), while Italians were predominantly over 64 years (56.5%). The sex distribution of cancer cases differed substantially between groups, with migrants showing a markedly higher proportion of female patients compared with Italian residents (74.1% vs. 54.3%). Most migrants originated from Europe (67.9%), with Eastern Europe representing the largest subgroup (35%). They were followed by migrants from America (15.8%), Africa (9.9%), and Asia (6.3%) (Table 1 ). The distribution of cancer cases among migrants showed some differences compared with the overall population of foreign-born residents living in Campania region. While the latter mainly originated from Europe (42.0%), followed by Asia (27.5%) and Africa (25.9%) [ 16 ], cancer diagnosis were predominantly concentrated among migrants of European origin (67.9%, largely from Eastern Europe), with much smaller proportions from Asia and Africa. This discrepancy may reflect differences in demographic composition, duration of residence, health-seeking behaviour, and access to healthcare across migrant subgroups. Eastern European migrants, who are generally more stably settled and better integrated into the regional healthcare system, may therefore be more likely to be diagnosed and registered. Consistent with patterns reported in other European settings, migrant patients in our cohort were overall younger and more frequently female than their Italian-born counterparts. The strong predominance of women among migrant cancer patients (74.1% vs. 54.3% among Italians) further highlights gender-specific differences in healthcare utilization and participation in screening programs. Female migrants, particularly those from Eastern Europe and Latin America, may engage more frequently with health services through reproductive and preventive care, leading to higher detection rates of breast, cervical, and endometrial cancers. Conversely, male migrants may experience greater barriers to access (occupational instability, lower screening uptake, cultural/linguistic hurdles), resulting in under-diagnosis or delayed diagnosis [ 17 ]. Multiple mechanisms may underlie the observed disparities. The higher frequency of cervical, liver and gastric cancers among migrants is likely related to the lower access to vaccination and to the greater prevalence of infectious aetiologies (such as HPV, HBV/HCV and Helicobacter pylori ) in many countries of origin [ 18 ].The higher burden of cervical cancer among women from Eastern Europe, Asia and Latin America is consistent with lower screening uptake and higher HPV prevalence [ 19 ], indeed, several systematic reviews showed that migrant and refugee populations have significantly lower HPV vaccine initiation and completion rates compared with non-migrant populations [ 20 , 21 ]. The increased proportion of HCC among migrants from Asia and Africa likely reflects endemic viral hepatitis and lack of systematic program of vaccination for HBV [ 22 , 23 ]; while the excess of stomach cancer among Asian and Latin American migrants may be associated with persistent H. pylori infection and dietary factors [ 24 ]. Indeed, lifestyle and environmental exposures also contribute to shaping cancer risk profiles and vary according to region of origin and duration of residence. To reduce cancer-related morbidity and mortality, high-income countries employ a combination of primary prevention (vaccination), secondary prevention (screening), and effective therapeutic interventions. Prophylactic HPV vaccines has become a cornerstone of cervical cancer prevention and the WHO recommends routine vaccination of girls aged 9–14 years (with one or two dose schedules) as well as the inclusion of boys where feasible [ 25 ]. However, vaccination does not replace routine cervical cancer screening, and vaccinated women should continue to undergo screening according to local guidelines using Pap testing and/or HPV-DNA testing [ 26 ]. Universal HBV vaccination is standard in most high-income countries and has contributed to reductions in chronic HBV infection and HCC [ 27 ]. Migrants from endemic regions frequently arrive unvaccinated and may not be systematically offered catch-up vaccination or HBV screening, leaving them at sustained risk of liver cancer [ 22 ]. Regarding hepatitis C, several western countries have implemented universal or age-based screening to expand access to curative direct-acting antivirals [ 28 ]. Italy has recently introduced national HCV screening for selected groups, including individuals born between 1969 and 1989, people attending addiction services, and incarcerated persons [ 29 , 30 ]. In addition, international guidelines increasingly recognise H. pylori testing and eradication as an effective strategy for gastric cancer prevention in high-risk populations [ 24 ]. Breast and colorectal cancer screening programs have been widely implemented across the European Union; however, a substantial disparity persists between high-performing Northern European countries, where mammography coverage exceeds 80%, and the Mediterranean and Eastern regions, where colorectal screening rates fall below 50% [ 31 ]. In Italy, the landscape is similarly heterogeneous: while overall coverage for breast and cervical cancer reaches 75–78%, the adjusted participation rates within organized programs are significantly lower, approximately 60% and 50%, respectively, with critical deficiencies observed in colorectal screening (33–35%) [ 32 ]. In the migrant population, the numbers are further reduced: coverage drops to 45–50% for mammography and 55–60% for cervical screening [ 33 ], the latter being particularly concerning given that cervical cancer incidence in migrant women is higher. A profound gap is also evident in primary prevention via HPV vaccination: while the pooled adherence rate for the complete vaccination cycle in Europe is 63.4% [ 34 ], local Italian data reveal alarmingly low effective administration rates, reaching a nadir of 0.7% among refugees and migrants [ 35 ]. These findings underscore highlighting linguistic, cultural, socioeconomic, and administrative barriers that reduce screening uptake and lead to a higher proportion of advanced-stage and poorer clinical outcomes. Interestingly, our analysis revealed a reduced representation of hereditary tumors among migrants, which may reflect both biological and systemic factors. In our setting, the Campania Oncology Network (ROC) has implemented a specific Diagnostic-Therapeutic-Assistential Path (PDTA) for hereditary-familial cancers —specifically those of the breast, ovary, endometrium, colon, prostate, pancreas, and melanoma. This pathway aims to guarantee timely diagnosis, standardized genetic counseling, and high-quality testing to identify pathogenic variants. Moreover, it facilitates targeted oncological treatments for patients and preventive screening for high-risk healthy carriers. However, these specialized services are often less accessible to foreign-born populations due to linguistic barriers, limited awareness, and reduced referral rates. Hereditary cancer programmes typically require stable healthcare engagement and detailed family history documentation—conditions less common among recent or socioeconomically disadvantaged migrants. Similarly, the lower PMR for melanoma may be influenced by phenotypic factors, such as darker skin phototypes in populations from Africa or Asia, though underdiagnosis related to limited dermatologic screening and differences in health-seeking behavior cannot be excluded. Strengths and limitations Strengths of our study include the use of a comprehensive regional digital platform capturing all cancer diagnoses within a defined network (ROC), the ability to adjust PMRs for sex, age and calendar year, and the focus on region of origin stratification. Regarding limitations, while the ROC data is highly representative, it remains distinct from the gold-standard Regional Cancer Registry. Future research would benefit from a formal linkage between these databases to provide an exhaustive, population-based view of cancer incidence. Nevertheless, the ROC’s reliability as a dynamic indicator of oncological management has been well-validated. As demonstrated by Coppola et al. (2025) 14 , the rise in patients evaluated by Multidisciplinary Oncology Group (G.O.M. in the Italian context)—increasing from 64% to 83% between 2022 and 2023—confirms the network’s utility as a monitoring tool. Furthermore, recent analyses (2024–2025) indicate that the ROC platform covers over 99.9% of expected cancer incidence cases in Campania. This high level of coverage confirms that our dataset, despite its clinical-managerial origin, reflects the regional oncological burden with high precision, ensuring that our PMR analyses are highly representative of true population-based trends. Methodologically, our reliance on PMRs rather than pure incidence rates implies a potential sensitivity to mortality patterns and underlying cancer-type distributions. Our analysis also lacks granular data on stage at diagnosis, socioeconomic status, length of residence, legal status, and screening history—variables that serve as critical mediators of health outcomes. Statistical power for certain subgroup analyses may be constrained by the relatively small proportion of migrants (3.3%), and the reliance on fiscal codes for defining migrant status and region of origin introduces a risk of misclassification. Furthermore, the regional specificity of the data may limit the generalizability of our findings beyond Campania. Finally, as incidence reflects exposure to risk factors but does not account for patient survival, it does not fully capture the healthcare system’s capacity to ensure early diagnosis and appropriate treatment. Future studies should prioritize survival analysis to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of health equity and system performance. Implications for policy and practice Our findings highlight the urgent need for tailored public health strategies and equitable cancer-care pathways that adequately address the needs of migrant populations. Efforts should focus on expanding culturally and linguistically adapted outreach to increase participation in organized screening programmes, particularly for cervical and colorectal cancer among women from Eastern Europe, Asia, and Latin America. Strengthening infection-control and prevention initiatives—such as HBV and HCV screening, Helicobacter pylori eradication, and HPV vaccination—should be integrated into broader cancer-prevention frameworks. In parallel, enhancing data-collection and surveillance systems to systematically capture migrant status, region of origin, duration of residence, stage at diagnosis, and survival outcomes is essential to better understand disparities and evaluate intervention effectiveness. Reducing barriers to timely diagnosis and treatment will require coordinated, multilevel interventions involving community organizations, primary care, and specialist oncology services. Finally, improved coordination within existing cancer-care networks, such as the ROC, could ensure that migrants are identified early and managed through multidisciplinary care pathways. Collectively, these actions may help mitigate inequities in cancer prevention, diagnosis, and treatment, ultimately improving outcomes for migrant populations in the Campania region and beyond. Future research Unlike population-based cancer registries, this study was based on data from the ROC platform, which monitors the entire patient journey within the regional oncology network—from initial referral and diagnosis to treatment and continuity of care in the community. This comprehensive approach provides valuable insight into real-world care pathways but does not allow for the estimation of cancer incidence or survival trends in the general population. Future research should therefore aim to complement ROC-derived information with registry-based epidemiological data to capture incidence, stage at diagnosis, and survival among migrants. Analyses disaggregated by length of stay, legal status, and region of origin will be essential to better understand heterogeneity within migrant populations. Moreover, studies evaluating targeted interventions to improve participation in screening programmes, promote early diagnosis, and enhance continuity of oncologic care for migrants are urgently needed. Conclusion In conclusion, within the Italian national and regional health system context, migrants are a growing but vulnerable population whose cancer-related health needs warrant dedicated attention. Their cancer patterns differ from native residents in a way that suggests both biological (infection and lifestyle exposures) and systemic (screening, access to care) drivers. Regional care networks such as ROC should incorporate precision public health approaches targeting vulnerable migrant populations to identify, monitor and address migrant-specific needs in cancer prevention, early detection, and management.
