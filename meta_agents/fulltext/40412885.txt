Introduction to artificial intelligence in healthcare Artificial intelligence (AI) is the science of building machines that can perform tasks usually requiring human intelligence [ 36 , 73 ]. While narrow AI applications like spam filtering and language translation have been part of everyday life for some time, recent advancements in the AI field suggest we may see Artificial General Intelligence emerge in the next few decades [ 33 ]. In medicine, AI offers transformative potential by improving diagnostics, patient management, resource allocation, and administrative tasks [ 11 , 92 ]. Supervised machine learning, which analyzes labeled data automatically, has become essential in clinical AI research over the last ten years [ 48 ]. Image recognition models using this technique have reached diagnostic accuracy comparable to that of humans in identifying pneumonia on chest X-rays, diabetic retinopathy in fundus photos, and intracranial hemorrhage in CT scans [ 34 , 42 , 46 ]. However, clinical use is still limited by the need for large labeled datasets, challenges in integrating various types of data, and complex decision-making processes [ 28 , 41 ]. Recent research shows significant progress in addressing these issues, as shown in Fig. 1 . Projects like the International Skin Imaging Collaboration have led to the creation of large, standardized dermatoscopic datasets. This shift has moved research from proof-of-concept toward real clinical evaluation [ 71 , 95 ]. In dermatological oncology, deep learning frameworks, especially convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and vision transformers, have shown performance comparable to dermatologists and improved prognostic capabilities through the integration of dermoscopic, histopathologic, genomic, and clinical data [ 21 ]. These advancements offer great promise for early detection, risk assessment, and tailored treatment in skin cancer management. Additionally, considering patient metadata and using explainability techniques such as Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) have improved how we interpret models [ 68 , 71 ]. A key moment came in 2020 with the approval of the uAI Derma system, which supports AI-based diagnosis of 12 skin diseases and represents an important step toward clinical integration [ 92 ]. Current studies emphasize the increasing importance of AI in dermatology. Advanced deep learning systems are now consistently performing well in both diagnosing and predicting outcomes for skin cancers [ 84 ] [PMID: 39225214]; [ 50 ] [PMID: 40231527]). Detailed reviews further show that adding AI-driven models to clinical practices can decrease variability among observers and standardize how patients are managed (PMID: 36892022; [ 67 ]. Overall, these developments underline AI’s growing role as an effective and intelligent diagnostic tool to help address the global challenge of skin cancer. This review brings together current evidence on AI-driven methods in skin cancer diagnosis and prognosis, focusing on deep learning models, integrating various data types, adjusting clinical workflows, regulatory advancements, ongoing challenges, and future directions for fair clinical deployment. Public availability of large and diverse datasets has been crucial for training robust models [ 18 ]. In skin cancer, multiple high-quality dermoscopic image repositories exist, most prominently the International Skin Imaging Collaboration (ISIC) archive, which provides over 25,000 expert-annotated dermoscopy images together with segmentation masks, diagnostic labels, and additional metadata [ 18 ]. The ISIC dataset is continuously expanded, with recent releases incorporating dermoscopic attributes, clinical indications, and, in some cases, genetic information. AI models in skin cancer leverage multiple data modalities. Dermoscopy is a non-invasive imaging technique central to early melanoma detection and underpins initiatives such as the ISIC project and related machine learning challenges organized by the University of Pennsylvania. For dermoscopic analysis, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are the most widely used architectures due to their strong performance and reasonable computational cost [ 21 , 27 ]. Histopathology, the gold standard for cancer diagnosis, provides high-resolution tissue images that enable detailed assessment of tumor architecture and margins. Genomic profiling adds molecular-level information for diagnosis and prediction of disease progression, which can be mined by deep learning models to detect subtle patterns associated with prognosis [ 44 ]. AI architectures, workflows, and clinical applications Across medical imaging, deep learning algorithms support top-notch image classification, especially in fields like radiology, ophthalmology, infectious diseases, and dermatology [ 44 , 48 ]. These models automatically learn hierarchical feature representations from data, which removes the need for custom features or specific programming [ 48 ]. In skin cancer imaging, dermoscopic classification pipelines usually involve segmenting lesions and then classifying the segmented area of interest (ROI) [ 21 ]. Fully convolutional networks (FCNs) and architectures like U-Net are commonly used for accurate lesion boundary detection [ 52 , 69 ]. The segmented ROI is then examined by classifiers based on CNNs or Vision Transformers (ViTs), which model long-range dependencies through self-attention mechanisms [ 25 , 99 ]. Hybrid models incorporate attention blocks within convolutional backbones to merge local feature extraction with global context [ 106 ], while ensemble strategies combine various CNN and ViT models to lower variance and improve robustness [ 24 ]. CNNs identify local visual patterns like edges and textures using convolutional kernels. They preserve spatial structure and remain robust to minor distortions [ 32 , 45 , 47 ]. Transformer-based models, on the other hand, detect global relationships among image patches through stacked attention layers [ 99 ]. AI applications in skin cancer diagnosis Skin cancer is the most common cancer worldwide, with over 1.5 million new cases diagnosed each year in the USA alone. Melanoma makes up over 90% of deaths linked to skin cancer [ 6 , 104 ]. Getting an accurate diagnosis is essential for effective treatment and management. Artificial intelligence (AI) has grown quickly in healthcare, especially in dermatology and radiology. Deep learning algorithms now perform diagnoses as well as, or even better than, clinicians for certain cancers [ 37 ]. AI applications in skin cancer include dermoscopy images, histopathologic slides, 3D total-body imaging, clinical data, and genomics [ 49 ]. This review combines findings from public datasets, studies on melanoma and non-melanoma, and research on prognosis and risk. It covers the entire workflow, from taking images to deploying AI models [ 75 ]. Large public datasets and initiatives like the International Skin Imaging Collaboration (ISIC) have accelerated AI growth in dermatology [ 18 , 21 ]. Since the ImageNet breakthrough in 2012, models like convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and transformers have significantly improved performance, as shown in Fig. 4 [ 45 , 99 ]. In fact, 34% of top systems achieve dermatologist-level accuracy, while 51% exceed it [ 13 ]. The COVID-19 pandemic also drove the uptake of AI-enabled telemedicine and teledermatology, making remote consultations and early skin cancer screenings easier [ 75 ]. Deep learning in dermatoscopic image analysis Deep learning has produced impressive results in detecting melanoma from dermatoscopic images, outperforming clinical smartphone images [ 23 ]. Advances in Vision Transformer models show promise for using clinical photos in early screenings [ 43 , 111 ]. During the pandemic, there was a sharp increase in the use of smartphone-based teledermatology and AI decision support [ 51 ]. While dermatoscopic images remain the gold standard for diagnosis, clinical photos are helpful for prognosis and ongoing monitoring of high-risk patients [ 38 ]. Currently, computer-assisted diagnosis (CAD) systems that combine AI with dermatologist reviews are favored for melanoma classification [ 21 ]. For example, Google’s deep-learning system for diabetic retinopathy has reached ophthalmologist-level accuracy [ 34 ], showcasing its broader applicability. In dermatology, deep CNN-based systems like ResNet-152, trained using ISIC datasets, are increasingly available for diagnosing melanoma [ 21 ]. Mobile platforms like Apps4Melanoma provide real-time evaluation of lesions through smartphones (Apps4Melanoma). Smartphone apps and teledermatology Smartphone apps for assessing melanoma risk became common during the pandemic, although most lack strong clinical validation [ 83 ]. SkinVision gives risk estimates with reported accuracy between 85% and 95%, while VerifyEDx has FDA approval for risk assessment. Teledermatology improves access to care, with diagnostic sensitivity reported between 86% and 100% [ 51 , 54 ]. Teledermoscopy systems allow cost-effective triage and remote management of suspicious lesions. Integrating telemedicine and CAD systems Smartphone photography and teledermatology work well with traditional CAD systems by increasing access to expert evaluation [ 51 ]. However, differences in image types, such as between dermatoscopic and close-up clinical photos, limit the interchangeability of current AI models [ 23 , 93 ]. While smartphone tools show promise for early detection and self-monitoring, more research and validation are necessary for ensuring clinical reliability. AI in skin cancer prognosis Cancer prognosis refers to predicting the evolution of tumors, i.e., the patients’ expected response to treatments and expected survival time. Prognostic models allow patient risk stratification by recurrence and survival chances and help in choosing more personalized treatments. In skin cancer, asking deep learning models about patient survival and recurrence risks is more challenging than recognized: answers can be used to shape follow-up scheduling, and missing them leads to harmful overtreatments. In melanoma, for example, accurate recurrence and survival predictions contribute to treatment decisions and follow-up scheduling, maximizing care and minimizing unnecessary visits (Fig. 5 ). Minimally invasive techniques based on gene-expression tests for predicting the prognosis of cutaneous melanoma have been recently explored. Despite remarkable results, these prognostic tools remain partially utilized in clinical practice due to the lack of validated, publicly available, clinically annotated gene-expression datasets. Recent deep learning studies propose alternative approaches using non-invasive standard-of-care hematoxylin and eosin (H E) stained histopathology images, which are ubiquitously available in early medical care and directly correlated with biological features [ 4 ]. Experimental results demonstrate that clinicopathologic models trained on the TCGA-SKCM dataset and validated on three independent clinical cohorts from Colombia can predict recurrence risk and provide valuable patient stratification in terms of overall and progression-free survival regardless of tumor stage (deep learning histology prognosis studies; [ 4 , 20 ]. Fig. 5 AI in skin cancer prognosis: from image and genomic analysis to risk stratification, survival prediction, and personalized treatment Predicting recurrence, metastasis, and survival Studies show that machine learning (ML) models can predict the recurrence and spread of nonmelanoma skin cancers (NMSC), including squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and basal cell carcinoma (BCC). These models can lead to more personalized treatment plans for high-risk patients, improving preventive care and follow-up strategies (NMSC prognosis literature; [ 4 ]. By assessing recurrence and metastasis risks accurately, these models can also help allocate resources better, improve patient outcomes, and lower morbidity. However, effectively identifying high-risk SCC and BCC patients requires a thorough evaluation of past clinical data, since prognostic factors for NMSC are not well defined. Without clear prognostic indicators, current treatment guidelines depend mainly on expert opinion [ 23 ]. Over the years, researchers have used various ML methods for melanoma diagnosis and prognosis. The most common approach involves deep learning models trained on public skin lesion image datasets [ 21 ]. Other methods use multimodal data, such as clinical, demographic, and histopathological information, to improve prediction accuracy (GEP literature; [ 4 ]. Most studies focus on predicting recurrence and survival, but some also look at staging, identifying risk factors, guiding treatment, and assessing sentinel nodes. A continuing issue is bias from limited data diversity, which can lead to inaccurate prognostic estimates [ 43 ] ISIC analyses). Integrating imaging with clinical and molecular data Skin cancer prognosis involves complex tasks like assessing recurrence risk and predicting survival. Deep learning models that process various inputs—including clinical metadata, dermoscopic and histopathologic images, and genomic profiles—can create individualized risk assessments to guide treatment and follow-up care [ 16 , 29 ]. When used on histopathologic images, AI models usually predict recurrence or mortality risk either categorically (e.g., high vs. low risk) or in terms of survival time [ 107 ]. These models often incorporate metadata like age and sex, as well as genomic information when available [ 20 , 102 ]. The analysis process generally involves cropping whole-slide images into smaller patches, using convolutional neural networks to extract features, and combining these image-derived features with clinical and genomic data for comprehensive patient-level prognostic predictions [ 16 , 39 , 60 ]. Risk stratification models A high-risk population includes individuals who are much more likely to develop a specific health condition compared to the general population. Risk stratification looks at modifiable risk factors, genetic predisposition, and other relevant variables. In skin cancer, prognostic models can help stratify newly diagnosed patients and guide treatment decisions. This process assists in customizing adjuvant therapy, defining follow-up schedules, and determining the need for urgent care [ 107 ]. Traditional statistical methods often have difficulty integrating the complex clinical and histologic variables that affect melanoma progression. This makes accurate outcome prediction at the individual level challenging. Machine learning (ML) can handle many variables at once to improve prognostic accuracy [ 20 ]. However, there are still relatively few AI-based models for skin cancer. The existing models usually combine histopathologic, demographic, clinical, and genetic markers to create personalized risk assessments [ 29 , 107 ]. Personalized treatment guidance through AI-based prognostic tools Cutaneous melanoma develops from melanocytes in the epidermal basal layer, which are the pigment-producing cells responsible for skin, eye, and hair color. As the fourth most common cancer worldwide, melanoma is the most aggressive and deadly type of skin cancer [ 88 ]. Its 5-year survival rate can reach 92.5%, but it accounts for over 80% of deaths related to skin cancer [ 29 ]. The most effective treatment is surgical removal of the primary tumor, followed by immune or targeted therapy. Unfortunately, the prognosis worsens significantly in advanced stages, with 5-year survival rates dropping to 53.8% for regional metastases and 15.9% for distant metastases. Late-stage diagnoses often complicate melanoma treatment. AI-driven prognostic stratification can lead to personalized medicine by identifying patients who may need increased follow-up and customized treatment. Predictive models can assist in selecting therapies, guiding follow-up procedures, and helping to prevent disease progression. In addition, accurate survival predictions allow patients to make informed decisions about their lives and care [ 107 ]. AI-enhanced integration of functional imaging in treatment planning Current AI applications in skin cancer mainly focus on diagnostic imaging and histopathologic evaluation. However, functional imaging, especially 18 F-FDG PET-CT, is showing promise in radiotherapy (RT) planning for advanced or metastatic melanoma. Insights from non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) highlight its potential: PET-CT improves target volume delineation by finding hidden metastases in 20.6% of cases and leads to changes in RT plans in 76.5% of cases by better detecting primary (66.7%) and nodal (43.3%) disease [ 63 ]. In melanoma, PET-CT addresses the limitations of CT-only planning by revealing metabolically active areas, clarifying unclear findings, and showing extra nodal involvement (as much as 35.3%). This allows for dose escalation to affected areas while protecting normal tissue. With AI enhancement, PET-CT segmentation, multimodal data fusion (including dermoscopic, histopathologic, and genomic data), and radiomics-based response prediction can further increase precision and lessen variability in gross tumor volume (GTV) contouring. Early studies indicate that PET-CT–guided RT can improve locoregional control and shift up to 11.8% of oligometastatic cases from palliative to radical treatment approaches. AI-driven adaptive planning and dose personalization may boost these advantages. Future prospective validation of AI-integrated workflows is needed to optimize outcomes for high-risk melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC) patients. Challenges in implementing AI solutions Despite the many benefits of AI and ML in healthcare, particularly in skin cancer diagnostics and prognostics, several challenges remain. Firstly, data-related issues such as poor data quantity and quality impose constraints on the development of AI models. To overcome this issue, several approaches have been proposed, including data augmentation, data synthesis using generative adversarial networks (GANs) [ 31 ], transfer learning, and capsule networks. Secondly, the lack of model interpretability reduces the trust in and, hence, acceptance of the AI models as part of real-life clinical decision-making. To enhance model explainability, a wide variety of approaches, such as Grad-CAM [ 79 ], saliency maps [ 82 ], and layer-wise relevance propagation [ 8 ], have been proposed and are being extensively implemented in AI models for skin cancer diagnosis and prognosis. Beyond the aforementioned challenges, regulatory and legal-related issues must also be addressed. Each AI solution must undergo regulatory approval to determine its suitability for deployment. Data handling in the development phase and in the eventual real-life deployment of an AI model must adhere to patient confidentiality and privacy regulations. Finally, the existence of healthcare disparities, manifesting as an unequal distribution of healthcare resources, diagnostics, and treatment procedures, further exacerbates the challenge of implementing AI solutions. Since AI models are often trained with data from regions with abundant healthcare assets, these models can suffer from a lack of generalizability when deployed in underserved regions [ 16 , 20 ]. Data quality and availability Deep learning (DL) image recognition techniques depend on large, high-quality datasets for effective training. Limited access to such data has slowed the development of computer-aided diagnostic models in clinical and research contexts. Several initiatives have addressed this gap by improving dermoscopic datasets, including HAM10000 [ 93 ], BCN20000 [ 22 ]/2024), MoleMap, the Interactive Atlas of Dermoscopy, ISM—the Dermoscopic Atlas of Melanocytic Tumors of the Skin, Dermofit Image Library, MED-NODE, and PH2 [ 57 ]. The ISIC archive has played a crucial role in dataset curation and benchmarking, offering over 23,000 dermoscopic cases through its archives and annual challenges [ 18 ]. Since 2019, these datasets have included dermoscopic images along with clinical and follow-up metadata for each lesion. Generative adversarial networks (GANs) also help by augmenting datasets, which improves model training and performance [ 31 ]. However, labeling quality remains a concern because general practitioners often perform annotations instead of dermatology specialists [ 93 ]. Histopathological datasets like BreakHis [ 85 ] have also grown and are widely used for benchmark evaluation. Additional repositories are mentioned in reviews on computational pathology [ 16 ], but they still have limited volume. Projects like The Cancer Genome Atlas [ 102 ] have improved access to genomic data, yet its availability still lags behind that of imaging datasets. As a result, recent genomic classification research increasingly uses a multimodal approach that combines gene expression profiles with imaging data [ 20 ]. Model interpretability and “black-box” problem DL models have shown strong performance in skin cancer diagnosis [ 27 , 95 ], but their decision-making processes are often unclear and referred to as “black boxes” [ 72 , 76 ]. Several reviews have explored methods to improve explainability, including surveys by [ 10 , 17 , 101 ] categorized these approaches into four types: text-based explanations, visualizations, local explanations, and example-based explanations. More recent overviews have expanded these ideas to interpretable AI in biomedical contexts [ 72 ]. Explainability algorithms like heatmaps and image gradient visualizations have been integrated into skin cancer models to highlight the areas that influence model predictions. While these methods assist in medical interpretation, thorough evaluations of post-hoc explainability tools are still lacking [ 1 , 90 ]. Segmenting relevant skin structures can improve understanding for dermatologists [ 95 ], creating a clearer link between model outputs and clinical features. Alternatively, using confirmation or penalization functions—similar to landmark-based face recognition systems—can focus on important regions during training, enhancing both transparency and performance [ 103 ]. Regulatory, ethical, and legal considerations Societal and regulatory factors greatly affect the clinical adoption of AI in dermatology. While DL-based dermatoscopic systems often match or exceed human experts [ 27 , 95 ], integrating them into practice remains challenging. Data privacy issues, especially those governed by the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the European Union, are critical. Ethical and regulatory standards increasingly demand interpretable AI to ensure accountability and fairness [ 10 ]. Furthermore, uneven distribution of AI resources could worsen healthcare inequalities, favoring areas where such technologies are developed. Despite AI’s success in areas like mammography screening [ 55 ], few computer-aided diagnostic or PrognosTILs tools for skin cancer have been implemented in clinical settings. Currently, none have received regulatory approval from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration or the European Medical Device Directive [ 105 ]. Overcoming this challenge requires thorough quality assessment, systematic validation, and prospective clinical trials to evaluate AI performance in real-world situations [ 10 , 95 , 100 ]. Limitations of AI in skin cancer diagnosis AI models for skin cancer detection, while achieving high accuracy on benchmark datasets, frequently underperform in real-world scenarios due to dataset biases and poor generalizability. Most training data derives from fair-skinned populations (Fitzpatrick types I-III), leading to failure in recognizing atypical presentations in darker skin tones (types V-VI), where melanomas often appear acral or amelanotic [ 16 , 20 ]. Smartphone apps like SkinVision exhibit low sensitivity on dermatoscopic benchmarks and misclassify high-risk lesions, as clinical images differ fundamentally from controlled dermoscopy [ 83 , 93 ]. Unsuccessful cases highlight overfitting to artifacts: models trained on ISIC data misinterpret rulers or skin markings as malignant features, inflating false positives by up to 28% [ 21 , 94 ]. Rare cancers like Merkel cell carcinoma (MCC) and amelanotic melanoma evade detection entirely; one app failed to rank MCC in the top diagnoses for all 28 cases, while another misclassified 18–23% as low-risk [ 2 , 105 ]. Prospective trials reveal further gaps: algorithms correctly flagged only 6–11% of novel categories absent from training, triggering unnecessary biopsies for 47% of benign lesions [ 83 ].​ Technical and operational challenges Black-box opacity undermines clinical trust, as explainability tools like Grad-CAM often produce misleading saliency maps without validating medical relevance [ 79 , 90 ]. Image noise, low contrast, and artifacts from varying devices degrade performance, with missing data in 30–40% of multicenter trials due to equipment failures [ 28 , 41 ]. Multimodal integration falters on heterogeneous inputs—genomics and histopathology rarely align with dermoscopy—exacerbating overfitting in small cohorts [ 20 , 98 ]. Regulatory and ethical hurdles compound issues: few tools like MelaFind gained FDA approval but failed market due to negligible clinical benefit, while unvalidated apps risk disparities in low-resource settings [ 100 , 105 ]. Meta-analyses confirm AI lags dermatologists in prospective, diverse evaluations, with pooled AUC dropping below 0.90 for out-of-distribution data [ 75 , 110 ]. Economic feasibility and resource-efficient deployment Skin cancer remains the most prevalent yet preventable malignancy worldwide, with over 5 million adults treated annually in the United States alone, incurring healthcare costs exceeding USD 8.1 billion [ 35 ]. While early detection is pivotal for a favorable prognosis and cost reduction, late-stage diagnoses significantly escalate both mortality and financial burdens. Artificial intelligence (AI) offers a transformative opportunity to mitigate these costs and reduce healthcare disparities by enhancing diagnostic accessibility through mobile applications and automated systems. However, the economic viability of these technologies requires rigorous validation through cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) to justify their integration into routine clinical workflows [ 70 ]. Recent economic evaluations present a nuanced landscape dependent on deployment strategy and model performance. AI-driven decision support systems have demonstrated potential as cost-saving interventions; a microsimulation study comparing AI support with standard care reported a lower mean cost per patient ($750 vs. $759) while maintaining comparable Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALYs) [ 70 ]. Conversely, the economic impact is sensitive to algorithmic specificity. A population-based study of an AI-powered screening app in the Netherlands revealed an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) of €2,567 per malignancy detected but associated this with a 32% surge in healthcare consumption due to false-positive referrals [ 83 ]. This highlights a critical risk: without high specificity and regulatory oversight—as emphasized by FDA guidelines on automated image assessment—AI tools may overburden healthcare systems with unnecessary biopsies and consultations rather than alleviating strain [ 100 , 105 ]. To maximize economic feasibility and global applicability, particularly in low-to-middle-income countries (LMICs), deployment must prioritize resource-efficient strategies: AI-Triage and Hybrid Workflows: Implementing AI as a triage tool in primary care can filter clearly benign cases, potentially reducing specialist referrals by up to 50% [ 100 ]. A “human-in-the-loop” model, where AI serves as a second opinion, currently represents the most viable approach to balance diagnostic sensitivity with the need to minimize costly false positives. Teledermatology Integration: Coupling AI with teledermatology significantly lowers indirect patient costs, such as travel and lost wages. In high-burden settings like Brazil, economic modeling has identified AI-assisted diagnosis as a dominant strategy—simultaneously more effective and less costly—with a negative cost-effectiveness ratio of –US$27,580 per QALY [ 78 ]. Comprehensive Risk Stratification: Beyond imaging, integrating clinical and genomic data into AI algorithms can further optimize resource allocation by predicting recurrence risks and mortality, thereby tailoring surveillance intensity to patient need [ 44 ]. Ultimately, transitioning from siloed, high-tech applications to scalable software solutions capable of running on consumer-grade smartphones is essential to democratize access and ensure the financial sustainability of AI in global dermatology. Stakeholder acceptance and trust Stakeholder acceptance of AI-based tools is emerging as a critical determinant of real-world implementation in dermatology, yet empirical data remain relatively sparse and heterogeneous. Recent patient surveys in dermatology clinics report generally positive attitudes toward AI, with over half of respondents expressing a favorable view of its use and large majorities expecting AI to increase diagnostic speed and accuracy [ 83 ]. However, when faced with suspected skin cancer, most patients still strongly prefer a dermatologist’s judgment over an autonomous algorithm, and only a small minority are willing to accept standalone AI decisions [ 77 ]. Across multiple studies, patients consistently indicate that their preferred scenario is a dermatologist supported by AI rather than AI replacing the clinician, and they often require AI to be substantially more accurate than human experts before they would consider AI-only evaluations acceptable [ 58 , 110 ]. Survey data also highlight specific concerns that may impede patient acceptance, including fears about privacy breaches, secondary uses of image data, potential de-skilling of clinicians, and erosion of the doctor–patient relationship [ 59 , 108 ]. Familiarity with AI appears to modulate these perceptions; patients who report prior exposure to or understanding of AI are more likely to believe that it can safely augment dermatologists and improve diagnostic accuracy [ 41 ]. These findings suggest that transparent communication about how skin images are processed, how AI outputs are validated, and how responsibility is shared between algorithms and clinicians will be essential for building patient trust in AI-assisted skin cancer pathways [ 76 , 91 ]. Clinician perspectives similarly indicate cautious optimism rather than unconditional trust. In a recent survey of dermatologists, most respondents agreed that AI will improve dermatology and should be integrated into training, yet they predominantly viewed AI as a decision-support tool rather than a replacement for specialist expertise, citing concerns about liability, bias, and loss of clinical skills [ 95 , 100 ]. Experimental studies of human–AI interaction further show that dermatologists’ diagnostic accuracy and confidence improve when AI assistance is provided, particularly when the system offers clinically meaningful, “dermatologist-like” explanations of its predictions, which in turn enhances trust in the tool [ 19 ]. Together, these early data underscore that successful adoption of AI for skin cancer diagnosis and prognosis will depend not only on technical performance but also on co-designed workflows, explainable interfaces, and prospective studies that explicitly measure patient and clinician satisfaction, trust, and willingness to rely on AI in routine care [ 58 , 76 ]. Reproducibility and standardized evaluation Despite the rapid expansion of AI applications in skin cancer, most published models are difficult to reproduce because of heterogeneous datasets, inconsistent train–validation–test splits, and incomplete reporting of preprocessing, hyperparameters, and threshold selection. Moreover, studies frequently report different performance metrics (e.g., accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, AUC, balanced accuracy) and evaluate on distinct internal or partially overlapping public cohorts, which hampers direct comparison across algorithms and raises concerns about true clinical robustness. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of melanoma imaging algorithms have highlighted that this methodological variability, together with small or single-center samples, contributes to optimistic performance estimates and limited generalizability in real-world settings [ 110 ]. To strengthen reproducibility, future work in AI for skin cancer should adopt unified assessment frameworks that specify a minimal set of metrics (including class-wise sensitivity, specificity, balanced accuracy, AUC, calibration, and confusion matrices at clinically relevant thresholds) and follow emerging reporting checklists for dermatology image-based AI, such as the CLEAR Derm recommendations. In parallel, there is a critical need for shared, well-annotated validation datasets with fixed public train–validation–test partitions, building on initiatives like the ISIC archive and related dermoscopic repositories, and extending them to multi-center cohorts that better represent diverse skin tones, imaging devices, and care settings [ 18 , 21 ]. Multicenter external validation—whether via centralized test sets or privacy-preserving federated learning—should become standard practice so that different groups can benchmark models under identical conditions, facilitating rigorous head-to-head comparison and accelerating the translation of truly reproducible AI tools into dermatological practice [ 80 ]. Technical hurdles in multimodal data integration While multimodal AI models offer superior diagnostic precision by synthesizing imaging, genomic, and clinical data, their development is impeded by significant technical barriers that are often understated. The integration of heterogeneous data sources introduces complex challenges in data compatibility, quality control, and computational resource management [ 20 , 98 ]. Data compatibility and heterogeneity A primary barrier is the inherent incompatibility between diverse data modalities. Dermoscopic and histopathological images differ fundamentally in structure and dimensionality from tabular clinical records and high-dimensional genomic profiles [ 20 ]. Images exist as unstructured pixel arrays requiring spatial feature extraction (e.g., via CNNs or ViTs), whereas genomic data (e.g., RNA-seq or mutation panels) function as high-dimensional vectors, and clinical metadata typically consists of sparse, categorical, or numerical values. Harmonizing these disparate formats into a unified latent space for fusion is technically demanding. Misalignment in data representation can lead to model bias, where one modality dominates the decision-making process simply due to its feature density or magnitude, effectively negating the benefits of integration [ 86 , 110 ]. Quality control and missing modalities Multimodal datasets are uniquely vulnerable to quality control issues, particularly the problem of “missingness.” In real-world clinical settings, it is rare for a single patient to have a complete set of high-quality dermoscopy, histopathology, and genomic data. Standard deep learning models often fail when input channels are incomplete, necessitating complex imputation strategies or flexible architecture designs that can handle absent modalities without performance degradation [ 4 ]. Furthermore, noise varies across modalities; for instance, histopathology slides may suffer from staining artifacts or scanning variations, while genomic data may contain batch effects. Ensuring quality consistency across all channels simultaneously is far more complex than in unimodal pipelines, as aggressive filtering in one modality may drastically reduce the available sample size for the entire multimodal cohort [ 16 ]. Computational demands and scalability The computational cost of training multimodal models is prohibitive for widespread deployment. Processing gigapixel whole-slide images (WSI) for histopathology alongside high-throughput genomic sequences requires substantial GPU memory and storage bandwidth [ 20 ]. Advanced fusion techniques, such as cross-modal attention mechanisms (which learn the relationships between specific image regions and gene expression patterns), scale quadratically with input size, creating a bottleneck for training and inference. This computational burden limits the feasibility of deploying such comprehensive AI systems in resource-constrained environments or on edge devices (e.g., standard clinical workstations or mobile platforms), which contrasts with the lightweight efficiency required for global health applications [ 86 , 105 ]. Geographic disparities and global access AI technologies for skin cancer diagnosis exhibit stark geographic disparities, with high-income countries (HICs) like the USA, Europe, and Australia leading in development, regulatory approvals (e.g., FDA-cleared tools like DermaSensor), and clinical integration, while low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) face severe limitations in access and applicability [ 100 ]. In HICs, advanced infrastructure supports widespread use of dermoscopy-linked AI, teledermatology apps (e.g., SkinVision), and large datasets like ISIC, enabling dermatologist-level accuracy and reducing specialist referrals [ 83 ]. Conversely, LMICs—home to diverse skin tones and higher burdens of neglected tropical skin diseases—lack dermatologists (e.g., only 28% of US counties have access, worse globally), robust digital infrastructure, and context-specific tools, exacerbating late diagnoses despite lower melanoma incidence [ 5 , 77 ].​ Key barriers in underserved regions Dataset biases dominate inequities: Most AI models (e.g., trained on HAM10000 or ISIC) overrepresent fair-skinned populations from HICs, leading to 10–20% lower accuracy for darker phototypes prevalent in Africa, Asia, and Latin America, as seen in Colombian validations [ 4 , 21 , 93 ]. Rural and low-resource areas encounter additional hurdles like high costs, poor internet, device incompatibility, and privacy concerns, limiting smartphone-based apps despite their promise for triage [ 83 , 108 ]. Regulatory gaps further hinder LMIC adoption; while HICs have cleared 1,000 + AI devices by 2025, LMICs rely on unvalidated imports prone to failure on local skin types and infections (Table 4 ) [ 100 ]. Future perspectives of AI in skin cancer management Multimodal AI for skin cancer prognostication The combination of multimodal AI algorithms has become a key focus in skin cancer prognosis research. Early models used clinical and histopathological data. Recent advancements now include genomics, imaging, and patient information over time. These developments allow for a better assessment of disease recurrence and survival outcomes [ 98 ]. Low-dimensional disease risk models constructed from standard dermoscopy images aid personalized treatment planning, providing population-level risk stratification for melanoma recurrence. Despite this progress, image-based diagnostic methods are still limited. Prognosis and post-operative risk evaluation depend significantly on multimodal clinical data, which includes demographics, histopathology, genomics, and patient history. Therefore, current research is looking into molecular signatures combined with clinical and imaging information to predict recurrence and survival. Although molecular profiling is highly predictive, its cost and turnaround time limit its use in clinical settings. New multimodal frameworks that integrate molecular, histological, and clinical data now allow for better estimates of key indicators like Breslow thickness, improving efficiency and lowering costs [ 98 ]. These studies highlight the need for strong strategies to combine different data sources effectively. Computer-aided diagnostic (CAD) systems have evolved to include genetics, clinical measures, and various imaging types to improve cancer prognosis and treatment response evaluation. In skin cancer, these multimodal systems play a critical role in patient risk assessment and post-surgical guidance [ 44 , 98 ]. Federated learning and privacy-preserving AI The rapid increase in medical data raises important issues about patient privacy and data security [ 59 , 108 ]. Regulations like the EU’s GDPR set the ethical and legal limits on how medical data can be used. To navigate these challenges, privacy-preserving learning frameworks, especially federated learning (FL), have gained attention in healthcare AI [ 89 , 97 , 108 ]. FL allows machine learning models to be trained across many institutions without transferring data. Model parameters are updated locally, centralized for aggregation, and then redistributed for further training. This method helps keep data confidential while using diverse, large datasets to enhance the model’s strength and adaptability [ 89 , 108 ]. This approach was especially useful during the COVID-19 pandemic, when sharing data between institutions faced logistical and ethical difficulties [ 97 ]. To strengthen privacy, FL often includes additional techniques like differential privacy. This adds mathematically defined noise to the training process to prevent model inversion and membership inference attacks, allowing secure pattern learning from distributed datasets without revealing identifiable information [ 64 , 109 ]. Together, multimodal AI and privacy-preserving frameworks like FL enhance the scalability and ethical use of AI in dermatological research and clinical practice, providing better diagnostics and prognostics beyond specialized facilities [ 10 ]. Internet of things (IoT) integration The Internet of Things (IoT) has transformed modern healthcare by creating interconnected systems for continuous data collection and remote patient management [ 62 ]. In dermatology, IoT enables real-time monitoring of skin cancer with wearable sensors and smartphone-based dermoscopes that capture detailed images of lesions, UV exposure information, and skin measurements for automated risk assessment. These data streams connect smoothly with teledermatology networks and cloud-based AI platforms, allowing for early diagnoses in underserved areas and reducing delays in clinical decisions. IoT applications linked to ISIC datasets now enable self-monitoring of suspicious moles using smartphone attachments and edge computing for initial AI-based risk assessment prior to specialist evaluation [ 21 ]. While these technologies improve screening access and encourage proactive monitoring in high-risk groups, issues persist regarding data privacy, device compatibility, and regulatory approval for clinical use [ 62 , 105 ]. The future integration of IoT with multimodal AI analysis is expected to enhance personalized and preventive dermatologic care. Explainable AI and trust-building with clinicians Skin cancer, particularly malignant melanoma, remains one of the most common cancers worldwide, making early detection vital for patient survival [ 5 , 66 , 88 ]. Artificial intelligence, especially deep learning, has become essential in dermatologic diagnostics, treatment planning, and prognosis prediction [ 14 ]. However, the widespread use of these models in clinical settings requires transparency, clarity, and clinician trust [ 76 , 91 ]. Explainable AI (XAI) seeks to overcome the problems of “black-box” algorithms by offering visual and quantitative explanations of predictions. For example, saliency maps can highlight important dermoscopic features that guide decision-making [ 1 , 19 ]. Despite significant progress, most XAI research in dermatology mainly focuses on image-level explanations, often neglecting patient-specific clinical variables [ 14 , 19 ]. This narrow focus limits clinical trust, as healthcare providers consider both lesion characteristics and patient context. Combining image and clinical insights into a single explainable framework is a crucial future direction for AI in dermatology. Role of AI in global health and low-resource settings High death rates and treatment costs linked to metastatic skin cancer create challenges in areas with limited resources [ 5 ]. Even though the disease often has visible signs, unequal access to healthcare, social disparities, and a lack of specialists make early detection and treatment difficult [ 5 , 81 ]. AI-driven teledermatology can help address these challenges by improving workflow efficiency, allowing automated triage of benign lesions, and prioritizing high-risk cases for dermatologist evaluation [ 77 ]. Mobile AI diagnostic tools further broaden these advantages by facilitating large-scale screenings and risk assessments through simple image uploads. These applications have improved dermatological access in remote areas, where many consultations come from regions that lack formal telehealth systems [ 77 ]. Such developments show how AI can help reduce healthcare disparities, enhance survival rates, and support global efforts to control melanoma [ 5 , 14 ]. AI in preventive dermatology and public screening campaigns Although wound healing is a common issue in dermatology, the underlying mechanisms are still largely unexplored [ 87 ]. New research on wound healing is starting to appear, analyzing healing processes and agents using AI models [ 56 ]. One study uses an AI model to assess nearly 5 million chemical compounds for wound healing, aiming to find new drugs that could speed up the healing process (example of large-scale computational screening; see related AI-driven compound-screening literature). Agents that promote wound healing are also being studied, with an AI model detecting these agents through a public platform that serves as a guide [ 87 ]. Performance is a crucial milestone in wound healing research. One study looked at using activity efficiency to speed up the healing process passively through external electric fields (bioengineering and computational modeling literature; see recent reviews). The shape and frequency of the electric field are adjusted to enhance cellular activity related to healing (engineering and biophysics studies). Wound detection and localization techniques are also being applied to cancer detection. Another study suggests combining meta-learning with unsupervised learning to improve cancer detection by enhancing the feature representations of meta-learning [ 30 , 65 ]. Case studies and success stories Several clinical trials and real-world pilots have demonstrated tangible benefits and practicality of AI-powered tools in skin cancer [ 58 ]. The first clinical study, irrespective of the anatomical site, performed at a primary care center in Spain, recently received FDA breakthrough device designation [ 100 ]. CASAnalysis, another AI framework, was also implemented in Spain and describes a clinical algorithm that submits suspicious images to a convolutional neural network (CNN): if the lesion is classified as high-risk, the patient is immediately referred to the hospital; if the lesion is low-risk, the patient is sent to a digital flow for remote assessment. The ISIC Challenge promotes AI progress and real-world use [ 3 ]. Through ISIC, DICOM systems can automatically generate structured diagnostic reports to enhance tele-dermatology and centralized care (ISIC, 2024). Deployment-specific frameworks have been developed for tele-dermatology control of local, intracranial, and gunshot hemorrhages, plus subsets of the most common tumors [ 86 ]. Smartphones have been emphasized for their potential in prevention and medical/family orientation by enabling integrated, accessible, low-threshold consultations [ 40 ]. Deployable smartphone-enabled AI frameworks guide early patient orientation—from self-examination, development of a suspicious lesion, medical orientation, biopsy, and treatment—to minimize morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs. Meta-analytic techniques have been extrapolated to summarize the effectiveness of teledermatology in diagnosing skin cancer [ 110 ]. Clinical trials and research findings Many clinical trials have been designed to explore the application of machine learning approaches in guiding personalized treatments for malignant neoplasms. The need for further technological development is highlighted by the fact that approximately half of these studies are still enrolling participants or have yet to start [ 58 ]. Ongoing research is investigating the role of AI in enhancing screening processes and in predicting patients’ prognosis [ 86 ]. Beyond academic interest, the translation of AI research into practical guidelines is evident in recent Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approvals of dermatology tools incorporating AI for clinical use in cancer diagnostics [ 100 ]. Fundamental research in healthcare-machine learning also encompasses meta-analyses of case-control studies and population-based research on risk factors for multiple cancer types [ 110 ]. The increasing adoption of AI-based devices in healthcare has underscored the need for a comprehensive assessment of the associated risks. Risk assessment involves the identification, analysis, and evaluation of potential risks linked to the use of an AI device in clinical practice, spanning both technological and medical considerations [ 100 ]. Developing dependable AI solutions requires strategies for data selection and information gathering, informed by insights from prior studies. Particular emphasis is placed on integrating systems for risk stratification and scoring with predictive prognostic models that employ diverse data sources and supervised learning techniques [ 86 ]. Various methodological approaches harness data from multiple sources, with public image databases such as the International Skin Imaging Collaboration (ISIC) repository playing a pivotal role in testing and validating developed methods [ 3 ]. Landmark studies and benchmark datasets (e.g., ISIC) Several landmark studies focused on melanoma classification from dermoscopic pictures demonstrated that DL outperformed human experts [ 27 ]. In 2018, two independent teams showed that convolutional neural networks (CNNs) could classify histopathologic images of melanoma primary tumors at a performance level comparable with that of expert pathologists [ 86 ]. The availability of expert-labeled datasets represents a critical issue in the development of DL approaches for image classification in the field of melanoma. Extensive datasets were provided by the International Skin Imaging Collaboration (ISIC) archive, an initiative of the International Society of Digital Imaging of the Skin, a non-profit organization with the mission of improving melanoma diagnosis accuracy through digital images of skin lesions [ 3 ]. To date, the ISIC archive contains more than 25,000 dermoscopy images of pigmented lesions. Battery-powered digital dermatoscopes that can be connected to smartphones, such as the Cable Dermatoscope, iDermo ® , MoleScope ® , DermLite ® , and Dermoscop ® , can be used for screening, detection, and long-term follow-up. Several applications or apps can support clinicians in their decision-making process thanks to the availability of CAD analysis for the images acquired, such as ”SkinVision,” ”SkinCheck,” or ”SkinScan.” Combining teledermatology with the support of CAD analysis in teledermoscopic images can allow remote evaluation of pigmented skin lesions with satisfactory clinical accuracy for the early detection of melanoma [ 40 ]. FDA/CE-approved AI tools in dermatology Several AI-CAD tools for skin cancer diagnosis are commercially available. However, “previous FDA approvals have been limited regarding AI/ML image quality assessments of dermatological diagnostics (eg, dermoscopy or histopathology images)” [ 100 ]. The classification of pigmented skin lesions is the main goal of AI-CAD tools for skin cancer diagnosis, although “it does not represent the routine assessment of dermoscopic images by specialized dermatology experts” [ 58 ]. Interestingly, it is assumed that the AI-CAD device could be an aid for nondermatologists in a clinical setting [ 100 ]. Spes one can classify pigmented skin lesions into melanocytic and nonmelanocytic lesions; their area scanner permits CET 1 classification and risk assessment of the skin tumor (anamnestic data incorporated as well). Deep—and shallow—CNNs, actually “implemented for the detection of nonmelanoma skin cancers, commercially available for purchase or at an advanced stage of development, are trained for a binary classification task distinguishing between malignant and benign lesions. The VIDA tool is “cleared by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for assessing the malignancy of pigmented skin lesions in dermoscopic images” [ 100 ]. MedytoxDx, finally, offers a tool for classifying the malignancy of pigmented skin lesions and a tool for triaging skin lesions based on dermoscopic images [ 58 ]. Meta-analyses comparing AI vs. expert dermatologists Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses on AI diagnostic performance for melanoma classification in dermoscopic images have pooled area under the curve (AUC) results from different studies [ 110 ]. These meta-analyses indicated that the pooled AUC for AI algorithms was 0.92–0.98, with comparable performance to expert dermatologists [ 110 ]. Yang et al. similarly demonstrated that the pooled AUC for computer-aided diagnostic (CAD) systems and experience-to-expert-level dermatologists was approximately 0.9 [ 110 ]. Higher AI performance can be attributed to larger training datasets and the use of convolutional neural network (CNN) classifiers. In contrast, traditional machine learning models, such as artificial neural networks (ANNs) and support vector machines (SVMs), remain sensitive to feature selection and are inadequate for managing large, high-dimensional image data [ 86 ]. Meta-analyses tend to be restricted to closed-source studies that evaluate performance on a defined, limited number of public test sets, with relatively small pooled sample sizes [ 58 ]. For instance, both meta-analyses conducted by Lu et al. [ 53 ] included only six studies. In contrast, a meta-analysis of prospective clinical trials directly comparing AI versus dermatologist performance in imaging-based diagnosis found that AI performance is inferior to that of dermatologists [ 110 ]. Real-world applications Some published research has addressed practicalities, translation, and testing of artificial intelligence in real-world settings. The International Skin Imaging Collaboration (ISIC) archive and challenges stand as key contributors to these interests since their inception, thanks to ongoing updates and expansion [ 3 ]. In addition, clinical trials have been conducted over the years to investigate the feasibility and use of AI-powered diagnostics and triage decision-making [ 58 ]. In December 2021, the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved the first medical device that uses artificial intelligence to detect melanoma. The software can be used on dermatoscopic images of pigmented skin lesions and categorizes those images as “benign” or “suspicious for melanoma.” The FDA stated that the technology “could improve early detection of melanoma” when used alongside traditional methods of evaluation [ 100 ]. Barata and colleagues have published a systematic review and meta-analysis of studies comparing the diagnostic accuracy of deep learning algorithms against health-care professionals in dermatoscopic diagnosis of malignant melanoma. The review included 76 studies, comprising over 120,000 image samples (63,364 images from malignant melanomas), all published until 2023. Overall, the pooled diagnostic accuracy of deep learning exceeded that of healthcare professionals, evidencing that deep learning models can perform better at melanoma diagnosis [ 110 ]. The authors also indicate that, despite these promising results, further evaluation through well-designed clinical studies is needed to demonstrate that deep-learning algorithms can improve clinical outcomes and be safely incorporated into clinical practice [ 58 , 110 ]. Recent regulatory developments and newly authorized devices The regulatory landscape for AI in dermatology has undergone a significant transformation between 2024 and 2025, shifting from initial frameworks to more agile, lifecycle-based oversight. This period marked the authorization of high-profile diagnostic tools and the enforcement of comprehensive legal standards in both the United States and the European Union [ 100 ]. Newly authorized devices A pivotal development occurred in January 2024, when the U.S. FDA granted De Novo marketing authorization to DermaSensor, the first AI-enabled medical device for skin cancer detection intended for use by primary care physicians (U.S. Food and Drug Administration [FDA], [ 96 ]. Unlike previous image-based CAD tools restricted to specialist use, DermaSensor utilizes elastic scattering spectroscopy (ESS) combined with AI to evaluate lesions suggestive of melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, and squamous cell carcinoma [ 100 ]. In pivotal studies, the device demonstrated a 96% sensitivity for detecting malignancy, designed to assist non-specialists in referral decisions for patients over 40 [ 61 ]. This authorization represents a paradigm shift, moving AI diagnostic support from the dermatology clinic into the primary care setting to improve early detection rates [ 100 ]. Beyond individual devices, the volume of authorized AI products has surged. As of late 2024, the FDA’s list of authorized AI/ML-enabled medical devices exceeded 1,000 entries, with a notable acceleration in 510(k) clearances for radiological and dermatological applications [ 74 ]. Updated regulatory frameworks To address the rapid iteration inherent in AI algorithms, regulatory bodies have moved away from “locked” pre-market approvals toward dynamic lifecycle management. FDA Predetermined Change Control Plans (PCCP): In late 2024, the FDA finalized its guidance on PCCPs, allowing manufacturers to specify planned modifications to AI models (such as re-training on new data) during the initial marketing submission. This framework enables “continuous learning” systems to update without requiring a new 510(k) submission for every iteration, provided the changes fall within the pre-approved protocol [ 26 ]. EU AI Act: In the European Union, the AI Act formally came into force in August 2024, classifying most AI-enabled medical devices as “high-risk” systems [ 15 ]. This legislation mandates stricter compliance regarding data governance, transparency, and human oversight [ 9 ]. Crucially, new harmonization pathways introduced in 2025 now allow manufacturers to undergo a single conformity assessment that satisfies both the Medical Device Regulation (MDR) and the new AI Act requirements, reducing administrative duplication [ 12 ]. These updates collectively aim to resolve the “regulatory lag” that previously hindered the deployment of adaptive AI tools, balancing rigorous safety standards with the need for timely technological updates in clinical practice. Conclusion Artificial intelligence has emerged as a transformative force in the diagnosis and prognosis of skin cancer, offering unprecedented opportunities for precision medicine. By leveraging advanced computational models, particularly deep learning techniques such as convolutional neural networks and vision transformers, AI systems have achieved diagnostic performance comparable to or exceeding that of expert dermatologists. Furthermore, the integration of multimodal data—including dermoscopic images, histopathological slides, genomic profiles, and patient metadata—has enhanced prognostic modeling, enabling more accurate risk stratification and individualized management strategies. Such innovations hold particular promise for reducing mortality in aggressive cancers like melanoma and improving early detection in resource-limited settings through mobile health applications. Nevertheless, the path toward clinical adoption is complex. Current limitations include data quality issues, biases related to ethnicity and skin tone, limited interpretability of black-box models, and regulatory uncertainties. Ethical concerns, particularly regarding patient privacy and healthcare disparities, further underscore the need for responsible innovation. Strategies such as explainable AI, federated learning, standardized evaluation protocols, and multicenter clinical validation are vital for addressing these barriers. Importantly, successful translation of AI into practice will require close collaboration among clinicians, computer scientists, regulatory authorities, and policymakers. Looking forward, AI-driven tools must be designed not only for accuracy but also for transparency, accessibility, and equity. Equitable deployment in both high- and low-resource settings, supported by teledermatology and smartphone-based platforms, has the potential to expand access to timely cancer screening significantly. Additionally, fostering patient trust through explainability and clinician oversight will be central to widespread acceptance. Ultimately, while challenges remain, the integration of AI into dermatological practice represents a paradigm shift that could redefine the future of skin cancer care, enabling earlier detection, more accurate prognosis, and improved survival outcomes across diverse populations.
