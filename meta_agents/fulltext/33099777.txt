Introduction Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer among Asian American women, yet this population remains significantly underserved in prevention and early detection efforts [ 1 ]. Alarmingly, breast cancer incidence among Asian American women under age 50 has increased by approximately 50% over the past two decades, representing the highest rise among all racial and ethnic groups and now approaching rates observed in White women [ 2 ]. Across age groups, breast cancer incidence among Asian American women has increased by 2.6% annually, the fastest-growing rate of any racial group in the United States, a trend documented consistently across multiple epidemiological studies [ 2 – 6 ]. Despite these rapid increases in prevalence, Asian American women have the second lowest breast cancer screening rates (62%) compared to White (68%), Black (73%), and Hispanic (65%) women [ 2 ]. In addition, breast cancer among Asian American women tends to present at younger ages, often one to two decades earlier than among non-Hispanic White women, with peak incidence occurring in the 60s rather than between ages 75–79 [ 4 , 7 ]. These disparities also extend to breast cancer outcomes. Certain Asian American subgroups experience higher rates of late-stage cancer diagnosis [ 8 – 14 ]. Over the past thirty years, Asian American women have experienced only a modest 13% reduction in breast cancer mortality, compared to 43% among White women and 31–32% among Black and Hispanic women [ 2 ]. Notably, Asian American are the only racial group in the United States for whom cancer remains the leading cause of death [ 15 ]. Together, these trends underscore a critical and persistent gap in breast cancer prevention and control for Asian American women. Existing literature consistently demonstrates the critical role of patient education in promoting breast cancer screening among Asian American women. Primary care plays a central role in breast cancer education, prevention, and screening. However, multiple structural and clinical constraints limit the extent to which comprehensive education can be delivered in these settings, especially for Asian American populations which requires tailored educational information [ 16 – 18 ]. For example, time-limited visits, competing clinical priorities, and variability in culturally appropriate resources often result in brief, guideline-focused discussion rather than in-depth education to address patients’ knowledge gaps, beliefs, and emotional concerns [ 19 , 20 ]. Prior studies indicate that limited knowledge of screening guidelines, culturally influenced beliefs (e.g., low perceived susceptibility, fatalism, and fear of diagnosis), language barriers, and low health literacy are major contributors to underutilization of screening services [ 9 , 10 , 21 ]. Community-based patient education interventions have been shown to improve breast cancer knowledge, correct misconceptions, increase screening intentions, and prompt mammography scheduling [ 21 ]. Effective education strategies identified in this literature extend beyond information dissemination to include interactive, face-to-face learning, narrative approaches, skill-building activities (e.g., breast self-examination practice), and repeated reinforcement delivered in trusted community settings [ 22 , 23 ]. Importantly, these studies highlight that patient education is most impactful when it addresses both cognitive (knowledge, perceived risk) and affective (fear, stigma, confidence) dimensions of screening behavior. However, the existing literature remains sparse and much of the evidence is from more than a decade ago, with the predominant focus on screening promotion or reminder-based interventions, with limited integration of foundational cancer education or primary prevention strategies within a unified educational framework. While screening-focused patient education remains an essential of breast cancer prevention, the emphasis on early detection alone does not address the substantial role of modifiable risk factors in disease development. Approximately 90% of breast cancer cases are attributable not to genetic predisposition or family history but to modifiable environmental, behavioral, lifestyle, and unknown factors [ 24 ]. These data underscore the need for patient education curricula to extend beyond secondary prevention and incorporate primary prevention strategies aimed at reducing breast cancer risk before disease onset. Among modifiable behaviors, physical activity (PA) represents one of the most evidence-based and actionable strategies for primary prevention of breast cancer. Regular PA reduces breast cancer risk through multiple biological pathways, including prevention of overweight and obesity, decreased chronic inflammation, improved immune function, and regulation of sex hormone levels [ 25 , 26 ]. Excess body weight is a well-established risk factor for breast cancer, particularly among postmenopausal women [ 27 , 28 ], and is of growing concern given rising rates of overweight and obesity among Asian American women [ 29 ]. In addition, PA has been shown to lower basal levels of inflammatory markers such as interleukin-6 [ 30 ], and to favorably regulate estrogen and insulin pathways linked to carcinogenesis [ 31 ]. Despite these well-established benefits, current patient education efforts have rarely prioritized PA as a core component of breast cancer prevention for Asian American women. National PA guidelines recommend at least 150 min of moderate-to-vigorous activity per week and muscle strengthening activities twice weekly [ 32 ]. However, only 34% of Asian American women meet national aerobic PA guidelines, substantially lower than the national average of 51% [ 33 ]. This pattern of low PA participation among Asian American women has been consistently documented across multiple studies [ 34 – 38 ]. These findings highlight PA as both a critical gap and a key opportunity for patient education-based breast cancer prevention for Asian American. In response to persistent breast cancer disparities among Asian American women and the underutilization of PA within breast cancer prevention education, we developed the Breast C ancer A wareness and R isk E ducation for Asian Americans (Breast CARE) patient education program. We recognize that Asian Americans represent a highly heterogeneous population encompassing diverse ethnicities, languages, migration histories, and sociocultural contexts. Breast cancer risk, screening behaviors, and health outcomes may vary across subgroups. However, population-level epidemiologic trends demonstrate consistently rising incidence rates and persistent screening and outcome disparities when Asian Americans are examined aggregately. Given shared structural barriers, common cultural influences on health beliefs, and the limited availability of subgroup-specific data and interventions, examining Asian American women as an aggregate population provides an important starting point for prevention-focused education. This approach enables development of broadly accessible curricula while allowing for future refinement tailored to specific subgroups. The purpose of this paper was to describe the development of the Breast CARE, a culturally tailored, community-based breast cancer prevention curriculum for Asian American women, developed through an iterative, Community Advisory Board (CAB)–informed process that integrates empirical evidence with community priorities. Methods This study was approved by the Intuitional Review Board of University of Wisconsin-Madison. Framework to inform teaching strategies The curriculum design was informed by multimodal learning principles (i.e., visual, auditory, read/write, and kinesthetic [VARK] model). Multimodal learning emphasizes engaging learners through multiple, complementary instructional modes to support comprehension, retention, and accessibility across diverse learning styles [ 39 ]. Breast CARE patient education program: curriculum development process The CARE curriculum was developed through iterative, CAB-informed co-development, in which an evidence-based draft curriculum was refined through systematic incorporation of CAB feedback on content, framing, sequencing, and instructional strategies. Guided by community-based participatory research principles [ 40 , 41 ], the process emphasized cultural relevance, feasibility, and alignment with breast cancer prevention priorities among Asian American women. The process consisted of five core steps (Fig. 1 ): (1) Gaps identification and evidence review, (2) building on an existing program, (3) research team deliberation and content development, (4) CAB co-development, and (5) integration of CAB contributions with evidence-based decision-making. Although presented sequentially, these steps were non-linear and highly iterative. Findings from one step frequently prompted revisiting earlier phases. For example, issues raised during CAB discussions often led to additional literature review, research team deliberation, and refinement of curriculum content to ensure scientific accuracy and cultural appropriateness. Fig. 1 Iterative Program Development Process Step 1. gaps identification and evidence review The first step of curriculum development focused on identifying gaps in existing patient education related to breast cancer prevention efforts for Asian American, synthesizing empirical evidence and prior community-engaged research to inform the structure and content of a breast cancer prevention curriculum for Asian American women. Building on gaps identified in the literature and the research team’s prior work with this population, this step aimed to determine how educational content could best support understanding, engagement, and informed decision-making [ 9 , 10 , 21 – 23 , 42 – 45 ]. Barriers to screening Evidence from prior studies identified several barriers to breast cancer screening among Asian American, including low perceived susceptibility, fatalistic beliefs, and fear of diagnosis and malformation [ 9 , 13 , 21 ]. These barriers informed both framing and content of our curriculum. The curriculum intentionally incorporated the breast cancer epidemiologic data specific to Asian American populations to contextualize risk and support more accurate risk perception [ 2 – 5 , 46 ]. To address misinformation, the research team reviewed myths and misconceptions documented in literature and commonly described in online sources (e.g., health-related blogs, community organization websites). A targeted list of prevalent breast cancer–related myths was compiled and embedded within the curriculum. Review of screening guidelines To ensure accuracy and consistency of educational content, breast cancer screening and prevention guidelines from the American Cancer Society, U.S. Preventive Services Task Force, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) were reviewed [ 47 – 49 ]. The curriculum ultimately adopted NCCN guidelines, given their widespread use and status as the gold standard for clinical practice in the U.S. This review informed inclusion of explanation of screening modalities and recommendation difference between average risk and high risk. Integrating guideline-concordant information was intended to support informed decision-making. Integration of evidence-based risk reduction strategies Primary prevention strategies are often underemphasized in breast cancer prevention educational programs, despite representing the first line of disease prevention. Given the low prevalence of PA among Asian American women and resource constraints that limit access to structured prevention programs, the curriculum intentionally incorporated an overview of modifiable breast cancer risk factors, with particular emphasis on PA as a feasible and evidence-based primary prevention strategy [ 25 – 27 , 29 – 31 ]. To inform the integration of PA into the curriculum, we conducted two complementary reviews focused on PA in Asian American populations. First, a scoping review of PA components in cancer prevention and control programs among Asian American was undertaken to examine existing intervention approaches and educational components [ 50 ]. The review indicated that while PA recommendations were often included, few programs explicitly described the role of PA in breast cancer prevention or explained the biological mechanisms linking PA to risk reduction. Additionally, recommendations regarding body mass index frequently relied on general population criteria without addressing Asian-specific cutoffs. These findings informed two key curriculum decisions. First, Breast CARE explicitly incorporated evidence describing the benefits of PA in breast cancer prevention, including mechanisms related to weight regulation, hormonal balance, and inflammation. Second, the curriculum included instruction on interpreting BMI using Asian-specific criteria to provide culturally and clinically appropriate guidance. Second, a meta-synthesis of qualitative studies examining Asian American women’s experiences and perceptions of PA was conducted [ 51 ]. Findings from the meta-synthesis indicated that PA among Asian American women is often embedded in daily life rather than conceptualized as structured exercise and is strongly shaped by cultural norms, gender roles, family responsibilities, and social support. These insights informed the curriculum’s emphasis on reframing PA as flexible, achievable movement integrated into everyday routines, rather than as formal exercise alone. In addition, recognition of acculturation-related environmental and psychological barriers guided the inclusion of culturally relevant examples, social support strategies, and adaptable activity options to enhance feasibility and engagement. Step 2. Building upon a culturally validated existing prevention program The Breast CARE was developed by building upon an existing breast cancer prevention program designed for Hmong Asian women [ 52 , 53 ]. The original program, developed by ML, focused on culturally tailored cancer education, screening awareness, and community-based delivery. Drawing on the strengths of this foundational program, Breast CARE retained core elements that demonstrated cultural relevance and feasibility, including community-centered education, use of culturally appropriate examples, and an emphasis on reducing informational barriers to screening. Key educational components from the original program were reviewed and served as initial structure of the adapted curriculum. These included: 1) foundational cancer education to support understanding of cancer as a biological process, using plain-language explanations of abnormal cell growth and an interactive string-based activity to illustrate cell division and mutation; (2) breast cancer screening education covering mammography, clinical breast examination, and breast self-examination, including hands-on practice of self-examination techniques using breast models; (3) education on breast cancer risk factors; (4) common Breast cancer–related myths; and (5) overview of prevention strategies. Building upon this foundation and informed by findings from Step 1, the Breast CARE expanded the scope of breast cancer prevention to include multiple Asian American subgroups, incorporated updated epidemiological data and evidence-based prevention guidelines, and integrated PA as a primary risk reduction strategy. This approach allowed Breast CARE to leverage established, community-trusted intervention components while extending content and applicability to address emerging prevention needs across diverse Asian American communities. Step 3. Research Team Deliberation and Content Development Following the review of existing literature and adaptation of the Hmong breast cancer screening educational program, the research team engaged in a series of discussions focusing on identifying additional patient education content and areas requiring revision. The research team also discussed on balancing scientific accuracy with accessibility, determining appropriate literacy levels, and ensuring coherence across curriculum domains. As part of this process, the team made a deliberate decision to organize the curriculum around three core patient education domains: (1) Breast Cancer 101, (2) Breast Cancer Screening, and (3) Risk Reduction Strategies. The team collaboratively reviewed and revised session outlines, learning strategies, and educational materials. Attention was given to incorporating interactive components designed to facilitate the translation of content knowledge into actionable behavior. For example, in PA session, we included demonstrations, guided goal-setting activities, and practical strategy development to support sustained engagement in PA. Activities and strategies were tailored to participants’ individual baseline levels of PA, allowing for personalized goal formulation and adaptation of movement strategies that were feasible and culturally meaningful within participants’ daily lives. Step 4. CAB Co-development Consistent with CBPR principles, a defining feature of Breast CARE’s curriculum development was the active involvement of a CAB composed of Asian American women representing eight Asian American subgroups (i.e., Hmong, Chinese, Indonesian, Nepali, Korean, Japanese, Asian Indian, and Pakistan). CAB members served as co-developers throughout the process. While the research team and existing literature informed the initial evidence base and draft content, CAB engagement was essential for ensuring cultural relevance, acceptability, and practical feasibility of the curriculum, which are core elements of effective patient education interventions in underserved populations. A total of six CAB meetings were held, each lasting about 90 min throughout the curriculum development process. The final meeting extended to four hours to allow for a comprehensive review of the full Breast CARE. Across all meetings, average attendance was 10 of 14, indicating sustained engagement. The CAB provided critical input on: (1) additional educational content, (2) cultural relevance and appropriateness of content; (3) framing of breast cancer information and screening messages; (4) acceptability of PA recommendations and messaging within a breast cancer prevention context; (5) preferred learning formats (e.g., lectures, visuals, videos). Example questions are provided in Table 1 . After integrating CAB feedback, CAB members participated in a mock delivery of the near-final curriculum prior to pilot testing. During this session, CAB members engaged with the curriculum as participants and provided targeted feedback on content clarity, flow, emotional tone, perceived relevance, and overall delivery. Recommendations from this process informed final revisions before pilot implementation. Data analysis of CAB Feedback CAB meetings were audio-recorded and detailed field notes were taken by facilitators and research staff. Feedback was analyzed using Rapid Content Analysis, a structured qualitative analytic approach suited to implementation-focused research where timely, actionable insights are needed [ 54 – 56 ]. Audio recordings were reviewed alongside with field notes to confirm content, identify key themes, and extract illustrative examples relevant to curriculum development decisions. Codes were applied to segments of data representing distinct ideas or suggestions, which were then grouped into thematic categories aligned with the study’s domains (e.g., conceptual challenges, informational needs, instructional strategies). Step 5. Integration of CAB contributions with evidence-based decision-making Table 2 presents domain-specific content revisions, corresponding learning strategies (i.e., VARK model), and CAB’s quotes. CAB feedback guided substantive revisions to curriculum content and instructional strategies across all domains. The research team addressed these recommendations through evidence-based decisions and applied the VARK model as an instructional design guide to enhance clarity, engagement, and accessibility. CAB feedback also led to the development of a terminology glossary and a curated resources list focus on breast cancer screening and PA. The list include links to reputable webpages, information on accessing local mammography screening and patient navigation services, and physical activity channels identified by CAB members. To support diverse literacy levels across Asian American populations, content was delivered using plain language, visual aids, and multimodal strategies (e.g., demonstrations, discussion, and experiential activities). Learning was further reinforced through structured supports, such as providing answer keys with definitions for interactive activities (e.g., breast anatomy matching). CAB members emphasized the need for clearer sequencing of biological concepts and greater support for learners with limited prior exposure to cancer biology. The research team responded by applying scaffolding principles, restructuring content to build from foundational concepts to more abstract explanations of cancer development and genetics. Instructional revisions also reflected CAB priorities for interactive and multimodal engagement, while maintaining accuracy in the presentation of biological mechanisms. For example, pedagogical activities were revised, including modification of a role-play activity to better support understanding of cancer development, addition of virtual cues and embedded figures, and inclusion of a summary video. Domain 2 feedback: breast cancer screening CAB feedback underscored the importance of explicitly explaining the rationale for screening, supported by current data and real-world context. CAB members expressed that participants often seek not only information on screening recommendations, but also explanations of consequences following abnormal findings, including diagnosis and treatment. The research team integrated this feedback by expanding contextual content, incorporating brief introductions to diagnostic and treatment pathways, and strengthening narrative elements that connect screening to lived experience. Domain 3 feedback: risk reduction strategies CAB feedback in the risk reduction domain emphasized the importance of prevention strategies that are grounded in participants’ lifestyle and social contexts. In addition to calling for clearer explanations of why specific behaviors matter and the inclusion of population-specific data, CAB members identified social connection and mental health as integral components of breast cancer risk reduction. Although these topics were not emphasized in the initial curriculum design, the research team incorporated them in response to community priorities and aligned their inclusion with evidence linking psychosocial well-being to health behaviors and cancer prevention. CAB members also advised that discussions of healthy weight be approached with sensitivity, recognizing the potential for stigma. In response, the research team adopted a nonjudgmental, supportive framing that emphasized self-care and clarified that weight-related information was intended to support understanding of health indicators rather than to convey judgment. Content was reorganized to emphasize practical and actionable strategies. of health Visual: SlidesAuditory: Lecture, questioningReading/writing: Program package, flyer, notebook Domain 1: breast cancer 101 The Breast Cancer 101 domain established a foundational framework to support participants’ understanding of breast cancer and to prepare them for subsequent screening and prevention discussions. This domain integrated epidemiologic context, basic cancer biology, and breast anatomy to build shared conceptual understanding using culturally appropriate and accessible language. Key components included an overview of breast cancer patterns among Asian American women, simplified explanations of cellular processes and cancer development, and introduction of core anatomical concepts relevant to breast health. The domain also addressed common breast cancer myths and misconceptions and distinguished between modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors to emphasize participant empowerment. Content was framed within a prevention continuum by introducing primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention concepts. Domain 2: breast cancer screening The Breast Cancer Screening domain focused on early detection as a key form of secondary prevention and emphasized screening as an empowering, proactive health behavior. This domain integrated lived experiences, screening education, and skill-building activities to normalize screening participation and reduce fear and stigma. Core components included an overview of common screening modalities and risk-based screening recommendations, experiential activities to build confidence and body awareness, and tools to support individualized risk understanding. A brief contextual overview of breast cancer treatment was included to situate screening within the broader continuum of care. Domain 3: risk reduction strategies The Risk Reduction Strategies domain focused on primary prevention approaches to reduce breast cancer risk, with physical activity positioned as a central and modifiable behavior. This domain integrated evidence-based information on lifestyle risk factors, culturally relevant data, and skill-building activities to support practical application. Core components included education on the role of physical activity in breast cancer prevention, interpretation of physical activity guidelines and healthy weight indicators using Asian-specific criteria, and experiential learning to build confidence across varying activity levels. The domain emphasized adaptability, motivation, and sustainability by addressing social connection, mental health, and integration of movement into daily life. Discussion and lessons learned This study describes the development of Breast CARE, a culturally tailored, community-based breast cancer prevention curriculum for Asian Americans and offers insights into how community input and evidence-based pedagogy can be integrated to address persistent gaps in breast cancer prevention education. Our Breast CARE addresses the challenge of limited visit time to provide in-depth and culturally tailored education for Asian Americans in primary care settings. Consequently, Asian American women persistently have low breast cancer screening rates, despite clinical recommendations, underscoring the limitation of clinic-based approaches alone [ 8 , 9 , 13 ]. Specifically, our community-based program, Breast CARE can complement primary care by reinforcing guideline-concordant information while addressing sociocultural and informational barriers to health behaviors [ 21 – 23 ]. A central lesson from the development was that community-engaged cancer education must address informational and interpretive needs. Across domains, CAB members repeatedly requested more explanatory depth (the “why”), including epidemiologic data and basic information of diagnosis and treatment. These results align with prior research demonstrating that effective cancer prevention for this population require addressing both knowledge gaps and cultural beliefs [ 21 ]. In addition, consistent with prior research [ 40 , 41 ], the findings suggest that providing context depth can enhance perceived relevance and build trust, particularly when delivering information that challenges existing beliefs or requires integration of new knowledge. A second lesson was the importance of providing foundational cancer education before introducing screening or prevention recommendations. CAB members emphasized that understanding what cancer is and how it develops helped reduce fear, fatalistic beliefs, and confusion surrounding breast cancer screening and diagnosis. This supports existing evidence identifying misconceptions and low health literacy as barriers to screening among Asian Americans, and extends prior interventions that have incorporated foundational education as a strategy to improve screening behaviors [ 11 , 13 ]. For example, the Breast CARE intentionally framed cancer as a biological process involving abnormal cell growth rather than as an inevitable or mysterious condition. The use of scaffolded, plan-language instruction in the program reflects evidence-based educational strategies that enhance comprehension across diverse learner needs [ 57 ]. By strengthening foundational understanding outside of clinical settings, such programs may enhance the effectiveness of subsequent provider–patient interactions in primary care. A third lesson was that the key design problem was not whether to provide “more information,” but how to deliver more information without increasing cognitive burden. CAB feedback favored integrating multimodal and interactive approaches, including visual aids, role-paly, narrative elements, and facilitated discussion. These findings are consistent with prior intervention studies among Asian American populations demonstrating that interactive, community-based education improves engagement and screening uptake compared to didactic approaches alone [ 21 – 23 ]. The application of multimodal learning principles (VARK) further supports learning across diverse population by reinforcing key concepts while accommodating varied learning preferences [ 39 ]. Fourthly, our findings also highlight how using community engagement approach such as a CAB can build not only the cultural relevance but also the structural and pedagogical design of an intervention. Consistent with CBPR literature, CAB involvement contributed to refining content, improving acceptability, and ensuring alignment with community priorities [ 40 , 41 ], and has been shown to enhance feasibility and implementation success in community-based programs [ 40 ]. Beyond surface-level adaptations, CAB input informed content sequencing, depth of explanation, framing of sensitive topics, and instructional strategies. These findings illustrate that community engagement can influence core educational design decision, not only cultural tailoring. CAB guidance also informed how the curriculum addressed both cognitive and affective barriers. Breast CARE embedded structured myth debunking, narrative elements (e.g., testimonials), and facilitated dialogue to normalize discussion of breast health. Sensitive topics, such as weight and mental health, were framed using a supportive, nonjudgmental approach emphasizing self-care rather than evaluation. Similarly, an important consideration in curriculum design was the inclusion of breast self-examination, which remains a topic of ongoing debate given evidence suggesting limited effectiveness for early detection and potential to increase anxiety. This issue was extensively discussed within the research team and CAB. Rather than excluding self-examination based solely on current guidelines, CAB members emphasized its cultural relevance and potential role in reducing stigma around breast health. In response, self-examination was reframed within the curriculum as a self-care practice focused on body awareness and comfort with discussing breast health, rather than as a screening tool. The program explicitly highlights that BSE is not a substitute for mammography and reinforces guideline-concordant screening recommendations. These approaches are consistent with qualitative research demonstrating the importance of addressing emotional and cultural factors that affects health behaviors among Asian American women [ 44 , 51 , 58 ]. Compared to the original Hmong-focused breast cancer education program on which Breast CARE was partially based [ 52 , 53 ], several important distinctions emerged. While the original program emphasized culturally tailored screening education within a single subgroup and focused primarily on awareness and screening behaviors [ 52 , 53 ], Breast CARE expanded the scope to include multiple Asian American subgroups, incorporated updated epidemiological data [ 2 – 5 , 7 ], and integrated guideline-concordant recommendations [ 47 – 49 ]. In addition, Breast CARE explicitly incorporated primary prevention strategies (e.g., PA) as a central component of breast cancer risk reduction, addressing a gap identified in prior literature where prevention programs have largely focused on screening rather than modifiable risk behaviors [ 50 ]. This broader scope reflects a shift toward more comprehensive prevention frameworks that integrate both primary and secondary prevention within a single intervention. Contributions and implications for future research Collectively, these lessons advance the science of community-engaged cancer prevention education by demonstrating how CAB input can be systematically integrated with evidence-based decision-making to balance informational depth, accessibility, and emotional acceptability. Curriculum content and framing were grounded in community-identified beliefs, concerns, and misconceptions, enhancing relevance and acceptability. Prioritizing foundational knowledge supported health literacy and enabled participants to engage more meaningfully with screening and prevention information. Instructional design emphasized interactive, experiential, and discussion-based strategies to address diverse learning needs and the emotional dimensions of breast cancer education. Emphasizing PA as a primary prevention strategy within the breast cancer prevention curriculum addressed an important gap and aligned with community-identified needs among Asian American women. Breast CARE contributes a practical model for delivering comprehensive prevention content through multimodal, scaffolded instruction while remaining culturally responsive and prevention focused. Future research should examine how this approach influences knowledge retention, self-efficacy, and preventive behaviors, and assess its adaptability across populations and settings. Limitations Despite the strength of this work, several limitations should be considered. First, this study focused on curriculum development rather than evaluation of effectiveness; therefore, conclusions regarding impact on knowledge, behavior, or screening uptake cannot yet be drawn. Future studies could assess the Breast CARE’s effectiveness. Second, although the CAB included representation of multiple Asian American subgroups, finding may not fully capture the heterogeneity across all subgroups. Third, CAB members may represent individuals who are more engaged or health-conscious than the broader community, potentially influencing the type and depth of feedback received. Conclusion This manuscript describes the systematic development of Breast CARE, a culturally tailored, community-based breast cancer prevention curriculum for Asian American women. Breast CARE curriculum development process offers a practical model for combining community-engaged approaches with evidence-based pedagogy in cancer prevention education. By centering community voice while upholding scientific rigor, this approach underscores the potential of participatory, evidence-informed curriculum design to advance equitable and effective cancer prevention efforts.
