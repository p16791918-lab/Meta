Introduction Skin cancer is the most common malignancy worldwide and consists of three main types: melanoma, basal cell carcinoma (BCC) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) [ 1 ]. The latter two types are collectively known as non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC). Over the past few decades, skin cancer has affected the health of millions of people worldwide and has placed a huge economic burden on healthcare systems. Kornek, [ 2 ] Among them, NMSC plays a major role in causing extremely significant health and economic burden due to its increasing incidence and prevalence [ 3 ]. Studies by the World Cancer Research Fund indicate that in 2020, nearly 1.2 million cases of non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC) were reported globally, accounting for 78% of all skin cancer cases. 20% of Americans will develop NMSC in their lifetime, and the average total annual treatment cost for NMSC in the U.S. is estimated to be $4.8 billion [ 4 ]. In addition, the burden of NMSC in terms of mortality is often underestimated and has not been adequately documented [ 5 ]. Recent studies have shown that NMSC is the type of skin cancer that causes the most deaths, with NMSC causing more than 63,700 deaths in 2020 [ 6 ]. NMSC is now a major public health problem, and up-to-date and comprehensive studies are needed to understand the epidemiology and burden of NMSC globally. We used the latest 2021 data to characterize the burden of disease and temporal trends of NMSC at global, regional, national, gender and age levels. The aim is to provide better strategies in the prevention and treatment of NMSC and to contribute to the development of better public health policies with a view to reducing the impact of NMSC on human health and the economy. Methods Overview and data sources We assessed the world epidemiologic burden of non-melanoma skin cancer using the most recent data available from GBD2021, and all data from this study are available on the GBD website at https://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool . GBD2021 provides data for 204 countries and territories, which are further categorized into 5 regions based on Socio-Demographic Index (SDI), in addition to 54 GBD regions. In this study, we obtained and analyzed data on NMSC incidence, prevalence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) at the global, regional, and national levels. Each of these estimates was expressed in terms of counts and age-standardized prevalence rates (ASPRs) per 100,000 individuals, accompanied by a 95% uncertainty interval (95% UI) for clarity [ 7 ]. Human development index The Human Development Index (HDI) is a composite measure proposed by the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) to assess the social and economic development levels of countries [ 8 ]. Unlike the traditional Gross Domestic Product (GDP), the HDI is based on three main dimensions: life expectancy, education, and living standards. These indicators are aggregated and standardized to produce a value between 0 and 1. The closer the HDI value is to 1, the higher the level of human development in that country. Statistical analysis We used incidence, prevalence, mortality, DALYs, and their corresponding rates to characterize the burden of NMSC across different regions and dimensions (including age, sex, SDI, GBD region, and country). In order to balance the consistency between the estimates of certain regional groups, in this study we resorted to the DisMod-MR tool [ 9 ]. We fit the time series data based on a linear regression model and use EAPC (Estimated Annual Percentage Change) to quantify the annual percentage change of health indicators (such as incidence, prevalence, mortality, or DALYs) over a specific period. A positive EAPC value indicates that the health indicator increases annually during the studied period, while a negative value indicates a decrease. Hierarchical cluster analysis based on EAPC values to assess patterns of change in the associated burden of disease in each GBD area. Frontier analysis We used frontier analysis to identify the lowest achievable age-standardized prevalence, incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) rates based on the Socio-demographic Index (SDI) level. Using data from 2021, we calculated the effective difference (EF value) for each country and region, which represents the difference between their age-standardized prevalence, incidence, mortality, and DALYs rates and the lowest achievable age-standardized rates prevalence, incidence, mortality, and DALYs at their respective SDI levels. Ethical approval This study is a secondary analysis of the 2021 GBD data. Since these data are publicly available or have been anonymized, no ethical approval or consent is required. We assessed the burden by age, sex, SDI, and region, and projected future trends. Our findings show a global increase in NMSC burden, with significant rises in age-standardized incidence, prevalence, and mortality rates from 1990 to 2021. The same trend is observed in both the BCC and SCC subtypes. In 2021, an estimated 2.76 million people worldwide had NMSC. Given the aging population and rising incidence, as well as the increasing economic burden, it is crucial to understand temporal trends and regional variations. This can guide resource allocation and effective public health strategies to reduce NMSC-related deaths. Our study found that the global age-standardised prevalence of NMSC increased from 18.42 (95% UI, 15.26–22.12/100,000) in 1990 to 32.51 (95% UI, 28.22–37.54/100,000) in 2021. In addition, the number of deaths increased substantially by about 151.08% from 1990 to 2021. Factors contributing to this phenomenon include population ageing, [ 10 ] genetic factors, [ 11 ] and environmental factors such as ultraviolet light [ 12 ]. For instance, male populations, particularly those engaged in outdoor occupations (such as military personnel and construction workers), face significantly heightened occupational risks of ultraviolet exposure [ 13 ]. For the older population, interventions must account for their complex clinical conditions. Elderly patients frequently present with multiple chronic conditions, necessitating more frequent skin monitoring. Also, lifestyle changes, occupational exposures, [ 14 , 15 ] geographic location, and improved diagnostic techniques have contributed to the increase in cases of NMSC [ 16 ]. In addition, immunosuppressive drugs, complications and medical delays contribute to the elevated risk of death in elderly NMSC patients [ 17 , 18 ]. In 2021, age-standardised incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs of NMSC increased with age. This is consistent with previous research findings. 19 Additionally, we further discovered deaths globally were concentrated in the 70–89 years age group. This may be due to the presence of other fatal complications in patients over 90 years of age. In terms of gender, the global disease burden of NMSC is higher than that of females in all the years 1990–2021, which is in line with the findings of previous studies [ 20 – 22 ]. This may be related to hormones, longer outdoor exposure and less use of sun protection in men. 23 . We found significant differences in the burden of disease for NMSC between countries and regions. High SDI regions had the highest incidence and prevalence, and medium SDI regions had the highest mortality and death cases. This may be attributed to socioeconomic development, advances in medical diagnostic technology, and increased awareness of NMSC [ 24 – 26 ]. In recent years, the continuous development of diagnostic techniques has greatly increased the rate of early diagnosis of NMSC. Northern africa is the only region among all 54 GBD regions with a significant increase in disease burden. There are significant geographical variations in incidence and mortality rates across different countries and regions of the world, with the United States, New Zealand, and Greenland having the highest incidence rates globally. China, the United States, and India rank as the top three countries in terms of mortality cases. However, the observed correlations between SDI/HDI and NMSC burden in our study should be interpreted with caution, as they are ecological associations that are not adjusted for key potential confounding factors. Firstly, UV radiation exposure is the primary environmental risk factor for NMSC. The geographical distribution of UV intensity, occupational and recreational sun exposure patterns, and photoprotection behaviors vary widely and are not fully captured by SDI [ 27 ]. Secondly, differential access to and quality of healthcare significantly impacts NMSC detection and outcomes. Conversely, lower-resource settings may face delays in diagnosis and limited access to optimal treatment, contributing to higher case-fatality rates, even with lower reported incidence. Finally, individual-level factors such as skin phototype, genetic predisposition, and immunosuppressive status (e.g., in transplant recipients) are critical determinants of risk but are not accounted for in the SDI metric [ 28 , 29 ]. Therefore, while our analysis highlights important macro-level patterns, the unadjusted associations between development indices and NMSC burden may reflect a complex interplay of environmental exposure, healthcare infrastructure, and population susceptibility, rather than a direct causal effect of socioeconomic development alone. Previous studies have shown that the incidence of skin cancer is lower among people of color compared to white populations, and our findings are consistent with this [ 30 , 31 ]. However, we found that some regions with a high proportion of people with darker skin, such as Northern Africa, have the highest age-standardized death rate EAPC value of 7.94 (95% CI 6.83–9.06) and age-standardized DALYs EAPC value of 7.67 (95% CI 6.65–8.7) among all GBD regions. Additionally, regions like Africa and Southern Africa also show significant increasing trends in age-standardized death rate EAPC. This suggests that even countries predominantly inhabited by people with darker skin are facing an increasingly severe disease burden, indicating a need for effective intervention measures. Countries and regions with significant increase in deaths during 1990–2021 include Turkmenistan, Georgia, Egypt, Algeria, etc. The results suggest that countries with higher levels of human development are more likely to bear a lower burden of NMSC disease. Developed countries have better healthcare systems, stronger public awareness education, wider health insurance coverage, and more dermatologists [ 32 ]. In contrast, some poorer countries have problems with environmental pollution, occupational exposures, and inadequate healthcare services, resulting in failure to detect NMSC early and delays in treatment. Furthermore, it is crucial to recognize that national-level data can mask significant subnational disparities within large and geographically diverse countries. For instance, in Australia, which has one of the highest reported incidences of NMSC globally, mortality trends and risk factors may vary considerably between regions and demographic subgroups [ 33 ]. Similarly, studies from specific regions like Wales in the UK have demonstrated incidence rates of BCC that are among the highest in Europe, highlighting intra-national heterogeneity [ 34 ]. These subnational variations can be driven by differences in UV radiation exposure patterns, occupational risks (e.g.,military outdoor workers), healthcare access, and population demographics (e.g., skin phototype distribution) [ 13 , 35 ]. In addition, the significant increase in inequalities in the overall burden of NMSC disease across countries over time is worrisome, as it suggests uneven control of risk factors and a more acute public health problem. Our Fronitier analysis reveals significant variations in the disease burden of NSMC across countries and regions with differing SDI, with this disparity typically increasing alongside higher SDI levels. This finding underscores that socio-economic development alone does not automatically translate into superior disease control; effective public health strategies and the allocation of healthcare resources remain paramount [ 36 ]. For instance, certain countries or regions with high SDI – such as Australia and the United States – maintain relatively low mortality rates despite high incidence rates. This is attributable to robust healthcare systems, extensive public health education, advanced early diagnostic technologies (including dermoscopy and AI-assisted diagnosis), and effective treatment modalities (such as Mohs micrographic surgery). Consequently, their mortality figures align more closely with the ‘leading-edge’ performance expected for their respective SDI levels [ 37 ]. It is notable that even in regions predominantly inhabited by darker-skinned populations, such as North Africa, there has been a marked increase in ASDR. This indicates that when formulating interventions, reliance on traditional risk assessment models based solely on skin tone is insufficient. Instead, comprehensive factors must be considered, including local patterns of ultraviolet exposure, occupational risks, healthcare system capacity, and public awareness of protective measures [ 28 ]. Therefore, for nations aspiring to align with the fronitier curve, the focus must lie in implementing targeted, multi-tiered strategies: including strengthening primary healthcare and training dermatology specialists to enhance early diagnosis rates. Studies indicate that current treatments for NMSC include surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and photodynamic therapy (PDT), etc [ 38 , 39 ]. However, these methods have certain issues. Surgical treatment can significantly impact appearance and function, especially when the cancer is located on the face or other visible areas [ 40 ]. While NMSC has a high cure rate if detected early and treated well, its high incidence imposes a heavy burden on national health systems. Research shows that the annual cost of treatment in Australia, including hospitalization, specialist consultations, and procedural treatments, totals approximately AUD 703 million [ 41 ]. Therefore, the early prevention of NMSC is of great significance, specifically including sun protection, regular skin examinations, maintaining a healthy diet rich in antioxidants, and public education. This study relies on the GBD database, which has several limitations. First, many developing countries, especially low- and middle-income ones, lack robust health data collection systems and uniform methods, resulting in incomplete and unreliable data. Second, GBD uses complex statistical models to estimate missing data and adjust for bias, which rely on assumptions that may not be applicable to all regions, leading to potential bias. Third, in large countries like China, with diverse provinces and regions, local health issues may not be fully captured by national data, affecting local public health decisions. Fourth, due to the lengthy data collection, processing, and dissemination process, GBD data may lag in reflecting the latest health status and trends. Importantly, a key limitation affecting cross-national comparisons in studies like ours is the substantial variability in the quality and completeness of cancer registry data across different countries. For instance, many European cancer registries do not routinely record individual types of NMSC, 35 and incidence is often unknown due to incomplete or absent registration in most nation [ 42 ]. This heterogeneity in data sources, collection methods, and reporting standards introduces uncertainty into global comparative analyses and may obscure true epidemiological differences or similarities between regions. The GBD study attempts to harmonize these disparate data through modeling, but residual biases likely persist, and results for regions with poorer quality primary data should be interpreted with greater caution. In conclusion, the NMSC disease burden creates a heavy burden of disease globally, with men and the elderly being the most at-risk groups. With the rising number of deaths in recent years, NMSC remains an important public health problem that needs to be addressed. And large regional disparities indicate the importance of developing effective public health policies and preventive education. Raising public awareness of NMSC disease, implementing effective sun protection measures, and conducting early screening are critical to reducing the NMSC disease burden, improving quality of life, and enhancing public health outcomes.
