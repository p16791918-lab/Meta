Introduction Breast cancer (BC) is the most frequently diagnosed cancer in Asian American females. Across multiple studies, 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 mortality rates after diagnosis are lower in Asian compared with non-Latina White (hereafter, White ) females. Paradoxically, Asian females have a higher prevalence of certain poor prognostic tumor characteristics, such as ERBB2 -positive subtypes, 10 yet their survival advantage remains even after accounting for treatments, neighborhood socioeconomic status (SES), hormone receptor status, hospital characteristics, marital status, and health insurance. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Favorable survival patterns may therefore be due to factors uniquely enriched among Asian individuals, such as sociocultural, contextual, and lifestyle factors; treatment adherence and response; and cancer biological and genetic factors. We recently found, among a BC cohort diagnosed within an integrated health care system in Northern California, that lower mortality among female Asian American racial and ethnic groups compared with White females was partially explained by lower body mass index (BMI; calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared) and smoking prevalence. 11 Identifying the contributors to these favorable survival profiles among Asian individuals could inform strategies to improve survival for all patients with BC. Asian individuals are the fastest growing racial and ethnic group in the US, with an estimated doubling to 46 million by 2060. 12 They represent more than 30 countries, speak more than 100 languages, and encompass varied social and economic backgrounds. Reflecting immigration trends, the proportion of Asian individuals born outside the US varies widely across ethnicities, 12 and many cultures and lifestyles brought by Asian immigrants have been modified to varying degrees through acculturation in the US. The result is a heterogeneous population varying substantially by sociodemographic and lifestyle factors, access to health care, and cancer behavioral risk factors, with important implications for BC survival. We are aware of only 1 US study that has evaluated survival after BC by Asian race and ethnicity and nativity; this literature gap is likely due to lack of datasets with adequate information and statistical power to assess associations by Asian race and ethnicity and nativity. In that study, we applied an approach to infer nativity in cancer registry data and found 50% to 400% higher BC-specific mortality among Filipina, Chinese, and Vietnamese females born outside the US relative to their US-born counterparts, while mortality was not statistically different among Japanese females. 13 In this study, we integrated data from four epidemiological studies—each with multilevel information derived from study questionnaires, clinical and follow-up data from cancer registries, and geospatial information—to assess all-cause and BC-specific mortality across patients identifying as an Asian race or ethnicity. Combining these data, we examined the contributions of demographic, clinical, lifestyle, neighborhood, and sociodemographic factors to survival differences among females of various Asian races and ethnicities compared with White females, and the extent to which survival differs when additionally considering nativity. Assessing both BC-specific mortality and all-cause mortality will provide insights into whether the mortality advantage is due to survival pathways unique to BC or other causes of death. Methods Participating Studies The 4 epidemiologic studies included in the Asian American Resiliency and Cancer (ARC) cohort study were the Pathways Study from Kaiser Permanente Northern California, 14 the Northern California Breast Cancer Family Registry (NC-BCFR), 15 , 16 the Asian American Breast Cancer Study (AABCS), 17 and the Multiethnic Cohort (MEC). 18 These studies were initiated in California and Hawaii from the 1990s to mid-2000s. Data Pooling Process The study population included females with first primary invasive BC. We included all women who identified as Asian, including those who also identified as another race or ethnic group. As a comparison group, we included women who self-identified as non-Latina White (single race and ethnicity). Descriptive statistics are reported for Asian races and ethnicities with as much disaggregation as possible, but Korean, South Asian, Vietnamese, and other single-race Asian or unspecified Asian groups were combined into 1 group in the models given their small sample sizes. Race and ethnicity were based on self-report and included Chinese, Filipina, Japanese, Korean, South Asian, Vietnamese, another single-race or ethnicity Asian or not specified (hereafter, another Asian group ), Asian and Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, and Asian and multiple races or ethnicities who were not Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander (hereafter, multiple races and ethnicities ), as well as White. Race and ethnicity were evaluated as social constructs in this study. Questionnaire Data Questionnaire data from the studies were harmonized for education, marital status, birthplace, and reproductive factors (eg, parity [number of full-term pregnancies], age at first full-term pregnancy, oral contraception use, menopausal status, menopausal hormone therapy use) (eTable 1 in Supplement 1 ). We also obtained data on family history of BC, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and history of diabetes. Baseline information was provided at study enrollment, except for MEC, a prospective cohort study in which follow-up questionnaires were also obtained. When possible, we used the updated information provided at the time closest to BC diagnosis (before or after) for MEC. Among Asian individuals, the World Health Organization Asian–specific categories for underweight or normal BMI ( 23), overweight (23.0-27.4), and obesity (≥27.5) were used. 19 , 20 Clinical and Treatment Data We obtained clinical data from the California Cancer Registry, Kaiser Permanente Northern California Registry, and Hawaii Tumor Registry, including tumor summary stage (local, regional, distant), grade, size, nodal involvement, histology, estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR) status (positive or negative), first-course treatments received, and insurance status. Given the years of diagnosis of the cohort, ERBB2 information was available for only 20% of Asian and 40% of White participants and thus not included in the models. Neighborhood Data Neighborhood SES was based on the census tract of residence at diagnosis and measured using an established multicomponent index derived from a principal component analysis of 7 US Census (1990 and 2000) and American Community Survey (ACS; 2008-2012 and 2018-2022) constructs: education, employment, poverty, occupation, household income, home value, and rent. 21 Neighborhood SES was categorized into state-specific quintiles. The census tract–level Asian racial and ethnic enclave variable was derived using 4 Census and ACS characteristics to capture racial and ethnic composition, immigration, and linguistic characteristics 22 and was similarly categorized into state-specific quintiles. The enclave measure was not available for females diagnosed from 1992 to 1995. Statistical Analysis Follow-up data through the end of 2020 were obtained through the state and KPNC cancer registries, which in turn were determined through active and passive means, primarily linkage to vital statistic records. BC-specific cause of death was defined using International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision ( ICD-10 ) code C50. Kaplan-Meier curves were used to visualize all-cause and BC-specific mortality over time by racial and ethnic group (eFigure 1 and eFigure 2 in Supplement 1 ). Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs for all-cause mortality and BC-specific mortality (using Fine-Gray models for competing risks) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards (PH) regression models for each Asian ethnicity compared with White females. The PH assumption was assessed using the correlation between time and scaled Schoenfeld residuals for each covariate. Due to PH violations, we included as strata study, stage, 10-year age categories, and ER or PR status. Spline functions were included for age (continuous) and year of diagnosis, allowing for potential nonlinear effects of these variables on the HR estimates. For models with neighborhood SES and Asian ethnic enclave, we adjusted for clustering by census tract using robust sandwich covariance matrix estimates to account for potential correlation among participants living in the same tracts. 23 To assess which factors were associated with mortality differences between Asian females and White females, groups of factors were included in separate models, built on the baseline model (eTable 1 in Supplement 1 ). The fully adjusted model included race and ethnicity and all covariates. We also examined HRs for combinations of races and ethnicities and birthplace as US-born or born outside the US. Unknown values were included in models as their own categories, except for missing nodes and diabetes history, which were excluded to improve model fit due to PH violation. Analyses were conducted in SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute) and conducted in February 2024 to February 2026. To assess the potential impact of missing data, we conducted sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation for missing covariates in both the base and fully adjusted models. Multiple imputation was performed in SAS PROC MI using fully conditional specification under a missing-at-random assumption, incorporating all variables included in the analytic models. P values were 2-sided, and statistical significance was set at P .05 Results Of 9723 individuals with BC, we excluded those who had a history of in situ BC (122 individuals), had a history of prior cancers except nonmelanoma skin cancer (555 individuals), were not female (14 individuals), were not linked to a cancer registry record (7 individuals), participated in more than 1 study (ie, duplicate study records; 13 individuals), or did not meet the criteria for included race and ethnicity categories on further review (18 individuals). The final sample included 8994 females (mean [SD] age at diagnosis, 59 [12.8] years), with 3973 Asian females and 5021 White females. There were 2637 total deaths and 1140 deaths attributed to BC during a mean follow-up time of 12.6 years (range, 1 month to 28 years). The final study population included 3973 Asian females with BC, with 1339 Chinese females, 1259 Filipina females, 525 Japanese females, 78 Korean females, 73 South Asian females, 43 Vietnamese females, 11 females identifying as another Asian ethnic group, 354 females who identified as Asian and Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, and 291 females who identified as multiple races or ethnicities, as well as 5021 White females. With the exception of the Asian and Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander group, all other Asian groups had a higher proportion diagnosed with BC before age 50 years compared with the White group: from 34% among Filipina females to 73% among females identifying as another Asian group, compared with 15% among White females; one-quarter of Vietnamese females and females identifying as another Asian group were diagnosed at younger than age 40 years ( Table 2 ). Except for Korean females, all Asian groups had lower proportions (45%-67%) of localized stage disease relative to White females (69%). Filipina females, South Asian females, Vietnamese females, and females identifying as another Asian group were less likely to have radiation therapy (27%-39%) than Chinese (43%) and Japanese (47%) females. All Asian groups, except for the other Asian ethnicity group and Asian and Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander group, were more likely to have chemotherapy than the White group, with 42% to 62% vs 38%, respectively, undergoing chemotherapy. Proportions receiving surgery ( 90%) were similar across all groups except for the Asian and Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander group, who had the lowest surgery rate (79%). Except for the Asian and Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander group, all Asian groups had lower proportions (46%-60%) of positivity for both ER and PR compared with the White group (62%). Chinese, Filipina, and Japanese females were more likely to be married, premenopausal at diagnosis, never-users of oral contraceptives or menopausal hormone therapy, and have a first full-term pregnancy at age 25 years or older than White females ( Table 3 ). Compared with 49% of White females, nearly 80% of Asian females had never smoked, from 91% in Chinese females, to 65% in Japanese females, and 48% in Asian and Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander females. Higher proportions of Asian females reported not drinking alcohol than White females. Higher proportions of Chinese (51%) and Japanese (46%) females were not overweight compared with White females (40%). Chinese females were more likely to have less than a high school education than White females (21% vs 3%). The percentage of females who had completed a college education varied, with 36% among Chinese females, 39% among Filipina females, 25% among Japanese females, 55% among South Asian females, and 48% among White females. Asian females were less likely to have a family history of BC than White females, ranging from 2% in Vietnamese to 18% in another Asian group, vs 25% in White females. Groups with the highest percentages living in the lowest-SES neighborhoods were Asian and Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander females (21%), females identifying as multiple races or ethnicities (13%), and Filipina females (10%). In contrast, only 3% of Japanese females, 1% of South Asian females, and 5% of White females lived in the lowest-SES neighborhoods. Compared with White females, all Asian groups were more likely to live in a neighborhood in the highest quintile for Asian enclaves, except for Asian and Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander females. Compared with White females, most Asian groups showed numerically lower, but not statistically significantly lower, HRs for BC-specific mortality (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1 ) in base and subsequent models ( Table 4 ). When stratified by stage, Asian groups had lower HRs compared with White females only for localized disease (eTable 2 in Supplement 1 ). We conducted a sensitivity analysis comparing the use of World Health Organization Asian-specific BMI categories for Asian females (main analysis) with standard BMI categories applied to all females. HRs for race and ethnicity for all-cause and BC-specific mortality were generally consistent across the 2 approaches. Estimates were closer toward the null when standard BMI categories were used, but differences were small and did not alter the overall interpretation of the results. We also compared our results from the base and fully adjusted models to analyses using multiple imputation for missing covariates. Results were similar to the primary analysis. The HRs differed modestly, CIs overlapped, and there was no change in the overall interpretation of findings for race and ethnicity (eTable 5 in Supplement 1 ). We examined all-cause and BC-specific mortality among Asian females defined by race and ethnicity and nativity (born outside the US vs US-born) compared with White females (regardless of nativity) ( Table 5 ). For most Asian groups, numbers of individuals born in the US were small, with inconsistent nativity patterns across ethnicities. To assess generalizability of the ARC sample, we compared characteristics to Asian and White females with BC in the population-based California Cancer Registry (CCR) (eTable 3 in Supplement 1 ). Clinical characteristics were similar between the ARC sample and CCR data. However, for neighborhood SES, a higher proportion of White females from the ARC study were living in the highest SES areas (36%) than White females in CCR (30%). We also found similar HRs for Asian females compared with White females for all-cause and BC-specific mortality in the ARC pooled dataset compared with HRs from the CCR (eTable 4 in Supplement 1 ). Discussion In this cohort study using a pooled dataset with nearly 4000 Asian females with BC, we evaluated potential contributions of multilevel factors to lower mortality among females identifying as diverse Asian racial and ethnic groups compared with White females with BC. Several Asian groups (Chinese and Japanese) had significantly lower all-cause mortality than the White group. BC-specific mortality among Asian females was similar to White females; HR estimates were less than 1.0 but not statistically significant for all Asian groups except for the Asian and Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander group. HRs for Asian groups varied depending on which set of covariates (clinical, reproductive, behavioral, and socioeconomic factors) was included, with no pattern of specific factors driving mortality differences between Asian and White females. The observed differences in all-cause mortality but lack of differences in BC-specific mortality suggest that the differences in all-cause mortality are likely due to causes other than BC disease progression. This is further reinforced by the fact that both all-cause and BC-specific mortality HRs were essentially unchanged after adding tumor factors and treatments to the base model. Lower mortality in Asian females with BC has been observed in several studies across a range of time periods and with varying adjustments for clinical, neighborhood, and lifestyle factors. Although risk estimates varied by study and were not always statistically significant, the general pattern was consistent with lower mortality risk in Asian females compared with White females. Within an integrated health care system in Northern California, we found similar patterns of lower mortality among Asian females compared with White females with BC for overall but not BC-specific mortality, with BMI and smoking attenuating but not fully explaining the associations. 11 Our analysis is complementary to and extends on this prior study based on electronic health record data from a single integrated health care system, with our study having a broader study population based on pooling data across 4 epidemiological studies and incorporates a more extensive set of sociodemographic and reproductive factors ascertained from patient self-report in the epidemiologic studies. The somewhat consistent results between the 2 analyses demonstrate that observed survival resiliency among Asian American patients compared with White patients were not due to differences in sociodemographic, reproductive, or clinical factors examined in this study, but differences driven by body size and composition warrant further investigation. In the California Breast Cancer Survivorship Consortium, 1 which pooled data from 6 BC studies (1505 Asian females), Asian females as an aggregated group had lower all-cause (HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.57-1.01) and BC-specific (HR, 0.60; 95% CI, 0.37-0.97) mortality compared with White females, and these estimates were not affected by clinical, lifestyle, or neighborhood SES factors. 24 However, that analysis was underpowered to disaggregate Asian races and ethnicities. In population-based CCR data, 3 Asian females with BC had favorable survival, with HRs ranging from 0.6 to 0.8 (compared with White females) for Chinese, Filipina, and Japanese, females. This pattern persisted after adjusting for treatment, neighborhood SES, ER or PR status, hospital characteristics, marital status, and health insurance. Another study using CCR data for BC diagnosed from 2000 to 2013 also found lower BC-specific mortality in Asian females, but the finding was only statistically significant for Filipina, Japanese, and Southeast Asian females. 25 Three prior studies used Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) data to examine Asian women with 8 disaggregated races or ethnicities. 2 , 4 , 7 Using data from 1991 to 2007, lower risk of BC-specific mortality was observed for all Asian groups, although HRs were not statistically significant for any group except Japanese and other Asian females compared with White females. 2 A more recent SEER analysis also found Asian females had better overall and disease-specific survival than White females across all Asian groups, except for Southeast Asian females for cancer-specific survival. 4 A novel contribution of the ARC study is the ability to examine mortality patterns among Asian racial and ethnic groups by birthplace. We found that nativity patterns were not consistent across Asian groups, with lower mortality relative to White individuals for Chinese and Filipina females born outside the US and lower mortality for Japanese females and females identifying as multiple races or ethnicities born in the US, demonstrating important heterogeneity across Asian groups and the relevance of immigration and acculturation to cancer survival. In the ARC sample, some demographic factors differed by birthplace, such as education, where females born outside the US generally had lower education levels than those born in the US; however, these differences did not explain mortality differences by nativity, suggesting other, unexamined factors may be responsible. A study by Aoki et al 26 found that, despite lower education and income among Asian females with BC born outside the US vs those born in the US (although still higher than US-born White females), Asian females born outside the US were more likely than Asian females born in the US to have a healthier lifestyle based on the cancer prevention healthy lifestyle index. 26 Although our mortality models adjusted for selected health behaviors, a more detailed assessment of anthropometric (eg, waist circumference) and dietary factors (eg, fruit and vegetable consumption) may explain some of the observed mortality differences by nativity. Limitations A potential limitation of the ARC study is heterogeneity across the 4 pooled studies, including differences in inclusion criteria, selection and response biases, variability in survey instruments, and timing of study enrollment (3 postdiagnosis and 1 prediagnosis). However, ARC participants were similar to the CCR sample when comparing distributions of key factors and mortality results, indicating generalizability to population-based data. To account for potential differences across studies, we included study as a stratum in proportional hazards regression models. Another limitation was absence of detailed treatment information and incomplete data on ERBB2 status. While we observed differences by nativity, we did not include detailed information on length of residence in the US for females born outside the US, which may influence potential acculturation-related exposures and health behaviors. Furthermore, we included diagnoses from 1992 through 2019, a wide time range in which secular trends in BC diagnosis and treatment may have affected results. Conclusions In this cohort study, Asian females had lower overall mortality than White females for most Asian racial and ethnic groups. This was seen even after accounting for clinical factors, lifestyle factors, multilevel SES factors, and comorbidities. Further details could provide insight into whether other factors might explain some variation in mortality differences. The ARC study is further investigating central obesity, specific lifestyle factors (eg, diet), tumor molecular factors, and specific neighborhood characteristics. Identification of these factors and others will provide insights into mechanisms that may improve survival after BC, with potential to benefit all populations.
