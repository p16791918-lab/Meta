Introduction The US NCI’s Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program collects and publishes cancer incidence and survival data from SEER-funded population-based cancer registries. The first seven SEER registries began collecting data in 1973, with two more joining in 1975. These nine registries, collectively known as SEER 9, comprise approximately 10% of the US population. In 1992, four more registries were added to increase representation of racial minorities, resulting in a total of 13 registries collectively known as SEER 13. SEER has continued to grow over time and today includes 22 registries covering almost 50% of the US population, with all registries having data going back at least to the diagnosis year 2000. Also, in 1992, the National Program of Cancer Registries (NPCR) was established and administered by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (https://www.cdc.gov/national-program-cancer-registries/about/index.html). Together, NPCR and SEER provide funding for the collection of cancer incidence data for the entire US population, with some registries funded by both programs. Canada has a similar cancer surveillance program. Beginning with cases diagnosed in 1992, cancer incidence data collected by provincial and territorial cancer registries have been reported to Statistics Canada to populate the Canadian Cancer Registry ( https://www23.statcan.gc.ca/imdb/p2SV.pl?Function=getSurvey SDDS=3207 ). All registries in the United States and Canada are members of the North American Association of Central Cancer Registries (NAACCR; https://www.naaccr.org/ ), which is a professional organization that develops and promotes uniform data standards for cancer registration. Registries make separate submissions to each of the registry programs of which they are a member (Canadian Cancer Registry, NAACCR, NPCR, SEER). SEER registries report to the NCI on November 1, submitting all cases diagnosed at least 22 months prior, allowing for an approximate 2-year lag in reporting for the consolidation of records for each person, coding, editing, and other quality assurance processes at each registry. For example, cases diagnosed in 2020 are first reported to SEER in November 2022, along with updates of cases diagnosed in earlier years. NPCR and NAACCR programs have a similar timeline. In between submissions, cancer registries add newly discovered cases and remove or modify previously reported cases based on the continuing consolidation and adjudication of data from multiple sources. The nonstatic nature of registry data reflects the complexity of the cancer care delivery system and the data collection process, in which cases diagnosed in outpatient settings can take longer to locate the patient’s records, and important information such as the cancer site or patient’s age or race may have initially been missing or misreported due to limited data in outpatient clinics. For example, two separate cases are found to be for the same person after the consolidation of records from various sources; a case is determined to have a different primary cancer site than originally determined; or the race is changed from unknown to a specific race. The initially reported case count for a given diagnosis year tends to be smaller than the updated counts later reported for that year. This means that the most recent diagnosis year will have a larger undercount than earlier diagnosis years that have longer follow-up periods. Failing to account for this differential bias can lead to spurious results and hinder timely reporting of cancer incidence trends. For example, Horm and colleagues ( 1 ) reported in 1986 that the age-adjusted lung cancer incidence rate for White males in SEER 9 declined 4.1% between 1982 and 1983 (from 82.7 to 79.3 cases per 100,000 person-years), indicating a long-anticipated downturn in lung cancer incidence consistent with the earlier decline in smoking rates. Current data, however, indicate that the incidence rate for White males continued to increase until 1988 before beginning a steady decline ( https://seer.cancer.gov/statistics-network/explorer/application.html ). Cancer data without adjustment for reporting delay produce biased estimates of cancer rates and misleading cancer trend analyses, which could potentially have large implications for cancer surveillance, public health awareness, medical research planning, and health-care resource funding. Although originally the bias in trends in observed incidence rates caused by reporting delays was ignored or adjusted in ad hoc ways, this approach quickly became untenable. This led to the development of statistical methods for estimating “delay-adjusted” rates for the SEER 9 registries ( 2 , 3 ), which were later modified for the SEER 13 registries ( 4 ). As the registry environment evolved to include more registries across the United States and Canada, it became apparent that these models had to be adapted to the larger registry community. However, there were significant challenges in modifying the original structure of these models. NAACCR introduced a certification program based on standardized criteria measuring completeness, timeliness, and accuracy of data in 1997. As more registries were initiated and complied with NAACCR standards, it became more difficult to integrate them into a single model, taking into account the varying number of submission years, which is essential for estimating reporting delays. Moreover, SEER registries generally had consistent reporting of high quality (as this was a requirement for admission to the program). Other registry programs encouraged the development of nascent registries so that, especially in their early years, many had “gaps” in reporting when a registry did not report, did not meet NAACCR quality standards, or had anomalies in reporting. Anomalies in reporting can occur, for example, when they lose experienced staff or when a registry identifies specific prior issues, reviews data across several prior diagnosis years, and makes corrections as necessary. The original statistical models for SEER registries were prone to lack of fit or convergence issues when used with non-SEER registries. Also, rates by race were initially only reported for Whites and Blacks, and subsequent demand for more racial and ethnic specificity in reporting required changes to the models, especially as cases were sometimes first reported with missing race or ethnicity and were only later updated. Finally, with SEER 9 and SEER 13 registries, we were content to estimate delay-adjusted rates for these large, stable combinations of registries, but in this new environment, there was a growing need to produce rates for any combination of registries desired by the user. This article presents a unique story of how a statistical model must be modified to adapt to changing data and user needs within the evolving landscape of population-based cancer registration in the United States and Canada. The lessons learned here apply not only to this specific problem but also to the more general issue of adapting the framework and implementation of a statistical model used as part of a maturing reporting system. Although we focus on cancer registries, the methods described are applicable to other population-based disease registries, including those for AIDS, cardiovascular disease, stroke ( 5 ), and chronic kidney disease ( 6 ). Materials and Methods Modeling reporting delay We consider a discrete-time model. Let Y i j be the number of cases diagnosed at time t i and first reported at time t i + d j , i = 1 , … , m , j = 1 , … , J , with 0 t i t i + 1 and 0 d j d j + 1 . In cancer registry data, t i is the year of diagnosis, and d j is the number of years since diagnosis. The lag time d j between diagnosis and reporting is called the reporting delay, and the cumulative sums N i j = ∑ k = 1 j Y i k , j = 1 , … , J , provide a series of snapshots of the case count for diagnosis year t i over time. By convention, the case count for diagnosis year t i is considered complete at time t i + d J . At time t i + d k , N ij is unobservable for j k , and the goal is to predict the complete case count N iJ given N ik . Models for reporting delay were first proposed in the actuarial literature in the context of “incurred but not reported” insurance claims ( 7 , 8 ). In the 1980s, the AIDS epidemic spurred research into delay models among statisticians ( 9 – 11 ). The popular reporting delay model, described by Harris and colleagues ( 11 ) and used for the reporting of AIDS incidence, assumes the Y ij are independent Poisson variables. The original SEER model In 2002, Clegg and colleagues ( 2 ) proposed a model for cancer incidence data that includes both reporting delay and reporting errors. Cancer registries collect information from multiple sources, including hospitals, doctors’ offices, pathology and blood testing labs, and vital status records. These separate records are linked using identifying information and consolidated into a single case report. Incorrect linkages, unknown values, or mistakes in the original records can cause reporting errors, including duplicate entries and miscoding of the cancer site or demographic information such as age or race. Correction of these errors over time can lead to positive or negative changes in reported incidence, making the Poisson model considered by Harris and colleagues ( 11 ) inappropriate for this type of data. The model of Clegg and colleagues ( 2 ) [see also Midthune and colleagues ( 3 ) for a more technical description] assumed data of the form ( Y ij , Z ij ), available in SEER, where Y ij is the number of cases first reported at time t i + d j and Z ij is the number of previously reported cases removed at time t i + d j . They proposed modeling the Y ij as independent Poisson variables and Z i ( j +1) as conditionally binomial given net count N i j = ∑ k = 1 j ( Y i k - Z i k ) . Maximum reporting delay d J , the time when reporting is considered complete, was set equal to the largest value for which N iJ was observable; as a result, the largest delay time was increased by one in each subsequent submission, changing the interpretation of the delay-adjusted rates slightly from year to year as more data were collected. Because the pattern of reporting delay differs by cancer site (see Fig. 1 ), the delay model needs to be fit separately to each cancer site. Figure 1. Since 2004, these delay-adjusted incidence rates have been published in the SEER Cancer Statistics Review ( https://seer.cancer.gov/csr/1975_2001/ ), the Annual Report to the Nation on the Status of Cancer ( 12 ), and SEER Explorer ( https://seer.cancer.gov/explorer/ ), which replaced the Cancer Statistics Review in 2021. The model was originally implemented in the combined pool of SEER 9 registries and later modified to handle the more complex data structure of the SEER 13 registries, in which the additional four registries had different dates when they started collecting data ( 4 ). As the four additional registries had been operating for a shorter time, reporting delays for them were modeled separately from those for SEER 9. This resulted in two sets of registries that had been adjusted to different maximum reporting delays ( d J ); the adjusted counts in the four newer registries were then further adjusted under the assumption that by the end of the shorter maximum delay, the percentage of still-unreported cases in the two sets of registries was the same ( 4 ). The new US–Canadian consolidated model In 2010, the NCI, NPCR, and NAACCR began a collaboration to develop a standard approach to adjust cancer incidence rates for reporting delay in US and Canadian cancer registries. A decision was made that modeling pooled groups of registries was boxing us into a solution that would not be tenable in the long term. With the increasing number of registries, modeling every possible grouping of interest to analysts became infeasible. Therefore, we shifted to modeling reporting delay for individual registries and developed a method to dynamically estimate delay-adjusted rates for any possible grouping. The goal, therefore, was to develop a parsimonious yet robust model that would be fit separately to each registry to produce delay-adjusted case counts. A further requirement was that the delay-adjusted counts be summable across registries to facilitate the estimation of incidence rates for any combination of registries (e.g., geographic region, NPCR registries only). The original SEER model was not well suited to meet these goals because the model was designed for aggregated registries such as SEER 9. When applied to single registries, especially those having relatively few cases or unusual reporting patterns, the model was prone to convergence issues. Furthermore, non-SEER registries did not have readily available separate data for additions and deletions of individual cases for each submission [i.e., ( Y ij , Z ij )], and although such data could be constructed from archived data, it would take considerable time and effort. It was decided that the new standard approach would model the net counts N ij rather than ( Y ij , Z ij ) and use generalized estimating equations (GEE; ref. 13 ) rather than a likelihood approach. Although the net counts N ij are marginally Poisson under the original SEER model, they have a complex joint distribution, with the conditional distribution of N i ( j +1) given N ij being a convolution of Poisson and binomial distributions. The GEE approach allows us to ignore the complexity of the joint distribution. We refer to the new standard model as the US–Canadian model or, from a technical standpoint, the GEE model. The US–Canadian model is fit separately to each registry and cancer site. To allow patterns of reporting delay to differ by demographic characteristics, we assume the population covered by a registry is partitioned into n S subpopulations. For a given registry, let N sij be the cumulative case count for cancers diagnosed in subpopulation s at time t i and reported by time t i + d j , s = 1, ..., n S , i = 1, …, m , and j = 1, …, J . At the most recent reporting time ( t* ), N sij is observed if t i + d j ≤ t* , but otherwise represents an unobserved future count. Let J i be the maximum value of j for which N sij is observed ( J i does not depend on s but may differ by registry). In SEER and NAACCR, in which cases for a given diagnosis year ( t i ) are first reported after a two-year delay and then reported annually: t* = t m + 2, d j = j + 1, and J i = m – i + 1. As in the original SEER model, we assume that the conditional mean of the reported case count N s i ( j + 1 ) given previous counts depends only on the most recent count, that is, E ( N s i ( j + 1 ) | N s i 1 , … , N s i j ) = δ s i j N s i j , (A) so that the predicted complete case count given the observed counts is E ( N s i J | N s i 1 , … , N s i J i ) = δ s i N s i J i , (B) where δ s i = ∏ j = J i J - 1 δ s i j . Under assumption (A) , the ratios R s i j = N s i ( j + 1 ) / N s i j have means E ( R s i j ) = δ s i j and are uncorrelated with each other and with the N sij , and we can specify a model for R sij to estimate parameters δ s i j . To preserve the multiplicative structure in (B) , we model R sij on the log scale. Letting W sij = log( R sij ), i = 1, …, m , and j = 1, …, J – 1, we assume a linear model: W s i j = X s i j T β + ε s i j , (C) where X sij is a q × 1 vector of design variables, β is a vector of fixed effects, and var ( ε s i j ) = σ j 2 to allow for decreasing σ j 2 over time as the net counts stabilize. The design variables X sij allow the ratios to vary by diagnosis year (i), reporting delay (j), and demographic variables such as age and race. The parameters in (C) are estimated using GEE with a diagonal working correlation matrix, which provides consistent estimates of β as long as the mean of W sij is correctly specified. The covariance matrix of parameter estimates β ^ is estimated using the GEE robust “sandwich” estimator, V ^ β ^ ( 13 ). GEE estimators are robust in that they do not make distributional assumptions about the error terms ε s i j . We estimate δ s i j by δ ^ s i j = exp ( X s i j T β ^ ) , which is conservative and can be justified by a Taylor series approximation. When j = J i , N i ( j + 1 ) represents a future (unobserved) case count that can be predicted by N ^ s i j = δ ^ s i j N s i j . By iteratively applying this formula, the complete case count N s i J can be predicted by N ^ s i = δ ^ s i N s i J i , where δ ^ s i = ∏ j = J i J - 1 δ ^ s i j . This approach is similar in spirit to the “chain ladder” technique used in actuarial science ( 14 ). We refer to N ^ s i as the “delay-adjusted case count” for diagnosis year i and δ ^ s i as the “delay adjustment factor,” or, more simply, the “delay factor,” for subpopulation s . The delay factor characterizes the effect of delay adjustment. For example, a value of δ ^ s i = N ^ s i / N s i J i = 1.02 represents a 2% increase over the observed case count N s i J i . Because case counts tend to increase over time, delay factors are almost always ≥1. The delay factors ( δ ^ s i = N ^ s i / N s i J i ) characterize the effect of delay adjustment on case counts in a single subpopulation. As the effect of delay adjustment on multiple subgroups of the population does not depend on a single delay factor, we define a more general “composite” delay factor, δ ^ i ( k ) = N ^ i ( k ) / N i ( k ) , which implicitly utilizes more than one delay factor to characterize the effect of delay adjustment on case counts in subgroup k , where N i ( k ) = ∑ s = 1 n S N s i ( k ) is the unadjusted case count for subgroup k . To distinguish δ ^ s i from δ ^ i ( k ) , we call δ ^ s i a “primary” delay factor. An important consideration in specifying the delay model is the choice of J that determines when reporting is considered complete. For delay-adjusted counts to be summable across registries, they must be adjusted to a common delay time d J . Our original approach was to choose the largest possible d J in order to make delay adjustment as complete as possible, so we set J = max( J i ), the maximum delay time with observable counts [in any registry; without further restrictions, identifiability requires that J ≤ max( J i )]. This approach worked well for the SEER 9 registries, which all have the same maximum observed delay times, but quickly led to complications requiring two adjustments when applied to SEER 13 (see The original SEER model section). As the number of registries grew and with the registries having different starting years, this approach became less practical, and we adopted a more straightforward approach that requires d J to have observable counts in all registries. A further consideration when choosing J is that patterns of reporting delay tend to change over time and have fewer missing data (due to registries more consistently meeting NAACCR quality standards) as registries gain experience and technologies improve, making reporting delays from diagnosis years that occurred in the distant past less predictive of current patterns. As only the most recent diagnosis years ( t m – J + 1, t m – J + 2, ..., t m ) are needed to fit the model, one can ignore earlier years by choosing an appropriate J . Under this consideration, the choice of J becomes a compromise between making the delay adjustment as complete as possible (larger J ) and using the most recent and relevant data (smaller J ). In our implementation, we set J = 10, corresponding to a delay of d J = 11 years; for most cancer sites, SEER- and NPCR-funded registries are at least 99% complete after 11 years compared with 17 years (see Fig. 1 ). Even the nonsolid tumors (leukemias and myeloma), which tend to have longer reporting delays, are still at least 98% complete in 11 years. In order to have multiple observations at delay time d J = 11, we include two extra diagnosis years in the analysis data [i.e., ( t m – 11, t m – 10, ..., t m )] as shown in Table 3 . As J = 10 is kept constant as t m increases each year, the analysis data are continuously updated to include only the most recent diagnosis years, in which the overall quality of most registries has improved significantly. Diagnosis years with 11 or more years of follow-up are considered complete, and the counts are not further adjusted for reporting delay. Variance of delay-adjusted counts For a given registry, cancer site, and diagnosis year i , let δ ^ i = ( δ ^ 1 i , … , δ ^ n S i ) T be the n S × 1 vector of delay adjustment factors for all subpopulations, and let N i = ( N 1 i J i , . . . , N n S i J i ) T be the corresponding vector of the most recent case counts. The delay-adjusted case count for the total population is N ^ i = δ ^ i T N i . The covariance matrix of δ ^ i can be estimated using the delta method, V ^ δ ^ i = Δ ^ i X i T V ^ β ^ X i Δ ^ i , where V ^ β ^ is the estimated covariance matrix of regression parameters β ^ and Δ ^ i = diag ( δ ^ i ) is the n S × n S diagonal matrix with diagonal elements ( Δ ^ i ) s s = δ ^ s i and X i = ( X 1 i , … , X n S i ) . Similarly, under the assumption that the elements of N i are marginally Poisson, the variance of N ^ i can be estimated as follows: v a ^ r ( N ^ i ) = δ ^ i T D i δ ^ i + N i T V ^ δ ^ i N i , (D) where D i = diag( N i ). After fitting the GEE model separately to each registry, the delay-adjusted case counts N ^ i and N ^ i ( k ) can be summed over any combination of registries or subgroups of registries provided the registries are adjusted to the same maximum delay time J . As the registries are independent, the estimated variance of the sum of the delay-adjusted counts is just the sum of the estimated single-registry variances v a ^ r ( N ^ i ) and v a ^ r ( N ^ i ( k ) ) . Early estimates of SEER cancer incidence As mentioned previously, SEER registries were originally required to submit data to the NCI in November each year; these submissions do not include cases diagnosed in the current or previous calendar year. For example, cases diagnosed in 2008 were first submitted to the NCI in November 2010. This gave registries an approximate two-year period to collect cases for a particular year before having to submit them for reporting. In this setting, the reporting delay times are d j = 1 + j . Starting in 2011, motivated by the desire of the cancer research community to have estimates in a more timely manner, SEER registries were required to submit data twice a year, in February and November. The February submission covers the same diagnosis years as the later November submission, providing an earlier but less complete report of cancer incidence. Although most analyses still use the November submissions to correct for reporting delay, the February submissions have been used in combination with the November submissions to generate early estimates of SEER cancer incidence ( 15 – 18 ); see also https://seer.cancer.gov/statistics/preliminary-estimates/ . The analysis of February and November submissions to obtain early estimates of cancer incidence is similar to the regular analysis of November submissions described in The new US–Canadian consolidated model section, with the following differences. First, as the data are reported twice a year (February and November), the reporting delay times are reported in months ( d 1 = 14, d 2 = 22, d 3 = 26, d 4 = 34, ...) rather than years; we note that reporting delay is treated as an ordinal variable, and the actual values function merely as labels to help interpret results. Second, the number of cases reported in the first February submission ( j = 1) represents only about 92% to 98% of the number of cases reported the following November ( j = 2; ref. 18 ), so the need to adjust for reporting delay is even greater. Third, as the February submission began in 2011 (which included cases diagnosed through 2009), submissions will have missing data (by design) for some delay times. For example, the diagnosis year 2008 will have missing data for j = 1, and the diagnosis year 2007 will have missing data for j = 1 and 3. Data availability Datasets used in the analysis are publicly available and can be accessed through the NCI’s SEER*Stat software ( https://seer.cancer.gov/seerstat/ ). Results We illustrate some of the steps involved in adjusting cancer incidence rates for reporting delay using data from SEER 21 and the US registries in the NAACCR. Using liver and intrahepatic bile duct (IBD) cancer incidence for White males (including Hispanics) in the United States, we compare the observed and delay-adjusted incidence rates for the period from 2000 to 2018 reported by the SEER 21 registries. We show in detail the steps taken in estimating the delay factors of the by-race model for a typical registry. In addition, we present the composite delay factor from a 2-year reporting delay for all-race and by-race models from registries of NAACCR. Finally, a 1-year reporting delay model is applied to SEER 18 early submission 14-month data. The 1-year delay model is validated by comparing delay adjustment estimates to a 2-year delay model. Observed and delay-adjusted trends in liver cancer incidence Liver and IBD cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer deaths globally. It is the most rapidly increasing cause of cancer death in the United States between 2002 and 2015. Liver and IBD cancer is associated with chronic hepatitis B virus or hepatitis C virus (HCV) infections, liver cirrhosis, tobacco use, excessive alcohol consumption, obesity, and type 2 diabetes. It has one of the largest delay factors of any cancer with the exception of some soft-tissue tumors (e.g., leukemias, myeloma, and non-Hodgkin lymphoma; see Table 5 ). We applied model C to liver and IBD cancer cases of White males (including Hispanics). The joinpoint model ( 19 ), developed at the NCI, has been extensively utilized to summarize trends in population-based cancer rates and other health indices. In this study, it is applied to the trend of both the delay-adjusted rates and observed rates. The joinpoint model identifies both the number and location of the change points of linear trends on the log rate. Because the modeling is conducted on the log rate, the slope of each segment increases at a constant annual percentage change. The joinpoint model fit for observed incidence rates shows a significant increase of 2.76% annually from 2006 to 2015 and a significant decrease of 2.34% annually from 2015 to 2018. The joinpoint model fit for the delay-adjusted rate, however, shows a similar upward trend from 2006 to 2014 with a significant 3.2% increase annually but with a flat most recent trend of a 0.17% nonsignificant increase between 2014 and 2018. Without delay adjustment, the most recent trend modeled by observed data could lead to a misleading conclusion of a rapid decline in incidence rates of liver and IBD cancer. The result shows that the incidence rate for liver and IBD cancer, in fact, has not yet started a downward trend, but rather, that an increasing trend has now stabilized. We chose liver and IBD to demonstrate that unadjusted observed data can lead to incorrect conclusions about trends. The reporting delay of liver cancer could be influenced by the rapid mortality of the disease, and registries may not ascertain the death until they complete death clearance. Another possible reason is the complex medical histories often seen in patients with liver cancer, such as chronic liver disease and hepatitis, which can complicate diagnosis ( 20 ). Obtaining early estimates for SEER using a 1-year reporting delay for cases diagnosed through 2016 The delay model was applied to the November 2018 submission data for SEER 18 cases diagnosed through 2016, and the delay-adjusted rates and their associated cancer trends were published based on this model. SEER 18 registries also submitted preliminary data in February 2018 for cases diagnosed through the same diagnosis year (2016). Using these preliminary data with a 1-year delay model to predict the eventual counts allows the reporting of cancer trends 8 months earlier (see https://seer.cancer.gov/statistics/preliminary-estimates/ ). However, the preliminary data usually have substantially larger delay factors compared with the regular November submission. We validate the 1-year delay model by comparing the delay-adjusted rates predicted by using the February 2018 and the subsequent November 2018 submissions for cases diagnosed in 2016. The comparison for all cancer sites combined and 24 cancer sites by sex using the SEER 18 registries for all races for cases diagnosed in 2016 is provided in Supplementary Table S1. When comparing the observed age-adjusted rates (Supplementary Table S1), as expected, the November 2018 submission data have larger rates than the February 2018 submission data. After delay adjustment, the age-adjusted rates between the November 2018 and February 2018 submissions become comparable. Discussion The evolving development of the reporting delay model for North American cancer registry data continues. We have progressed a long way from the initial recognition that reporting delay existed and needed to be accounted for to its current implementation in software available to analysts using data from all North American registries. The liver and IBD cancer results shown in the Results section are consistent with the findings that liver cancer rates have been increasing steadily for decades, and the increases in incidence and mortality from liver cancer in the United States are closely related to chronic HCV infection. HCV is generally asymptomatic and has a particularly high prevalence in the baby boomers, a birth cohort born between 1945 and 1965 ( 21 ). This cohort had an elevated risk of acquiring an HCV infection associated with such causes as receiving tainted blood and blood products before testing of the blood supply began in 1992, or from injecting drugs, even if only once in the past. Reasons why some cancers have larger delay factors are not completely understood but have been indirectly inferred. Reporting of incidence for cancer sites with larger delay factors seems to be affected by the diagnosing facility type. For example, prostate cancer and melanoma are often diagnosed in independent practices instead of hospitals. Disease management plans, such as active surveillance versus curative-intent treatment, may also play a role; some may take longer to consolidate a case. The time it takes to consolidate a case is related to the size of the “footprint” that the diagnosis of specific cancers leaves on the medical care system. Cancer registrars spend most of their time looking for information to consolidate their case information in places where large numbers of cases are typically diagnosed and treated. Small practices in which few cases are diagnosed, new practices are opening and closing, and some may be across state lines are more difficult to track efficiently. Nonsolid tumors (e.g., myeloma and leukemias) have longer delay times in which diagnoses may mainly depend on blood tests conducted in a doctor’s office, and treatment may not occur immediately. As patterns of reporting delay differ by registry and cancer site (e.g., lung, colon/rectum), delay models for US and Canadian cancer registries are fitted separately by registry and cancer site. A more serious situation occurs when information needed to define subpopulations of interest is itself a cause of delay. A patient’s race and ethnicity are often missing in initial case reports and are only added in subsequent reporting years, making the reported counts less complete and the delay adjustment factors larger. AI/AN have larger delay factors partly due to delays in the time it takes to get specialized cancer care when they live in rural areas and partly due to the challenges associated with the misclassification of AI/AN individuals ( 22 ) . Patients from rural areas would probably have longer reporting delays because small rural health-care facilities, where the initial diagnosis may take place, may not be as prompt in reporting, and it often takes longer for them to be referred to tertiary care centers for specialized cancer care. The strengths of this ongoing project, now reaching its 25th anniversary, include the adaptability of the methods to meet the changing needs of both the cancer registry community and users of the data. From the outset, we realized that it was important to implement a consistent approach across all registries in the United States and Canada, conducting the modeling centrally and disseminating the results so that registries could be combined in any combination to meet the differing needs of analysts. The scale of the project necessitated a two-step automated approach, with the modeling conducted in step 1 and the dissemination of delay-adjusted rates for any selection of registries and cancer types implemented in step 2 using SEER*Stat. The continuity of such a long-term project was greatly facilitated by having a core group of staff who have been with the project since its inception and the support of NAACCR, an organization to which all registries in North America belong and which provides a vital sounding board for new methods for the analysis, presentation, and interpretation of cancer registry data. Limitations include that the reporting delay for the most recent diagnosis years is basically a projection into the future based on past patterns of delay, and if these patterns change, the projections will be biased. Another limitation of the current methodology is that the reporting delay is modeled as a function of the major variables used in basic reporting, that is, cancer site, registry, and race and ethnicity. An alternative approach could focus on some of the socioeconomic and geographic aspects of reporting delay (e.g., using the ecological characteristics of counties and possibly census tracts) and would be useful when reporting on cancer rates in areas of the country not strictly defined by registry borders (e.g., Appalachia). Work to extend delay estimates beyond the current 22 cancer sites, for subtypes of cancer, and by stage of disease is ongoing. For example, although leukemias have large delay factors, the larger delays are associated with chronic rather than acute forms of leukemia. For example, chronic lymphocytic leukemia is diagnosed through blood tests and is often not treated for many years until it progresses. In addition, training on the understanding and use of delay adjustment is ongoing ( https://seer.cancer.gov/news/seerstat-webinars.html ). Although time to the first reporting improved from 22 to 14 months after the end of the diagnosis year, allowing for preliminary trends using SEER data, even this lag time is difficult to appreciate for those who do not understand the complexities of locating and consolidating records on patients with cancer. This lag can be especially frustrating for those who want to monitor the impact of recent events on cancer incidence. For example, the COVID-19 pandemic, starting in March 2020, resulted in delays in cancer screening and diagnosis ( 23 – 26 ). We expect the impact of these delays to be an initial decline in cancer incidence as cases that would have been screen-detected are missed, followed by a subsequent increase as these cases are detected through symptoms. The preliminary 2020 cases were submitted to SEER in February 2022, with final data submitted in November 2022 and released in April 2023. When the most recent diagnosis year in a registry submission has a drop in the case count, it is important to try to ascertain if the drop is “real” or instead attributable to an individual registry/system-wide operational issue. If the issue is operational and the registry will later “catch up” after its operational issues are rectified, the delay model cannot account for this—as the model depends solely on historical delays, and a one-time spurious delay cannot be accounted for. This problem is outside the purview of delay modeling, but other indicators, such as declines in electronic biopsy pathology reporting (which is automated and generally unaffected by operational issues), are being utilized to detect these issues. Distinguishing between operational and “real” declines in reported incidence is critical as researchers study the short- and long-term impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the delayed diagnosis and treatment of cancer. Operational delays in reporting will not have an impact on the number of cases diagnosed or cancer deaths in future years (i.e., the cases were diagnosed but just took some extra time to be consolidated and reported by the registry). Real delays in the diagnosis of cases mean that these cases will be shifted to be diagnosed in a future year (a so-called COVID rebound in cases), and the delay in the diagnosis and treatment of the cancer increases the chance that these cases will die of their cancer rather than from other causes or die earlier of their cancer than they would have otherwise. Researchers have modeled the impact of the COVID-related delayed diagnosis of colorectal and female breast cancers in 2020 on future cancer-specific mortality and estimated that this would lead to about 10,000 excess deaths from breast and colorectal cancers in the United States over the next decade ( 27 ). A new project has been initiated in SEER to produce data in “real time” ( 28 ), which is being operationalized as cases being submitted 2 months after the close of the year they were diagnosed. An article ( 28 ) presents a pilot study using consolidated tumor cases from electronic pathology reports and outlines the plan and challenges of such an effort. The promise of “real-time” reporting may be feasible because electronic pathology reports are transmitted to the registry immediately and are then read and translated into registry codes using natural language processing. Of course, pathology reports do not include all of the necessary data elements, but they may be sufficient to report basic trends. Work is ongoing to realize this ambitious goal, including modifications to registry operations, registry software, and the reporting delay model.
