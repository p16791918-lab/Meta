STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS OF THIS STUDY This study utilised a large, nationally representative dataset from 17 Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) registries, encompassing over 5.6 million adult-onset cancer survivors and more than 36 million person-years of follow-up, which enhances the statistical power and generalisability of the findings. The study applied standardised incidence ratios (SIRs) and excess risk calculations with age-adjusted, sex-adjusted, race-adjusted and calendar year-adjusted expected rates, enabling robust comparisons with the general population. A detailed stratified analysis was conducted across multiple dimensions—cancer type, treatment modality, latency period and demographic subgroups—allowing for nuanced insights into the epidemiological patterns of subsequent non-keratinocyte skin cancers. The use of the SEER*Stat MP-SIR session enabled consistent handling of multiple primary cancers and time-at-risk calculations, minimising misclassification of outcomes. Limitations include the absence of individual-level data on ultraviolet exposure, genetic predisposition and immunosuppression, which may confound observed associations; in addition, basal and squamous cell carcinomas were not captured in the SEER database. Introduction In recent decades, the population of cancer survivors has experienced substantial growth, attributed to advancements in early detection, better cancer treatments and population growth. 1 However, these survivors face several medical challenges, including an increased risk of developing a second primary cancer. 1 5 It is estimated that subsequent cancers among adult-onset cancer survivors account for around 20% of all new cancer diagnoses annually. 6 With the exception of studies focusing on survivors of childhood or young adult cancers, 2 5 there are few large-scale investigations that comprehensively examine the risks of developing secondary primary cancers in adult-onset cancer survivors. 7 Knowledge regarding the long-term risk of developing subsequent non-keratinocyte skin cancers (NKSCs)—the most common cancer type globally—in this population is conspicuously lacking. Two recent studies conducted in Southern California have shown that patients recently diagnosed with breast cancer or thyroid cancer, particularly those undergoing radiation therapy, exhibit a heightened risk of developing subsequent NKSCs, although these studies did not specifically focus on adult-onset cancer survivors. 8 9 As a result, comprehensive large-scale assessments of the overall risk of secondary NKSCs among adult-onset cancer survivors, irrespective of whether they have received radiotherapy or chemotherapy, hold significant public health and clinical relevance. In this study, we utilise the Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results (SEER) Program database, which collects comprehensive information on cancer incidence, patient demographics, tumour characteristics, treatment and survival from multiple regions across the USA, to analyse the risk of subsequent NKSCs in adult-onset cancer survivors. Besides evaluating the overall risk, we also identify specific risk factors associated with cancer subtypes, subsequent cancer sites, latency patterns and treatment modalities. A thorough and simultaneous investigation of secondary NKSC incidence across a diverse range of cancer survivors is essential for developing a comprehensive strategy for quality surveillance and follow-up care. Materials and methods Data sources and acquisition The data used in the study are publicly available and deidentified in the SEER database, 10 and thus the study was exempted from institutional review by the Xiangya hospital ethics committee and informed consent. Survivors of first primary cancers diagnosed in individuals aged 20–84 years between 2000 and 2021, across 17 registries in the SEER Program, were included in the study and classified based on the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, 3rd edition/World Health Organization 2008 (ICD-O-3/WHO2008) definition, encompassing a total of 35 types of cancers ( online supplemental Table S1 ). The study followed the guidelines set by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology. NKSCs ascertainment Subsequent NKSCs (ie, those other than squamous or basal cell skin cancers) were identified through the ICD-O-3 histology code ( online supplemental Table S2 ). We exclude those with subsequent cutaneous melanomas following a first primary cutaneous melanoma to mitigate the potential bias from misclassification of recurrence. 7 For similar methodological considerations, patients with a first primary NKSC other than melanoma were not included in the study. The default latency exclusion period of 2 months set by SEER*Stat was applied to prevent temporal overlap with the first primary cancer. 11 Second and higher order cancers were incorporated into the observed counts, as the expected values from SEER Program also account for multiple primary cancers within an individual, to ensure comparability between the observed and projected numbers of subsequent NKSCs. 12 Statistical analysis Primary outcomes were presented in terms of standardised incidence ratio (SIR), excess risk (per 10 000 person-years) and their corresponding 95% CIs for subsequent NKSCs among primary cancer survivors. The SIR was obtained by dividing the observed number of subsequent NKSC cases by the expected number. Statistical significance was determined using an exact Poisson test, and 95% CIs were computed based on the Poisson distribution. 13 14 The expected number of subsequent NKSCs was determined by multiplying age, sex, race and calendar year-specific incidence rates from the 17 SEER registries by the person-years for each stratum. 15 The excess risk per 10 000 person-years was calculated as ((observed count−expected count)/person-years)×10 000, which indicates the additional expected number of cancers per 10 000 person-years. 16 The proportional contribution of each first primary cancers and subsequent NKSC pair to the overall number of subsequent NKSC cases was computed across all survivors. To investigate whether treatment modalities modify the associations between first primary cancers and subsequent NKSCs, we examined the risk of developing subsequent NKSCs following radiotherapy or chemotherapy. We also conducted subgroup analyses stratified by age at the time of first primary cancer diagnosis, sex, race, year of diagnosis and SEER summary stage. To evaluate detection bias resulting from more intensive screening of cancer patients during initial medical workup, we analysed the risk of developing subsequent NKSCs across several time intervals following the first cancer diagnosis. 17 All analyses were performed using the ‘MP-SIR’ session of SEER*Stat V.8.4.4 and R software V.4.4.1. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a significance level set at p 0.05, and no multiple comparison corrections were applied, as the analyses were exploratory in nature. 7 Data analysis was conducted from September to November 2024. Patient and public involvement Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research. Result Among 5 691 336 cancer survivors, 46.1% were aged 65 years or older when they were first diagnosed with cancer, 51.3% were male and 81.4% were white. Cases at the localised stage comprised 42.1% of the cohort; 32.9% and 31.9% of cancer survivors received chemotherapy and radiotherapy, respectively. During a total follow-up period of 36 440 569 person-years (mean, 6.4 years), a total of 31 529 survivors with subsequent NKSCs were observed. Overall, cancer survivors exhibited a 12% increased risk of developing NKSCs compared with the expected risks in the general population (SIR, 1.12 (95% CI 1.10 to 1.13)), which corresponds to an excess of 0.91 cases per 10 000 person-years. This elevated risk was consistently observed across various subgroups ( table 1 ). Most survivor groups categorised by the type of first primary cancer demonstrated risks of developing NKSCs that were higher than expected when compared with the general population. Specifically, 19 primary cancer types exhibited a statistically significant increase in the incidence of subsequent NKSCs ( table 2 , online supplemental Table S3 ). The highest SIR for subsequent NKSCs was observed in patients with eye and orbit cancer (SIR, 2.96 (95% CI 2.55 to 3.41); excess risk, 16.95 per 10 000 person-years), followed by melanoma of the skin (SIR, 2.67 (95% CI 2.41 to 2.94); excess risk, 1.15 per 10 000 person-years) and chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (SIR, 2.24 (95% CI 2.10 to 2.38); excess risk, 13.19 per 10 000 person-years). Among the three most common primary cancers, the risk of NKSCs statistically significantly increased in prostate (SIR, 1.06 (95% CI 1.05 to 1.08); excess risk, 0.85 per 10 000 person-years) and breast cancer survivors (SIR, 1.20 (95% CI 1.16 to 1.23); excess risk, 0.93 per 10 000 person-years), while it was statistically significantly lower in lung and bronchus cancer survivors (SIR, 0.84 (95% CI 0.79 to 0.90); excess risk, −1.29 per 10 000 person-years) ( table 2 , online supplemental Table S3 ). To rigorously exclude synchronous cancers and assess long-term skin cancer risk among survivors, we conducted subgroup analyses based on latency intervals. Notably, as depicted in figure 1A , 10 bolded cells highlight elevated risks across all latency groups. Specifically, survivors with colorectal cancer (CRC) demonstrated an elevated risk of developing sebaceous adenocarcinoma. Survivors with melanoma, non-Hodgkin's lymphoma or chronic lymphocytic leukaemia exhibited a higher risk of subsequent Merkel cell carcinoma. Survivors with breast, prostate, eye and orbit, thyroid cancers, along with non-Hodgkin's lymphoma and chronic lymphocytic leukaemia, were identified as having a notably elevated risk of developing subsequent cutaneous melanoma ( table 4 ). Discussion Utilising the latest US population-based cancer incidence data, we identified that the cohort of 5 691 336 cancer survivors exhibited an increased overall risk of developing subsequent NKSCs in comparison to the general population. This elevation in risk was particularly significant for non-melanoma NKSCs, demonstrating a statistically significant increase of 43%. Furthermore, we observed that cancer survivors who received radiotherapy and chemotherapy exhibited a significantly increased risk of developing secondary angiosarcoma. To the best of our knowledge, this research represents the most comprehensive investigation into subsequent NKSCs across various first primary cancer types, thereby extending the findings of previous SEER analyses. 4 17 Among survivors of 35 types of first primary cancers, 19 demonstrated an increased risk of developing NKSCs. Notably, 14 types exhibited persistently elevated incidences of NKSCs 5 or more years after diagnosis. The most significant increases in risk were observed in survivors of orbital cancer (196%), melanoma (167%) and chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (124%). In terms of site-specific cancer factors, survivors of orbital cancer and melanoma frequently have a documented history of ultraviolet exposure, 18 19 which is also recognised as a risk factor for other skin cancers, 20 21 suggesting that ultraviolet radiation may serve as a common risk factor among these malignancies. However, due to limitations in the available data, we were unable to quantify the specific contribution of ultraviolet exposure to the incidence of secondary NKSCs in these patients. Nonetheless, given that ultraviolet exposure is the most preventable risk factor for all skin cancers, it is imperative to incorporate ultraviolet protection strategies into survivorship care plans. 22 23 Consistent with prior findings, we observed a significantly higher risk of secondary skin cancers among chronic lymphocytic leukaemia survivors. However, previous studies have primarily focused on keratinocyte skin cancers or involved smaller cohorts 24 25 or restricted their scope to melanoma incidence. 26 27 Our findings highlight the increased risk of 19 NKSC subtypes, including melanoma, among chronic lymphocytic leukaemia patients, thereby emphasising the urgent need for comprehensive surveillance of NKSCs throughout the course of the disease. Similar to the previous research, 28 the most prevalent first primary cancers in our cohort were prostate cancer (17.3%), breast cancer (17.2%) and lung cancer (10.6%), reflecting the high incidence of these cancers. Our analysis revealed that survivors of prostate and breast cancer exhibited a heightened susceptibility to developing NKSCs. In contrast, lung cancer survivors did not exhibit an increased SIR, possibly due to the high mortality rate of primary lung cancer, 29 which acts as a competing event that obscures subsequent NKSC occurrences. Additionally, reduced physical activity among lung cancer survivors may lead to lower radiation exposure, 30 potentially decreasing the NKSC risk. Among the various types of subsequent NKSCs, melanoma (n=27 784) and Merkel cell carcinoma (n=1454) were the most prevalent, in line with findings from previous studies focusing on specific first primary cancers. 8 Notably, sebaceous gland carcinoma (n=663) emerged as the third most common secondary NKSCs. Consistent with the previous findings, 31 we observed that CRC survivors face a higher subsequent risk of developing sebaceous carcinoma compared with the general population and vice versa. The elevated ‘early-to-late’ risk of sebaceous carcinoma among CRC survivors likely reflects underlying deficiencies in DNA mismatch repair (MMR). Sebaceous carcinoma is a recognised cutaneous manifestation of Lynch syndrome, specifically its Muir-Torre variant, which arises from germline defects in components of the MMR pathway, including MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2 and EPCAM. 32 33 In line with this, a population-based study in England reported that 19% of patients with sebaceous carcinoma had at least one Lynch/MTS-associated visceral malignancy, and approximately one-third of tested sebaceous carcinomas exhibited dMMR by immunohistochemistry. 34 These findings provide strong biological and clinical rationale for MMR IHC/MSI screening and genetic counselling in CRC survivors. We investigated the impact of various treatment modalities on the subsequent development of NKSCs among first primary cancer survivors. The risks of different NKSC subtypes varied markedly across treatment groups. Both radiotherapy and chemotherapy are known to exert toxic effects on the skin, 35 36 potentially elevating the risk of subsequent skin cancers. Our analysis demonstrated that the risk of haemangiosarcoma was significantly elevated in both the radiotherapy and combined treatment groups. Hemangiosarcoma has previously been associated with radiotherapy for breast cancer, 8 37 and our findings further substantiate and extend this association. In the chemotherapy-only group, the risk of sebaceous carcinoma showed the most pronounced increase. In addition to the intrinsic skin toxicity associated with chemotherapy, the immunosuppressive effects of this treatment may also play a significant role, as sebaceous neoplasms have been observed in immunocompromised populations. 38 40 Overall, radiotherapy and combination therapies emerge as the primary contributors to the development of NKSCs. These findings underscore the urgent necessity for personalised surveillance and intervention strategies for cancer survivors, guided by their specific treatment histories. There are several limitations in our study. First, the data were derived from the SEER database, which may not comprehensively reflect the diversity of broader populations or encompass the full spectrum of cancer types. For instance, the SEER Program does not capture data on squamous cell carcinoma and basal cell carcinoma, which are the most common forms of skin cancer. Second, the study encompasses an extended timeframe, during which advancements in treatment modalities and screening technologies may potentially impact the relevance and applicability of the findings. Third, it is noteworthy that the potential misclassification of cancer diagnoses within the SEER database may compromise the accuracy of risk estimates. Fourth, important factors, including socioeconomic status, environmental exposures and genetic predispositions, were not thoroughly considered in the analysis. Fifth, the underlying causes of these trends remain unclear; however, they may be attributable to a combination of genetic predisposition, environmental risk factors and evolving treatment paradigms. Finally, the chance of multiple tests may result in some observed statistically significant associations. In summary, several types of primary cancers were strongly linked to an increased risk of developing subsequent NKSCs, compared with the general population. These findings highlight the importance of recognising the elevated risk of subsequent NKSCs among cancer survivors, underscoring the need for heightened clinical vigilance and focused skin examinations in this population. Notably, the screening strategies for NKSCs, excluding melanoma, remain to be further refined in order to effectively reduce the disease risk in these populations.
