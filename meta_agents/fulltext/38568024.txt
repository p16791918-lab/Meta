Background Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, systemic autoimmune disease characterized by persistent inflammation that leads to progressive joint destruction. The disease’s impact, however, extends far beyond the joints. Among these, osteoporosis is one of the most prevalent and debilitating consequences, arising directly from the systemic inflammatory load imposed by the underlying disease [ 1 ]. This positions RA-associated comorbidities not as separate conditions but as downstream effects of a shared pathophysiology, underscoring the need for biomarkers that can accurately quantify this systemic burden. Patients with RA face a substantially elevated risk of developing osteoporosis and sustaining fragility fractures, which lead to chronic pain, disability, and increased mortality rates [ 2 ].The cause of this bone loss is multifactorial, stemming from a combination of traditional risk factors like age and disease-specific drivers such as chronic inflammation, reduced mobility, and the use of glucocorticoids [ 3 , 4 ]. Accurately assessing this inflammatory activity is crucial for managing RA, but conventional markers like the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) have significant limitations [ 5 ]. Furthermore, they are non-specific and can be influenced by factors like age often correlating poorly with the specific immunological disturbances driving bone loss [ 6 ]. This highlights the need for more novel biomarkers that can better reflect the inflammatory state associated with skeletal fragility in RA [ 7 ]. In response, a new paradigm has emerged focusing on composite inflammatory indices derived from routine and inexpensive blood tests. These markers, such as the platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), offer a more holistic measure of the systemic inflammatory-immune status by reflecting the balance between different arms of the immune system [ 8 ]. More advanced indices integrate these cellular ratios with acute-phase reactants to create potentially more powerful markers. Examples include the CRP-to-lymphocyte ratio (CLR), which has been linked to lower bone scores [ 9 ]; and the Inflammation Burden Index (IBI), designed to quantify the total “inflammatory load” [ 10 ]. While these novel composite indices show considerable promise, their clinical utility for identifying RA patients at high risk for osteoporosis remains largely unexplored. Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to comprehensively investigate the association between a panel of novel inflammatory markers—including the CLR, ESR-to-lymphocyte ratio (ELR) [ 11 ], IBI, white blood cell-to-lymphocyte ratio (WLR) [ 12 ], and monocyte-to-lymphocyte-to-platelet ratio (MLPR) [ 13 ]— and bone mineral density (BMD), the prevalence of osteoporosis, and the history of fragility fractures in a well-characterized cohort of patients with RA. We also sought to determine if these novel indices provide superior predictive information compared to conventional inflammatory markers and other established clinical risk factors. Methods Study design and population This retrospective, cross-sectional study was conducted on patients diagnosed with RA at the Third Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University and Eighth Clinical Medical College of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine between January 2022 and August 2025. A total of 439 patients who met the 2010 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism (ACR/EULAR) classification criteria for RA were included in the final analysis. Patients were excluded if they had other metabolic bone diseases (e.g., Paget’s disease, hyperparathyroidism), malignancies with known bone involvement, or incomplete clinical or laboratory data. All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional and with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments. Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. Comprehensive data were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records. This included demographics; disease-related variables such as the Disease Activity Score in 28 joints (DAS28) and pain Visual Analog Scale (VAS) scores; and a detailed medication history, including the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), corticosteroids, conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs), biological DMARDs (bDMARDs), and targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs). Fragility fractures were recorded, defined as fractures resulting from low-energy trauma, such as a fall from standing height or less, reflecting reduced bone strength and increased skeletal fragility [ 14 ]. Fracture history was ascertained based on a combination of patient self-report and available imaging records (including radiographs or computed tomography reports documented in the medical records). An extensive panel of laboratory parameters was extracted for each patient. Complete blood counts provided the white blood cell count (WBC, ×10⁹/L), lymphocyte count (LYM, ×10⁹/L), monocyte count (MONO, ×10⁹/L), hemoglobin (Hb, g/L), and platelet count (PLT, ×10⁹/L). Key inflammatory markers included CRP (mg/L) and ESR (mm/h). Biochemical assessments comprised liver function tests, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST, U/L) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT, U/L); renal function tests, including blood urea nitrogen (BUN, mmol/L) and serum creatinine (Scr, µmol/L); and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH, U/L). The immunological profile analyzed included autoantibodies such as rheumatoid factor (RF, IU/mL), anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide antibody (ACPA, IU/mL), and antinuclear antibody (ANA), performed using indirect immunofluorescence (IIF), with results reported as a titer; complement components C3 (g/L) and C4 (g/L); and immunoglobulin levels for immunoglobulin G (IgG, g/L), immunoglobulin M (IgM, g/L), and immunoglobulin A (IgA, g/L). Levels of 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D, ng/mL) were also recorded. From these primary laboratory values, a series of novel composite inflammatory indices were calculated. These included the lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), WLR, MLPR, CLR, ELR, IBI, and the lymphocyte-to-platelet ratio (LPR). BMD was measured at the lumbar spine (L1–L4), femoral neck, and total hip using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA; HOLOGIC Horizon A). According to World Health Organization (WHO) criteria, patients were classified into normal BMD, osteopenia, or osteoporosis groups. In our study, we primarily used T-scores at the above locations for these classifications. A T-score ≥ − 1.0 was considered normal, a T-score between − 1.0 and − 2.5 indicated osteopenia, and a T-score ≤ − 2.5 was defined as osteoporosis [ 15 ]. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR). Comparisons were performed using t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, Kruskal-Wallis H tests as appropriate. The Jonckheere–Terpstra test was used to assess for linear trends across the ordered bone density categories, generating a P for trend. Correlation analysis between continuous variables was conducted using Pearson’s or Spearman’s tests depending on data distribution. Finally, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent risk factors, with results reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). To evaluate the diagnostic performance of conventional and novel inflammatory markers in predicting osteoporosis and fragility fractures among patients with RA, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were conducted. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify each marker’s discriminative ability. Sensitivity and specificity were determined at optimal cut-off points using the Youden index. Analyses were performed separately for osteoporosis and fracture outcomes. A two-sided P value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software. All ROC analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.0) with the pROC package. Results Clinical characteristics of the study population A total of 439 patients with RA were enrolled, of whom 243 (55.4%) were diagnosed with osteoporosis. As shown in Table 1 and Supplementary Tables S1–S2, the osteoporotic cohort presented a distinct clinical phenotype characterized by significantly greater age, longer disease duration, and a prolonged interval between symptom onset and diagnosis ( P 0.05). Clinically, these patients reported higher pain VAS scores and exhibited laboratory findings indicative of heightened systemic inflammation and metabolic disturbance, including elevated CRP, WBC counts, ALT, SCr, and LDH, alongside lower Hb levels. Notably, the two groups did not differ significantly in sex distribution, smoking status, DAS28, RF or ACPA positivity. Comparison of inflammatory indicators across bone density categories To investigate the relationship between inflammation and bone health, we stratified patients by BMD status (normal, osteopenia, osteoporosis). Inflammatory and biochemical profiles differed significantly across bone density groups (Table 2 , Supplementary Table S3). Conventional markers, including ESR and CRP, demonstrated a stepwise increase from normal BMD to osteoporosis ( P for trend = 0.012 and 0.001, respectively). This inflammatory gradient was even more pronounced for several novel composite indices. The CLR, ELR, and IBI and all increased robustly and progressively with worsening bone density (all P for trend 0.001). Other cellular-based ratios, including the WLR and MLPR, also followed this significant upward trend ( P for trend 0.05). Conversely, Hb levels exhibited a significant inverse trend, decreasing systematically with worsening bone density ( P for trend = 0.002). Notably, this inflammatory signature appeared to be specific rather than a result of generalized immune activation. As detailed in Supplementary Table S3, there were no significant differences across the BMD categories for absolute lymphocyte or monocyte counts, immunoglobulin levels (IgG, IgM, IgA), or complement components. Multivariate analysis of factors associated with osteoporosis To identify the most critical risk factors for osteoporosis, we conducted a multivariate logistic regression analysis. After adjusting for potential confounders, the model revealed three robust, independent predictors of osteoporosis in patients with RA (Table 4 ). The risk increased significantly with older age (OR = 1.071 per year, 95% CI: 1.047–1.094; P 0.001) and a longer interval between symptom onset and diagnosis (OR = 1.075 per year, 95% CI: 1.017–1.136; P = 0.010). Conversely, higher Hb levels were found to be protective, with each g/L increase associated with a 1.4% reduction in the odds of having osteoporosis (OR = 0.986, 95% CI: 0.974–0.999; P = 0.032). In the adjusted model, there was a non-significant negative association between corticosteroid use and osteoporosis. Overall, these findings highlight that a combination of demographic factors, disease chronicity, and systemic health status, as reflected by anemia, are key determinants of skeletal fragility in this population. Our findings demonstrate that these easily accessible and inexpensive indices, particularly those integrating CRP with cellular components, may provide an exploratory assessment of skeletal risk in combination with conventional clinical factors. The prevalence of osteoporosis in our RA cohort was 55.4%, which is higher than that reported in most contemporary studies, where estimates typically range from 30% to 50% [ 16 ]. Several factors may underlie this observation, including demographic, genetic, and disease characteristics [36689130]. Asian populations, including Chinese patients, are known to have lower baseline bone mass and different genetic determinants of bone metabolism compared with Western cohorts [ 17 ], which may predispose them to greater bone loss under chronic inflammation. Moreover, environmental and healthcare-related factors, such as insufficient dietary calcium intake [ 18 ], delayed diagnosis, long disease duration [ 19 ], cumulative inflammatory burden [ 20 ] and suboptimal adherence to glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis prevention [ 4 ] and treatment guidelines, may further exacerbate skeletal vulnerability. A key finding from our multivariate analysis was that a longer interval between symptom onset and diagnosis emerged as a robust, independent predictor of osteoporosis. This underscores the critical importance of early diagnosis and intervention in RA, suggesting that uncontrolled inflammation in the initial stages of the disease may trigger irreversible damage on the skeletal system, predisposing patients to later osteoporotic complications. A central finding of our investigation was the clear, stepwise increase in both conventional and novel inflammatory markers corresponding to worsening bone density categories, from normal to osteopenia and finally to osteoporosis. While ESR and CRP showed a significant trend, the gradient was even more pronounced for composite indices such as the CLR, ELR and IBI. This suggests that these composite markers may be more sensitive in detecting the subtle, yet persistent, systemic inflammation that drives bone loss in RA. Similar patterns have been reported in other populations, where composite or platelet/lymphocyte–based indices showed stronger associations with lower BMD and increased fracture risk compared with single inflammatory biomarkers [ 21 ]. The pathophysiology of this connection is well-understood through the lens of osteoimmunology, where pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1, and IL-6 disrupt bone homeostasis by promoting osteoclast-mediated bone resorption and suppressing osteoblast-driven bone formation [ 22 ]. Our results support the concept that composite inflammatory indices, which combine different aspects of immune and inflammatory activity, provide a more complete picture of the chronic inflammation that contributes to bone loss in RA. Notably, our correlation analysis demonstrated that CRP levels were not significantly associated with BMD at any skeletal site. This finding underscores the growing recognition that CRP, although widely used as a biomarker of systemic inflammation, may not fully reflect the multifaceted inflammatory processes driving bone loss in RA. Previous studies have shown that a considerable subset of patients with clinically active RA exhibit normal CRP values despite evident disease activity [ 23 ]. In one report, more than 40% of patients with RA had CRP levels within the normal range, highlighting the existence of a “normal-CRP” phenotype in which inflammation persists at the synovial or tissue level despite biochemical quiescence [ 24 ]. These observations collectively suggest that CRP alone may be insufficient to capture the full inflammatory burden contributing to skeletal fragility in RA, emphasizing the potential value of integrating novel composite inflammatory indices for a more comprehensive assessment. In contrast, several composite markers showed significant inverse correlations with BMD. The most consistent association was found for ELR, which correlated negatively with both femoral neck and hip BMD, suggesting that the ELR may reflect a specific immune dysregulation relevant to skeletal health. Other indices, including the WLR, CLR, and IBI, were significantly associated with lumbar spine BMD, further supporting their utility in reflecting the systemic inflammatory impact on bone. The most clinically relevant finding of our study was the association between certain composite inflammatory markers and a history of fragility fractures. Patients with prior fractures exhibited significantly higher median levels of CLR and IBI, linking these indices not only to low BMD but also to the clinical outcome of fractures. Notably, all three indices incorporate CRP, supporting the hypothesis that combining an acute-phase reactant with immune or hematologic parameters may better reflect the cumulative inflammatory burden relevant to bone fragility. Previous studies have similarly reported that composite inflammation indices—such as CRP- and platelet-based markers including PLR, NLR, and the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII)—are more closely correlated with disease activity or fracture risk than single biomarkers alone [ 25 – 27 ]. In RA, persistent systemic inflammation is known to exert direct and indirect effects on bone metabolism, with long-term cohort data showing that inflammation-related factors contribute independently to fracture risk beyond BMD [ 20 ]. In contrast, leukocyte ratios without CRP involvement (e.g., LMR, WLR, MLPR) were not associated with fracture history in our cohort, suggesting lower specificity for the degree of inflammation that drives clinically meaningful skeletal damage. In multivariable analyses, older age, a longer interval to diagnosis, and lower Hb emerged as independently associated with osteoporosis. The association with low Hb aligns with data linking anemia and declining Hb to lower BMD and higher fracture risk, and with mechanistic work implicating inflammation-driven hepcidin dysregulation in anemia of chronic disease in RA [ 28 ]. It is noteworthy that the novel inflammatory markers did not remain independently after multivariable adjustment in this model. This does not necessarily diminish their importance but may suggest they are part of the causal pathway through which inflammation exerts its effects (e.g., inflammation leads to anemia, which is associated with osteoporosis) or that their primary utility lies in identifying risk for fracture rather than the densitometric state of osteoporosis itself. This study has several strengths, including a large and well-defined cohort of RA patients and the comprehensive analysis of a broad panel of novel, readily available biomarkers against both BMD and fracture outcomes. However, some limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design of the study precludes the establishment of causality or predictive value; we can only demonstrate association, not prove that elevated inflammatory markers directly cause bone loss over time. Furthermore, fracture history was based on patient records and self-report, which may be subject to recall bias. Systematic radiographic screening for vertebral fractures was not performed in all patients; consequently, asymptomatic or previously undiagnosed vertebral fractures may not have been captured, particularly among patients without a diagnosis of osteoporosis. Finally, while we adjusted for numerous confounders, the potential for residual confounding from unmeasured variables such as physical activity levels, dietary intake, BMI, and cumulative treatment exposures, cannot be excluded. Future prospective cohort studies with standardized fracture assessment and longitudinal inflammatory measurements are therefore needed to validate these associations and clarify their clinical relevance. Conclusions Our findings suggest that novel composite inflammatory markers, particularly the CLR and IBI, are significantly associated with both osteoporosis and, more importantly, fragility fractures in patients with RA. These markers, which are derived from simple and inexpensive blood tests, appear to be more sensitive indicators of the systemic inflammation driving skeletal fragility than CRP alone. They hold promise as valuable clinical tools to help identify RA patients at high risk for fracture, thereby allowing for earlier and more targeted interventions to preserve bone health. Future prospective studies are warranted to confirm whether these markers can predict incident fractures and to determine if targeting a reduction in these indices can improve skeletal outcomes in this vulnerable population.
