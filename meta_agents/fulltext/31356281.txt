Background Breast cancer is the most common cancer among females in the U.S., with 316,950 estimated new cases in 2025. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is an aggressive subtype of breast cancer characterized by the absence of estrogen receptors (ER), progesterone receptors (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) [ 1 ]. In 2024, the incidence rate of overall breast cancer was 131.3 per 100,000 person-years in African American females and 137.9 per 100,000 person-years in European American females; the incidence rate of TNBC was 38 per 100,000 in African American females, approximately twice as high as that observed in European American females (19 per 100,000) [ 2 ]. Globally, there are higher TNBC proportions among breast cancer patients in Sub-Saharan African countries than other countries across the world [ 3 ]. Stratified by race/ethnic group, breast cancer patients of African ancestry showed a higher TNBC proportion than other groups living in Europe [ 4 , 5 ], South Africa and the U.S [ 3 ]. It is possible that genetic factors contribute to the elevated risk of TNBC in African-ancestry females. However, most of these previous studies relied on self-reported race and therefore treated African Americans as a single homogenous population to compare their risk of TNBC with other populations. In fact, African Americans represent a genetically admixed population with substantial inter-individual variation in African ancestry proportions. This variation can be estimated using genotype-derived ancestry, which provides a more precise measure of genetic ancestry than self-reported race/ethnicity. A previous study explored the African genetic ancestry estimates and reported a potential correlation between African ancestry and TNBC status, but it had a very small sample size, and no adjustment for potential confounding variables was made [ 6 ]. In this study, we investigated associations of genotype-derived African ancestry proportion (%AFR) with TNBC risk within African Americans, using data of 20,000 participants from the African-ancestry Breast Cancer Genetics (AABCG) Consortium [ 7 ]. We further performed ancestry-aware association analyses of known risk loci for TNBC to identify African-specific genetic risk variants. Methods Study population AABCG is a consortium established for breast cancer genetic research in females of African ancestry, which includes more than 20 studies conducted in the U.S. and the Africa [ 7 ]. In the current study, we used data for African American female participants from 11 participating studies that included 20 TNBC patients and were conducted in the U.S. In total, we included breast cancer patients diagnosed with TNBC (n = 2,335) or ER-positive breast cancer (n = 8,159) and 9,814 controls from the AABCG (Additional file 1: Table S1). The AABCG was approved by the Vanderbilt University Medical Center institutional review board. Briefly, the genetic data consisted of three main components: whole-genome sequencing data; newly generated genotyping data using the Multi-Ethnic Genotyping Array (MEGA); and genotyping data from existing studies or consortia (Additional file 1: Table S1). Genotype data were imputed using the Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine (TOPMed) as the reference panel, and samples genotyped by the same array were imputed together. Variants with an imputation quality score (r 2 ) greater than 0.3 were kept for analyses. Detailed descriptions of participating studies and genotyping quality control procedures have been reported previously [ 7 ] and are included in the Additional file 2. Global ancestry estimation To minimize the potential effect of the difference in genotype arrays, variants were selected for global ancestry estimation if they: 1) had an imputation quality score (r 2 ) greater than 0.8; 2) had a minor allele frequency (MAF) 0.01; and 3) were shared across all genotype imputation datasets and the 1000 Genomes Project. These variants were pruned using PLINK 1.9 (window size of 50 variant counts, window shift of five variants, r2 threshold of 0.2). Then two-way %AFR was estimated by ADMIXTURE (v1.3.0) [ 8 ] using African- and European-ancestry samples from the 1000 Genome Project as the reference. Participants with %AFR of 25% were excluded (4 TNBC, 7 ER-positive cases, and 12 controls). The bottom 5% of all participants had %AFR ranging from 25.4% to 54.8%, which led to a long-tailed distribution. To mitigate the impact of outliers, %AFR of the bottom 5% participants were set to 54.8% (value at the 5th percentile). Polygenic risk score (PRS) for TNBC We constructed a PRS for TNBC as a measurement of genetic contributors by known risk loci. Based on our previous genome-wide association study (GWAS) for TNBC among females of African ancestry [ 7 ], four variants at P 5 × 10 –8 were selected. We additionally included nine risk variants for TNBC which were previously reported for European-ancestry populations and showed an association with TNBC risk at P 0.05 in African-ancestry GWAS. In total, 13 variants at 12 loci were selected for PRS construction (Additional file 1: Table S2). Statistical analyses We compared characteristics of TNBC, ER-positive cases, and controls, including age, BMI, menopausal status, number of live births, breastfeeding, age at first live birth, age at menarche, and PRS for TNBC. Kruskal–Wallis test and Pearson χ2 test were used for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Global African ancestry was categorized as 55.0%, 55.0–64.9%, 65.0–74.9%, 75.0–79.9%, 80.0–84.9%, 85.0–89.9%, 90.0–94.9%, and 95.0% or higher. The category of 55.0–64.9% was selected as the reference group for stable estimates because some studies had few participants within the category of 55.0%. Logistic regressions were performed for case–control and case-case comparison with outcomes defined as TNBC vs. control and TNBC vs. ER-positive cases, respectively. We estimated odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for each %AFR category (55.0–64.9% as reference) or per 10% increase in %AFR. Among the top five genotype principal components (PC), the first PC was highly correlated with global African ancestry (Pearson correlation 0.99), and the other PCs (PC2, PC3, PC4, and PC5) were not correlated with global African ancestry (Pearson correlation 0.01). Therefore, PC2, PC3, PC4, and PC5 were included as covariates. Three models were defined using different sets of covariates. Model 1 included age, PC2, PC3, PC4, and PC5. Model 2 additionally included body mass index (BMI), menopausal status, number of live births, breastfeeding, age at first live birth, and age at menarche. Model 3 additionally included PRS for TNBC. Missing values for reproductive factors were treated as a separate category in the analyses. Analyses were performed within each study, and results were then combined by fixed-effects inverse-variance-weighted meta-analysis. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed by the Cochran’s Q statistic and I 2 . Local ancestry analyses To identify African-specific risk variant that may contribute to the association between African ancestry and TNBC risk, we conducted ancestry-aware association analyses. Due to a limited sample size, we investigated only known TNBC risk loci instead of the whole genome. As mentioned above, samples genotyped by the same array were imputed together as one dataset. Each genotype imputation dataset was first phased using Beagle [ 9 ] and then inferred for local ancestry using FLARE [ 10 ]. The reference dataset was the 1000 Genomes Project samples of African and European ancestry, keeping biallelic variants with MAF 0.005. HapMap GRCh38 genetic maps in cM units was used for local ancestry inference. Then, Tractor [ 11 ] was used to perform local ancestry-aware association analyses at the 12 known risk loci for TNBC, adjusted for age, study, and PC2-PC5. The association analyses were performed within each dataset, and results were combined by fixed-effects inverse-variance-weighted meta-analysis. Variants with a MAF 0.01 in the African-ancestry haplotypes were excluded from the association analyses. Local ancestry-aware association analyses were performed at 12 known TNBC risk loci identified among African-ancestry females rather than across the whole genome due to the limited sample size (Additional file 1: Table S2). Results of the African- and European-ancestry haplotypes were listed in the Fig. 2 and Additional file 1: Table S3, respectively. In case–control analyses, we identified risk variants on African-ancestry haplotypes at 5p15.33 (rs10069690, P = 2.26 × 10 –7 ) and 19p13.11 (rs8100241, P = 1.08 × 10 –11 ). These two risk association signals were also reported in our previous GWAS for TNBC [ 7 ]. The lead variant rs8100241 is a missense variant of ANKLE1 , and it was predicted as deleterious and probably damaging by SIFT and PolyPhen [ 12 ]. The lead variant rs10069690 at TERT locus has been reported as a high-confidence causal variant, with a posterior inclusion probability 0.99 to be the causal variant as reported in our recent multi-ancestry fine-mapping study [ 13 ]. Both variants had a higher risk allele frequency in African-ancestry haplotypes than that in European ancestry haplotypes, and they also showed significant associations in case-case analyses (Fig. 2 ). In case-case analyses, we identified additional five variants on African-ancestry haplotypes ( P 1 × 10 –4 ), including rs74806606 at 1q32.1, rs76664032 at 2q14.2, rs58582758 at 5p15.33, rs74821333 at 10q25.2, and rs79917372 at 11q22.3. At 5p15.33, rs58582758 was independent from rs10069690 (linkage disequilibrium [LD] r 2 = 0 in African-ancestry populations). Particularly, large ORs ranging from 1.66 to 1.79 were observed in case-case analyses for rs74806606, rs58582758, and rs74821333. The risk allele frequency of these three variants was 0.02 in African-ancestry haplotypes but 0.001 in European-ancestry ones, which indicated these three variants as African-specific risk variants. For rs76664032 and rs79917372, however, their major alleles showed higher odds for TNBC than ER-positive breast cancer. Effect allele/other allele; b. odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) estimated in African-ancestry haplotypes; EAF: Effect allele frequency Discussion In this study, we investigated the associations of African genetic ancestry with risk of TNBC among African American females. A higher proportion of African genetic global ancestry was not associated with risk of TNBC compared with controls, but it was associated with higher odds of being diagnosed with TNBC compared with ER-positive breast cancer. This association remained after adjustment for known risk variants for TNBC. We also identified seven subtype-informative variants, including three suggestive African-specific risk variants. Our study suggests a significant role of African-ancestry genetic factors in breast tumor subtype determination, and it highlights the importance of future research to identify ancestry-associated biological pathways contributing to TNBC susceptibility. We estimated the genetic African ancestry proportion among African American females and evaluated its association with risk of TNBC, rather than treating African Americans as a single ancestrally homogeneous group and comparing them with other populations. By focusing on African American females themselves, where lifestyle and environmental risk factors are more likely to be similar, our study design reduced the potential for confounding by non-genetic factors. We also adjusted for five reproductive risk factors for breast cancer to reduce possible confounding effects of these variables. This study included 20,308 African American females, including 2,335 TNBC cases from AABCG, the largest study ever conducted to address this question. Our case–control analyses revealed no association between African genetic ancestry and risk of TNBC after adjustment for BMI and reproductive factors. These findings suggest that non-genetic factors may play a more prominent role than genetic ancestry in contributing to the higher incidence of TNBC among African American women compared to White women in the U.S. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution. We were unable to adjust for additional lifestyle-related covariates, such as physical activity, alcohol consumption, and smoking, because these data were unavailable. Although such variables are typically not treated as confounders in single-variant association analyses within the conventional GWAS framework, they may act as confounders in this context due to their potential correlations with %AFR. Future studies of TNBC that incorporate more comprehensive covariate data will be needed to clarify the relationship between genetic ancestry and breast cancer subtype risk. Our case-case analyses revealed that females with a higher %AFR were more likely to be diagnosed with TNBC compared with ER-positive breast cancer. The attenuation of this association after adjusting for the TNBC PRS suggested that variants at known TNBC risk loci may partially contribute to this association. Notably, although the association was attenuated, it remained statistically significant after PRS adjustment, suggesting the presence of additional African-specific genetic risk variants outside of known TNBC risk loci. Individuals with higher African ancestry may be more genetically predisposed to developing TNBC once tumorigenesis is initiated, instead of having a higher overall likelihood of developing breast cancer. In breast tumors from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), ancestry-associated difference has been reported in activation of regulatory pathways like ATM , SP1 , and MAPK14 with adjustment for tumor subtype [ 14 ]. Future studies are needed to investigate African ancestry-specific biological pathways related to tumor subtype determination. Our local ancestry-aware association analyses identified potential African ancestry-specific subtype-informative variants at known TNBC risk loci, including MDM4 and TCF7L2 . MDM4 encodes a key negative regulator of the tumor suppressor protein p53 [ 15 ]. TCF7L2 encodes a transcription factor that functions primarily within the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, which is involved in cancer cell proliferation and invasion [ 16 ]. TNBC exhibits a TP53 mutation frequency of 95% among African-ancestry females [ 17 ], substantially higher than that observed in ER-positive breast tumors, and this has been considered a defining molecular feature of TNBC [ 18 , 19 ]. Activation of Wnt signaling pathway is enriched in TNBC [ 20 , 21 ] and is associated with metastasis [ 22 ]. Together, dysregulation of the TP53 and Wnt pathways represents characteristic molecular features of TNBC. The African ancestry–specific subtype-informative variants identified at these loci may partially explain the association between %AFR and TNBC. However, variants identified in local ancestry-aware association analyses are not necessarily causal. Additional studies are required to identify the causal variants and their target genes. Our study has several limitations. First, TNBC is a biologically heterogeneous disease comprising multiple molecular subtypes. However, TNBC subtype-specific analyses were not performed in this study due to limited data availability and insufficient sample size. Future studies are warranted to evaluate associations between African ancestry-specific risk variants and distinct TNBC molecular subtypes. Second, although this study was well-powered for analyses of global ancestry proportion, the sample size was still relatively small for local ancestry-aware genome-wide association analyses. Consequently, our analyses were confined to previously reported TNBC susceptibility loci rather than the entire genome. Future studies with a larger sample size are needed to investigate the whole genome to identify African ancestry-specific risk variants. Conclusion Our study suggests that African global genetic ancestry may be associated with a higher relative likelihood of TNBC compared with ER-positive breast cancer, but not with an increased absolute risk of TNBC among African American women. We identified suggestive African-specific risk variants that may contribute to this ancestry–subtype association. This study advances our understanding of the biological mechanisms of the difference in TNBC incidence across populations, providing a foundation for future genetic and functional studies to elucidate mechanisms underlying TNBC susceptibility.
