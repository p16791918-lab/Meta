Introduction With over 2 million new cases annually, breast cancer is the most common cancer among women and a growing public health concern worldwide, particularly in Asia. 1 Despite the urgent need for tailored prevention and treatment strategies for Asian patients, most of the published research have focused on Western populations, leaving significant knowledge gaps about Asian women, whose distinct genes, lifestyles, and cultures demand further study. 2 Singapore’s diverse population, comprising 74.3% Chinese, 13.5% Malay, and 9% Indian, each characterized by distinct genetic profiles and health behaviours, offers a unique setting for the study of breast cancer in Asian women. With national health screening initiatives like Breast Screen Singapore, we are witnessing a significant increase in breast cancer incidence since the 1970s. The age-specific incidence rate had nearly quadrupled, from 20.1 per 100 000 population in 1968-1972 to 76.2 per 100 000 in 2018-2022. 3 This sharp increase is marked by ethnic disparities: Chinese women exhibit a higher incidence of breast cancer, while Malay women face poorer survival outcomes, influenced by a combination of patient-related, treatment-related, and socioeconomic factors. 4 These alarming trends highlight the urgent need for timely and comprehensive data collection and analysis. To address this, the Joint Breast Cancer Registry (JBCR) was established at the National Cancer Centre of Singapore (NCCS) and has since expanded to include multiple health care institutions nationwide. By collecting real-world data from routine clinical care from a cross-section of the Singaporean population, JBCR offers a relevant perspective on the country’s breast cancer landscape—insights often lacking in controlled trials that inform evidence-based guidelines. 5 The data on breast cancer epidemiology, treatment patterns, and long-term outcomes enable personalized treatment, identify treatment gaps, and assess the effectiveness of interventions. 6 In this study, we present an overview of the JBCR, detailing its structure, governance, and data-related processes. We also offer a snapshot of the registry’s cohort, along with key trends and findings from research based on its data. This collaborative initiative underscores how JBCR is driving advancements in breast cancer care and research in Singapore and across Asia. Patients and methods Study design and population JBCR began as a retrospective study in 2004 focusing on breast cancer patients treated with breast-conserving therapy at NCCS. Patients aged 21 and above who received treatment from 1980 onwards were included. In 2007, JBCR transitioned into a prospective study, enrolling all breast cancer patients treated at Singapore General Hospital and NCCS. Over time, it expanded to include all four general hospitals within the Singapore Health Services (SingHealth) Cluster, adding KK Women’s and Children’s Hospital in 2011, Changi General Hospital in 2013, and Sengkang General Hospital in 2017. In 2020, Tan Tock Seng Hospital and National University Hospital, which maintain their own hospital-based breast cancer databases covering the central/northern and western regions of Singapore, respectively, joined the JBCR’s network. This national partnership enables JBCR to acquire detailed information on nearly all breast cancer patients treated within Singapore’s public health care system. JBCR operates with a Waiver of Informed Consent approval from the SingHealth Centralised Institutional Review Board (CIRB reference: 2019/2419). Data collection and management The JBCR maintains a comprehensive database that includes patient demographics; disease information such as histopathology reports; radiological and laboratory results; treatment details including surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and targeted therapies; and billing data. Information on breast cancer outcomes, such as recurrence, survival, treatment toxicities, and costs, is regularly updated. These data are gathered from various sources, including electronic medical records (EMRs) and national registries. To streamline data extraction and reduce manual entry, JBCR has been integrated into various clinical processes. For example, we developed a specialized Breast Tumour Board (BTB) software, which allows clinicians to input and review patient, tumour, and treatment details during multidisciplinary meetings, and have the data flowing directly into the registry. In turn, these data are then made available to the EMR, allowing clinicians to generate records of the BTB proceedings or use the information directly in clinical care. This software is currently used in three hospitals. In addition, significant clinical events, such as local disease recurrence or metastasis and occurrence of significant late toxicity, especially secondary cancers, are similarly recorded. While JBCR retains original data, such as histopathology and radiology reports where possible, in most cases, discrete data are extracted into a database for rapid analysis. JBCR utilizes a variety of extract, transform, load tools to automatically process radiology and histopathology reports. Text-mining techniques are also used to facilitate the accurate and efficient identification of significant medical events, especially disease recurrences and toxicity. Mortality data, including causes and dates of death, are obtained from the National Registry of Births and Deaths. A dashboard within the SingHealth enterprise data warehouse (electronic Health INTelligence systems; eHINTs) allows for real-time data retrieval and visualization. Data validation and quality control The registry operates on a zero-assumption basis to maximize information accuracy, meaning that no data point is taken for granted—every piece of information is verified and cross-checked. At the core of this approach is a multi-tiered review system. Critical data points, including patient demographics, tumour characteristics, and treatment details, undergo both automated and manual scrutiny. The JBCR conducts field-based validations regularly to ensure the logical consistency of clinical variables across the dataset. For example, the system checks that chemotherapy is only administered to patients at appropriate cancer stages; radiotherapy is not given to early-stage patients treated with mastectomy; and endocrine therapy is only given to patients with hormone receptor-positive disease. Automated tools are important for scanning the dataset for such anomalies. JBCR conducts regular audits to identify issues such as missing data, inconsistencies, and implausible values. Any issues flagged by the above processes are carefully reviewed by registry staff. Particularly significant clinical events, such as disease recurrence and metastasis, undergo additional manual validation to ensure accurate recording. Our clinical data analysts are extensively trained in oncology registry standards, including programmes such as Principles of Oncology for Cancer Registry Professionals and workshops on ICD-10-AM/ACHI/ACS. JBCR maintains a common data dictionary to ensure a standardized vocabulary across all participating institutions. Data security is a priority for JBCR. Daily backups are made to a network drive, complemented by secondary weekly backups to a separate network drive. Every 6 months, the principal investigator conducts additional backups on to a network-connected personal computer. Access to the registry is secured through routine password authentication, and all backups are encrypted and password-protected. Data governance The JBCR operates under a robust data governance framework to ensure that all data management practices align with ethical standards and regulatory requirements. This includes compliance with Singapore’s Personal Data Protection Act, the Human Biomedical Research Act, and hospital-specific guidelines on research data use. Access to the registry is limited to only those personnel authorized to retrieve or modify data records. The JBCR operates as a confederation model. This enables each participating institution to voluntarily contribute data to the registry while retaining full ownership and control over its information. The terms of data access, sharing, and usage are governed by the Research Collaboration Agreement established between all participating institutions. This fosters open participation from any institution interested in developing and utilizing the database. Researchers interested in using JBCR data follow a structured process: they first propose new analyses within the scope of JBCR, which are then circulated to all JBCR investigators for feedback and collaboration. Upon receiving support, the investigator submits a formal data request to the data access committee for final approval. External collaborators are welcome to participate in JBCR research by contacting the principal investigator, who shares the proposal with JBCR investigators for consideration. To acknowledge the collaborative nature of the registry, publications using JBCR data are expected to use consortium authorship where applicable and adhere to the authorship standards set by the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors. Results As of December 2023, the Joint Breast Cancer Registry included data from 35 157 unique patients, accounting for 60% of the 39 554 incident invasive female breast cancers diagnosed in Singapore from 2003 to 2022 according to the Ministry of Health, Singapore. 7 The median age at diagnosis was 54 years (range 21-103 years), with a median follow-up time of 7.6 years (interquartile range 3.5-14.1 years). Notably, 65.3% ( n = 19 448) of patients had been followed up for at least 5 years, and 39.5% ( n = 11 765) for at least 10 years. Patient characteristics are summarized in Supplementary Table S1 , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2025.100159 . Among the registry population, 8883 deaths were recorded, with 64% attributed to breast cancer. Significant disparities in breast cancer-specific survival (BCSS) and overall survival (OS) were observed across (i) stages at diagnosis, (ii) breast cancer subtypes, (iii) age groups, and (iv) major ethnic groups in Singapore (log-rank P value 0.001) ( Figure 1 and Supplementary Figure S1 , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2025.100159 ). Patients with higher-staged disease, triple-negative basal-like cancer subtypes, older age at diagnosis, or Malay ethnicity have decreased BCSS and OS. (D) Stratified by major ethnic groups in Singapore. HER2, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2; y.o., year old. Significant trends in breast cancer care have been observed over the past 20 years. The registry revealed relatively unchanging proportions of breast cancer stages between 2000 and 2020. The hoped-for increase in proportions of early staged cancers was not realized possibly indicating stagnated participation in breast cancer-screening programmes and no substantial change in breast cancer awareness ( Figure 2 ). This stagnation contrasts with Western countries, where expanded screening programmes have led to stage migration towards earlier diagnoses. The finding suggests that additional public health efforts are needed in Singapore to boost screening uptake and early detection, especially among at-risk groups. Among patients with potentially curative breast cancers, the registry observed a stagnation in the proportion of breast-conserving surgery (BCS) in the last 2 decades, although a clear increase in oncoplastic BCS and breast reconstructive surgery after mastectomy was discernible ( Figure 3 ). The plateau in BCS rates may reflect persistent patient and clinician preferences for mastectomy, cultural attitudes towards breast conservation, or a lag in adopting less-invasive surgical options compared with Western settings. However, significant shifts in treatment paradigms in other treatment modalities were observed. The registry data illustrated a transition in multidrug chemotherapy regimens, from cyclophosphamide, methotrexate, and 5-fluorouracil (CMF) to a rapid adoption of anthracycline-based chemotherapy, such as doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide (AC) and 5-fluorouracil, epirubicin (100 mg/m 2 ), and cyclophosphamide (FEC100). These regimens were in turn superseded by regimens employing sequential anthracyclines and taxanes, such as doxorubicin/cyclophosphamide followed by paclitaxel (AC-T). This progression mirrors global treatment trends following evidence from pivotal clinical trials demonstrating improved survival with anthracycline- and taxane-based regimens. The registry thus captures the timely translation of international evidence into local practice. In recent years, there has been a resurgence of taxane-based chemotherapy without anthracyclines, particularly in patients with low-to-moderate risk disease and those with human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-positive disease in combination with HER2-targeting drugs ( Figure 4 ). This shift away from anthracyclines reflects growing concerns about long-term cardiotoxicity and secondary malignancies, especially in patients with good-prognosis disease where the therapeutic risk–benefit balance must be carefully considered. This period also coincided with a significant increase in the use of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) and hypofractionated radiotherapy ( Figure 5 A and B). NAC was hypothesized to downstage cancers for better surgical outcomes and allows for the use of BCS in patients who would otherwise require mastectomy. Meta-analysis of NAC trials have confirmed that achieving a pathological complete response (pCR) following NAC is predictive of prognosis especially for patients with aggressive cancer subtypes such as triple-negative and HER2-positive cancers. 8 , 9 The increasing uptake of NAC reflects a broader shift towards more aggressive multidisciplinary management strategies, with a growing emphasis not only on oncological control but also on optimizing cosmetic outcomes and long-term quality of life. The shift in radiotherapy regimens followed the rapid adoption of practice-changing trials from the UK and Canada that demonstrated the safety and efficacy of reducing treatment duration from 5-6 weeks to 3-4 weeks. 10 , 11 The early adoption of hypofractionated radiotherapy in Singapore reflects the health system’s agility in translating high-level evidence into clinical practice, prioritizing both treatment efficacy and patient convenience. This readiness to embrace best evidence-based innovations proved particularly valuable during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, when the timely emergence of data supporting ultra-hypofractionation enabled swift implementation, allowing patients to complete treatment within a week while minimizing hospital visits and exposure risks. 12 , 13 Figure 5 Patterns of (neo)adjuvant therapies: (A) Pattern of use of chemotherapy from 2003 to 2022. Findings from JBCR The JBCR has been instrumental in furthering our understanding of breast cancer care. Numerous studies utilizing JBCR data have provided valuable insights into patient outcomes, treatment efficacy, and tumour characteristics. This summary highlights some of the key findings from these investigations. Tumour subtypes and long-term outcomes Research exploring the relationship between tumour subtypes and long-term outcomes has revealed significant differences in prognosis. For instance, HER2-low non-metastatic breast cancer patients exhibited better relapse-free survival and OS compared with HER2-zero patients, particularly those with hormone receptor-positive subtypes. 14 This suggests that HER2-low status could be a useful prognostic marker. Conversely, HER2-positive invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC) patients experienced poorer survival outcomes, underscoring the need for further research into effective treatment strategies for this subgroup. 15 Similarly, pure mucinous breast cancer patients showed superior outcomes compared with those with invasive ductal carcinoma or ILC, though chemotherapy did not significantly improve their prognosis. 16 Chemotherapy response and treatment-related outcomes Investigations into chemotherapy response and treatment-related outcomes have yielded important findings as well. Studies on NAC revealed that triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) patients faced the highest risk of relapse, especially if they did not achieve a pCR. 17 This highlights the need for closer follow-up in this high-risk group. Furthermore, studies using artificial intelligence and radiomics have shown promise in predicting NAC response, offering potential for more personalized treatment planning. 18 Impact of radiotherapy and novel treatments The impact of radiotherapy and novel treatments has also been a significant area of study. Radiotherapy has been shown to improve locoregional recurrence-free survival in patients with malignant phyllodes tumours, although it did not impact distant disease-free survival. 19 Additionally, advancements in radiotherapy techniques have successfully mitigated risks of cardiac toxicity in left-sided breast cancer patients. 20 Metaplastic breast cancer patients, in particular, significantly benefited from adjuvant radiotherapy, emphasizing its importance for aggressive subtypes. 21 Biological markers and prognostic indicators Researchers have also delved into the role of biological markers and prognostic indicators. Higher neutrophil–lymphocyte ratios and lower albumin–globulin ratios were found to correlate with worse survival outcomes, suggesting that these simple blood markers could potentially guide treatment decisions. 22 The JBCR has also validated combined anatomical and biological risk scores for staging breast cancer, demonstrating more accurate prognostication compared with anatomical staging alone. 23 Furthermore, studies have found that younger Asian breast cancer patients, compared with older ones, with a continuous year-on-year increase of about 4% in patients with luminal-type cancers and 8% in patients with TNBC in relapse rates and cancer deaths without any apparent threshold. 24 Socioeconomic and ethnic disparities in breast cancer outcomes Socioeconomic and ethnic disparities in breast cancer outcomes have also been demonstrated using JBCR data. Patients from lower socioeconomic strata, as estimated by patients’ residences housing value, experienced worse OS despite current medical subsidy schemes, suggesting that targeted, timely financial support can help to reduce disparities. 25 Despite having equivalent access to health care, Singapore Malay women have worse OS and cancer-specific survival compared with Chinese and Indian women. These differences do not appear to be related to screening uptake but may be influenced by genetic factors and other health-seeking behaviour including compliance to care. 26 Ethnic disparities were also observed in treatment-related toxicities, with Malay and Indian patients experiencing higher rates of febrile neutropenia compared with Chinese patients, suggesting the need for ethnic-specific management strategies. 27 Discussion The JBCR has demonstrated substantial value as a resource to further our understanding of breast cancer patients and their care in Singapore. By systematically amassing a large body of real-world data from the diverse population in Singapore, we have gained important insights into the complex interactions between breast cancer epidemiology, patients’ factors, environmental factors, disease subtypes, treatment patterns, and clinical outcomes. These findings not only inform clinical practice within Singapore but also contribute to the global understanding of breast cancer, particularly among Asian populations. 28 Large breast cancer-specific registries in Western countries have provided extensive information for what are largely Caucasian women. Efforts to address this blind spot to improve our understanding of Asian breast cancer have begun in several Asian regions ( Supplementary Table S2 , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2025.100159 ). For example, the Japanese Breast Cancer Society registry, with over 95 000 cases, collects comprehensive data on tumour characteristics, treatment modalities, and outcomes. 29 In Korea, data of patients with breast cancer were collected by the Korean Breast Cancer Society (KBCS) registry system and the Korean Central Cancer Registry. Since 1996, the KBCS has published articles annually sharing its analysis of the clinical characteristics and treatment patterns of breast cancer. An example of a report for the 29 729 women newly diagnosed with breast cancer in 2019 is referenced. 30 With over 25 000 patients documented, the Hong Kong Breast Cancer Registry focuses on disease patterns, treatment trends, and outcomes of breast cancer with an emphasis on physical and psychosocial impacts. Annually, the HKBCR publishes a report to communicate its findings to its stakeholders. 31 Also based in Singapore, the Singapore Breast Cancer Cohort Study (SGBCC) differs in a significant way from JBCR with comprehensive capturing of breast cancer risk factors, collection of biological material, and extensive germline genotyping for a significant portion of its cohort. Together, these registries aid to further characterizing Asian breast cancer. 32 To this growing body of evidence, the JBCR plays a complementary role in bridging the understanding of breast cancer trends within Singapore’s multi-ethnic population and the broader Asian context. JBCR aims to be of value to multiple stakeholders, including clinicians, researchers, health care administrators, policymakers, and patients. Clinicians can utilize JBCR data to predict patients’ treatment responses, refine prognostic models, and develop personalized and effective treatment plans tailored to each patient. Researchers can use the registry’s extensive baseline data to evaluate the impact of new treatments. Pharmaceutical companies and other research organizations can use JBCR’s data on patient demographics and clinical characteristics to assess Singapore’s suitability as a study site for recruiting patients into trials. With our detailed database, we can provide information to health care administrators and policymakers to make decisions on health budget allocation, resource management, and to develop interventions targeted to improve breast cancer awareness in underserved groups and to enhance financial support to improve treatment compliance among low-income patients. JBCR can also be used to conduct benchmarking between institutions in Singapore and against published standards of international leading cancer centres. This helps us to identify performance gaps and target areas for improvement. Based on each patient’s personal profile and disease characteristics, JBCR can provide personalized information on treatment and survival rates. This empowers patients to make more informed decisions for their shared care, leading to more individualized treatment plans that they are more likely to comply with and potentially better outcomes. For doctors, the BTB software carries out survival estimation for each patient using real-time calculations by the Kaplan–Meier method of a matched cohort drawn on-the-fly from JBCR, allowing treatment strategies to be made with outcomes in mind ( Supplementary Figure S2 , available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2025.100159 ). Strengths and limitations The JBCR’s broad-based collaborative approach, encompassing multiple institutions and a diverse patient population across Singapore, had significant strengths. This structure enabled research that is highly representative of Singapore’s population. The registry’s continuous, longitudinal data collection has facilitated tracking of critical outcomes, especially recurrence and survival. This process was significantly aided by the ability to link all health care data through the unique National Registration Identity Card number assigned to each Singaporean citizen and resident. Furthermore, the integration into routine clinical care and use of automated data collection tools, such as the BTB software and text-mining, have minimized manual entry, thereby making data collection more efficient and accurate. As an observational database, the JBCR is subject to inherent selection biases and variability in clinical practices across institutions. The reliance on existing routine clinical processes means that certain data, such as patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and quality-of-life indicators, may not be consistently captured. While relative survival is commonly used to account for background mortality without the use of cause of death data, applying this approach to the cohort in JBCR may result in biased estimates due to its relative incompleteness when compared with the Singapore Cancer Registry maintained by the National Registry of Diseases Office (NRDO) for the whole of Singapore. Accordingly, we present BCSS as a more appropriate and internally consistent outcome measure to illustrate the probability of a patient included in the JBCR to survive breast cancer. The 5-year BCSS in JBCR is 85.0% [95% confidence interval (CI) 84.8% to 85.2%], closely aligning with the NRDO’s published 5-year age-standardized relative survival rate of 83.1% (95% CI 82.3% to 83.9%) between 2003 and 2022 for Singapore. 7 Additionally, the lack of molecular and genomic data limits the registry’s applicability for translational research and precision medicine. Future research directions We have identified several areas for future research and development. Our top priority is to make genetic and molecular data available. The current limitations in this area restrict the JBCR’s utility for precision medicine and translational research. Efforts are under way to gain access to biological materials from hospital tissue banks and collaborate with national archives and programmes to access more genetic data which will significantly broaden the scope of research that JBCR can support. We are interested in improving the collection of PROMs to provide a more holistic understanding of the impact of disease and treatment on patients’ lives. While the JBCR currently has limited data on quality-of-life measures, the goal is to retrieve these measurements from the EMR as PROMs become increasingly used in routine clinical care. We believe this information will be a pre-requisite for research in cost–utility analysis and the value of patient-centric care. The registry already possesses a wealth of digital medical images, including mammograms, ultrasounds, computed tomography (CT) scans, and digital histopathology slides. Early work using radiomics of CT scans to predict responses to NAC has shown promise. Future plans include research to leverage artificial intelligence for multimodal analysis of clinical information and medical images to enhance cancer risk assessment, outcome prediction, and treatment response. To encompass a broader spectrum of the patient journey, the JBCR is expanding its scope to include the pre-diagnosis and long-term, post-treatment survivorship phases. To support this, collaborations with primary care providers, such as SingHealth Polyclinics, are being organized. Lastly, we hope to extend our network of collaborators from nationally to internationally. To support these efforts, the JBCR is working towards making its data Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP)-ready, facilitating interoperability with collaborators’ databases. This will make it possible to conduct comparative research across various populations, providing information about regional variations in breast cancer care and outcomes. Researchers interested in collaboration should reach out to the principal investigator. Conclusion The comprehensive real-world data in JBCR have contributed meaningfully to clinical care, research, and policy-making. By integrating data collection into clinical workflows, leveraging automation, and implementing rigorous quality control processes, we ensure the accuracy and completeness of JBCR. We uphold strict data security and governance protocols to balance patient privacy with the ethical and safe use of the data. Future efforts to expand the collection of PROMs, genetic, and molecular data will increase the value of the registry. Through ongoing and future collaborations, we believe JBCR will continue to serve our stakeholders well, improving breast cancer care in Singapore and beyond.
