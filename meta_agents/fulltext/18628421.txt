Introduction Body fatness (BF) during the life-course has been associated with breast cancer (BC); however, the results are discrepant depending on the timing of BF exposure, and whether women are pre- or post-menopausal. According to the Continuous Update Project, greater BF (body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference) during adulthood reduces the risk of BC in pre-menopausal women, while it increases the risk in post-menopause [ 1 ]. However, BF during childhood and adolescence is inversely associated with pre- and post-menopausal BC [ 1 ], suggesting a long-term protective effect of BF at young ages, despite adult BMI [ 2 ]. Although, there are scarce studies assessing BF under 18 years of age and BC risk, and most of them use self-report data on BF. There are some limitations at the moment to assess the association between BF during childhood and adolescence and BC. BF is measured in early life while BC is an outcome that occurs later in life. Thus, using an intermediate marker of BC such as breast density (BD) [ 3 ] could help in clarifying the cancer risk development. It is well established that mammographic breast density (BD) is a strong and independent risk factor for BC [ 4 ]. Currently, there are two measures of breast composition related to BC. The most common measure, percentage fibroglandular volume (%FGV), reflects the proportion of fibroglandular tissue within the breast but is strongly influenced by adiposity (higher body fat lowers %FGV). To account for this limitation, absolute fibroglandular volume (AFGV) is also assessed, representing the total volume of fibroglandular tissue independent of breast size. Both higher %FGV and higher AFGV have been linked to increased BC risk [ 5 ]. The association between BF and BD is contradictory and data at young ages is limited. BF at childhood and adolescence is consistently inversely associated with %BD, even after adjusting by BMI [ 3 , 6 – 9 ]. In contrast, the association with the absolute dense tissue is inconclusive; some studies have observed an inverse association [ 6 , 7 ], while others have observed no association at all [ 8 , 9 ]. On the other hand, puberty is a critical window of susceptibility for breast carcinogenesis [ 10 ]. During this period, particularly following the onset of menarche, BD reaches its peak, exhibiting considerable variability among young women [ 11 ]. Subsequently, BD tends to decline, influenced by various life events, including age, age at first full-term pregnancy, lactation, parity, age at menopause, and the use of drugs such as tamoxifen [ 12 , 13 ]. This period therefore represents an important opportunity to assess the role of body fatness and breast composition in the context of breast cancer risk. The Growth and Obesity Cohort Study (GOCs) is an ongoing cohort of 602 females recruited in 2006 at age 3.5 years in which we have collected data on body fatness (BMI, waist circumference % body fat) since birth throughout childhood and adolescence, and breast composition measured by Dual X-Ray Absorptiometry. Thus, we are able to assess if specific trajectories of body fatness during childhood and adolescence (4–19 y) are associated with (i) breast composition (%FGV and AFGV) at young adulthood (19 y) and (ii) changes in breast composition between the end of puberty and young adulthood. Methods Study design and population GOCs is a longitudinal follow-up of 602 girls from lower-middle socioeconomic status. In 2006, children attending public daycare centers in the South East area of Santiago-Chile were invited to participate in the GOCs study (mean age 3.5 years). Participants were singleton births during 2002–2003, who had birth weights between 2500 and 4500 g, with no medical or mental condition. We excluded girls with physical, medical, or endocrine diseases that could alter puberty or breast development [ 6 ] (Supplementary Fig. S1 ). Since 2006, GOCS girls had every year an appointment at INTA, but after 2009, visits were scheduled every 6 months to describe sexual maturation: debut and progression, according to Tanner Stage [ 14 ]. Breast development was assessed based on Tanner staging via visual inspection and palpation by a trained dietitian (inter and intra-observer concordance 0.9) [ 8 ] to differentiate from lipomastia with permanent supervision of a pediatric endocrinologist. We have previously reported a 7% prevalence of transient thelarche in this cohort; however, for the purpose of this study, we classified a breast stage 2 (B2) diagnosis only when girls subsequently progressed to B2 or higher. The date of the first menstrual bleeding (age at menarche) was recorded at every visit or by telephonic follow-up. Mothers and daughters had to answer a questionnaire to differentiate vaginal infections or other clinical conditions from first menses (further details of sexual maturation assessment have been publish previously) [ 15 ]. After menarche, visits have become annual based on the date of their first menstrual bleeding. Calendar menses are used to determine the day of menstrual cycling at different visits. We selected 322 nulliparous women who had body fatness evaluation during childhood and puberty and a breast DXA exam at 19 years. Based on Cohen’s effect size for multiple regression [ 7 ] using different scenarios of number of latent classes (i.e. adiposity trajectories) as predictor and number of confounders (five), we were able to detect statistically significant small-medium effects ( f 2 ) [ 9 ], assuming a power of 80% and alpha error of 5%. Measurements Breast composition (%FGV and absolute AFGV) Breast composition was measured 4 years after menarche (4PM) and at 19 years. We used a Dual X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA) from GE Lunar Prodigy Bone Densitometer (GE Healthcare) calibrated to measure absolute fibroglandular volume (AFGV), breast volume (BV), and % of fibroglandular volume (%FGV) as the product of AFGV/BV*100. DXA measurements were performed by a single trained technician, following a previously described positioning protocol [ 16 ]. The left breast was scanned first, then the right, and the left was repositioned to assess repeatability (precision: 2.8%) [ 17 ]. DXA was calibrated using a custom thickness step phantom (N17NS phantom) [ 18 ], made of reference material, which consists of a density seven steps phantom of constant thickness of different percentages of breast fibroglandular (from 0 to 100%) and adipose tissue. Breast composition data is calculated based on a two-compartment model (adipose and fibro-glandular tissue) using a software developed at the University of California San Francisco (Prof John Shepherd). A trained reader (ICC 0.9) manually delineated the total projected breast area, and the software automatically estimated %FGV, AFGV, and BV. The amount of ionizing radiation dose used is exceedingly small; the estimated effective dose is 1.32 uSv, which is much less than the effective dose of 1-day background radiation at sea level (8 uSv) [ 19 ]. A study conducted by our group in adult women (mean age 36.6 years) showed that %FGV and AFGV measured by DXA correlate strongly (r = 0.7) with percent density and dense area derived from mammography [ 17 ]. However, to our knowledge, no studies have yet evaluated whether breast composition measures obtained via DXA are associated with breast cancer risk. Anthropometry We have routinely collected data on weight, height and waist circumference at INTA (with participants being barefoot and wearing light clothes) by a trained dietitian following standardized protocols; ICC for all measurements is ≥ 0.75 [ 6 ]. Weight was measured with a portable electronic scale (Seca 770), precision of 0.1 kg, height was measured with a portable stadiometer (Harpenden 603) to the nearest 0.1 cm. Waist circumference (WC) was measured using a measuring tape (Lufkin, Model W606PM) with 200 cm capacity and 0.1 cm precision. The waist was the midpoint between the last rib and the right iliac crest in a horizontal line to the left iliac crest. BMI (weight (kg)/height (m) 2 ) and Z-scores were calculated based on WHO 2007 growth references [ 20 ]. Birth weight and weight and height before 2006 were obtained from medical records [ 21 ]. Bio-electrical impedance analysis (BIA) From 7 years of age and onwards, we have performed an annual BIA using an 8 electrodes bioimpedianciometer (Tanita BC-418). We estimated the %fat mass and the fat mass index (FMI: fat (kgs)/ height (m) 2 ). Maternal data Number of years of formal education was inquired as a proxy of socioeconomic level. Statistical analysis We conducted a comprehensive exploratory analysis to evaluate data quality and describe variable distributions. Continuous variables were summarized using means, standard deviations, percentiles, and transformed to z-scores when necessary, while categorical data were presented as percentages. Our primary outcome variables included %FGV and AFGV at the 19-year visit and the change in %FGV and AFGV between 4 years after menarche and the − 19 year visit. Change in breast composition was calculated as the value at 19 years minus the corresponding value at the 4PM visit. In addition, we examined the association between quartiles of body fatness at 4 PM and subsequent changes in %FGV and AFGV. Adiposity trajectories (BMI z-score, waist circumference, % fat mass, and FMI) were estimated using Growth Mixture Models (GMM) [ 22 ]. GMM is a statistical method that identifies distinct subgroups (latent classes) within a population, each following different growth trajectories over time. These trajectories, based on chronological and developmental age, account for individual variability in adiposity patterns. The optimal GMM model was chosen according the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) and the interpretability of the identified classes, ensuring they represent meaningful and distinct growth patterns. Probabilities of belonging to each latent class were estimated for all individuals, and each was assigned to the class for which they had the highest probability of membership. Linear regression models were used to examine the associations between adiposity markers (BMI z-score, waist circumference, % fat mass, and FMI) at various time points (from ages 2 to 19 years) and %FGV and AFGV at 19 years, as well as changes in breast composition between the 4 PM and 19-year visits. In the latter models we take into account the breast composition (%FGV and AFGV) at 4 PM visit, in order to distinguish whether any observed changes reflected true increases over time rather than associations driven by baseline breast composition levels. We also modeled the difference between body fatness indicators between 4 PM and 19 y (formula as: 19 y body fatness indicator – 4 PM body fatness indicator) and the period between 2 and 4 PM (formula as: 4 PM body fatness indicator – 2 PM body fatness indicator) with breast composition at 19 years. These models were adjusted by birthweight, maternal education, adiposity level at baseline (2 PM or 4 PM) and the time between menarche and 19-year visit. Additionally, generalized linear models were applied to explore the relationship between categorical latent adiposity classes and breast composition (%FGV and AFGV) at 19 years. These models were weighted by the individual probability of class membership. Beta coefficients and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were reported, both crude and adjusted for factors including birth weight, maternal education and time between menarche and visit 19 y. Confounders (birthweight and maternal education) were selected based on existing evidence in the literature, as they are associated with both the exposure and the outcome and are not part of the causal pathway. We further adjusted for the time between menarche and the visit at 19 years, given the potential variability in this interval. All analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.1.3 (R Core Team, 2022, Vienna, Austria) [ 23 ]. Ethics The study protocol was approved by the Ethical Committee of the Institute of Nutrition and Food Technology, University of Chile. Written informed consent was obtained from all parents or legal guardians, and assent was provided by all participating girls. Results From 602 girls invited to participate in 2006, we obtained complete data from 322 girls at 19 years of age (Supplementary Fig. 1 ). Among those without DXA scans at 19 years, maternal education levels were higher compared to those who underwent DXA scans (74% vs 63%) (see Supplementary Table S1 ). At 19 years, the prevalence of obesity among girls was 21.2%, with a mean %fat mass of 33.7% (SD = 8.9), and 55.3% reported low physical activity levels. The mean time between menarche and the 19y visit was 7.7 years (sd = 1.1). The mean %FGV among girls was 46.3% (SD = 14.9), with a mean change in %FGV between the 19-year visit and the preceding visit (4 years after menarche) of − 4.14% (SD = 10.8%) (Table 1 ). Adolescents in the lowest quartile of body fatness (any indicator) at 4 PM showed a significantly greater decline in %FGV compared with those in the highest quartile, no trend was observed with AFGV (Supplementary Table S2 ). 32.5] 0.607 Discussion To the best of our knowledge, this study is one of the first investigation to prospectively explore the relationship between various markers of adiposity (including BMI, waist circumference, % fat mass, and fat mass index) and breast composition during a pivotal period of susceptibility. Consistent with our expectations, we observed an inverse correlation between adiposity trajectories and %FGV at 19 years, as well as between adiposity markers assessed from ages 2 to 19 years and %FGV. We did not observe an association between adiposity markers and change in %FGV and AFGV during 4PM and 19 year visit. However, girls in the lowest quartiles of BMI z-score at 4 PM had higher %FGV reductions compared to girls in the highest quartile. Conversely, concerning AFGV, only recent measures of adiposity markers (at 4 PM or 19 years) exhibited a positive association, while adiposity trajectories during childhood and adolescence failed to demonstrate any significant relationship with AFGV at 19 years. Notably, an increase in adiposity over two consecutive visits was linked to a lower %FGV, whereas an increase in BMI, WC, and FMI between 4 PM and 19 years was associated with an increase in AFGV at 19 years. Understanding why childhood obesity prevents breast cancer is of interest, as it is not a recommendation we wish to promote. Many of these associations may be due to the fact that the data was collected a long time ago and the prevalence of obesity at that time is not the same as it is now, or it was self-reported. Therefore, measuring breast density at the end of puberty is an alternative to try to understand this process. A previously work by our team observed that BD is maximal after puberty onset and remains stable after 2 years post-menarche [ 24 ], suggesting that measuring BD at 4PM and 19 years previous the first birth could be a good endpoint to assess the relationship between early risk markers and BD. Observational studies measuring breast density (BD) in younger women align with findings in adults, showing that body fatness during childhood and adolescence is inversely associated with %FGV (fibroglandular volume) [ 11 , 25 – 29 ]. Girls with obesity tend to have more non-dense tissue (fat tissue in the breast), leading to a lower percentage of BD. While this suggests that BD could be a mediator in the relationship between adiposity and breast cancer (BC), it is important to explore how adiposity relates to absolute dense tissue. Earlier metanalysis performed in adult women indicated that %FGV is positively associated with BC risk in general [ 4 ], and recent data also from adult women supports that higher absolute fibroglandular volume (AFGV) is linked to BC [ 5 ]. However, similar to findings in adults, evidence regarding the relationship between adiposity and absolute dense tissue remains inconclusive. The Dietary Intervention Study in Children, a prospective study, reported that body fatness measured by trained personnel between ages 8 and 17 was inversely associated with %BD, absolute dense tissue, and non-dense tissue at ages 25–29 age (measured by Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)) [ 28 ]. Similar results were found when measuring body fat and the android: gynecoid ratio using DXA in the same cohort [ 26 ] as well as in the AVON Longitudinal study of Parents and Children that assess anthropometry since birth to 21y and BD at 21y using MRI [ 27 ]. Contrastingly two cross sectional studies, Boyd 2009 [ 11 ] using MRI in women aged 18–30, and Novotny 2011 [ 25 ], using DXA in girls aged 10–17, observed an inverse association between body fatness and %BD but a positive association with absolute dense tissue. Most of these studies have been conducted in Caucasian populations in the U.S. and Europe. To our knowledge, only one study in Latino women reported discrepant findings [ 30 ]. Rice 2013, using data from the ESMaestras cohort in Mexico, found that self-reported BMI before pregnancy (ages 25–35) was positively associated with mammographic %BD in both pre- and post-menopausal women, but no association was observed with absolute dense or non-dense tissue [ 30 ]. In line with this, our study also found a positive association between AFGV at age 19 and contemporary adiposity markers (measured at the 4 PM and 19-year visits). Another critical factor to consider in these associations is fat distribution. A study by Maskarinec 2020 [ 31 ] the Adiposity Phenotype Study, using a multiethnic cohort from Hawaii (mean age 69.1 ± 2.7 years), investigated how fat distribution (using DXA and MRI) correlates with mammographic percent breast density. It found that women with higher total body fat, trunk to periphery fat ratio, visceral fat and subcutaneous fat were inversely correlated to percentage of mammographic density. However, higher visceral-to-subcutaneous fat ratio were more likely to have higher mammographic percent density. This suggests that while most adiposity markers are inversely associated with breast density, it is important to further explore the specific relationship between visceral and subcutaneous fat and BD, particularly in pediatric populations. Even though childhood obesity might have a protective effect, some types of body fatness (visceral to subcutaneous ratio) may increase breast density. A recent Mendelian randomization study, based on data from Research Program on Genes, Environment and Health, which included women aged 40–74 years (80% postmenopausal), aimed at understanding the link between childhood obesity, breast density (BD), and breast cancer (BC) found that childhood adiposity (self-reported as “thinner than average,” “about average,” or “plumper than average” at 10 years old) was inversely associated with %FGV and AFGV, but positively associated with the non-dense area. The study also observed that 56% of the protective effect of childhood obesity on breast cancer risk may be due to the reduction in dense breast area during childhood [ 32 ]. The mediating role of BD in the relationship between childhood obesity and breast cancer has been supported by previous observational studies [ 3 ]. However, our findings do not align with these results, as we did not observe an inverse association with AFGV. Instead, we found a positive association, particularly when body fatness was measured closer to the DXA evaluation. Furthermore, we did not observe an association between body fatness and the change in %FGV or AFGV across this critical developmental period. However, when stratifying participants by quartiles of body fatness at 4 year after menarche, we observed that girls in the lower body fat categories were more likely to experience a greater reduction in %FGV compared with those with higher body fatness. To our knowledge, ours is the only study with repeated measurements during this transitional period, allowing us to evaluate how body fatness may influence the decline in %FGV after its peak around menarche. Nonetheless, further studies are needed to clarify how childhood adiposity may shape breast composition prior to pregnancy. Histological studies in normal breast tissue have permitted to better understand the association between body fatness (BF) and breast density (BD). OH 2021 [ 33 ] analyzed the breast composition of 788 women with benign breast disease (participants form the Nurses’ Health Study BBD subgroup), utilizing deep-learning image analysis to estimate the percentage of epithelial, fibrous stromal, and adipose tissue in breast biopsy slides. They found that recall body fatness using 9-level pictogram during childhood, at 18 years, and in adulthood was positively associated with adipose tissue and inversely associated with fibrous stromal tissue. Additionally, they observed an inverse association between recall body fatness at 18 years and epithelial tissue, suggesting that body fatness during puberty may differentially influence breast composition. In line with this, we observed positive associations between body fatness and AFGV in the same visit at the 19-year DXA assessment, which supports the evidence of this differential effect. However, contrary to OH 2021, our study found a positive relationship rather than the expected negative association. This study has several important strengths and limitations worth considering. One of the primary limitations is the lack of data on fat distribution, particularly visceral and subcutaneous fat, which might play a significant role in understanding the relationship between childhood adiposity and breast density. Additionally, the first two time points (2 and 4 years) had a higher proportion of missing data, which may have contributed to the greater variability observed during these periods. We also used breast density measures obtained from DXA scans, an alternative method suitable for younger populations. Although DXA-derived measures correlate with mammographic density [ 17 , 34 , 35 ], it remains unclear whether they show the same associations with breast cancer risk as those established using mammography. Lastly, as in any observational study, there is a potential for residual confounding due to unmeasured factors that could influence the observed associations. Potential non measured confounders in our study could be early environmental exposures, such as early age of tobacco and alcohol use, which both have been related to BD [ 36 – 38 ].On the other hand, the study has notable strengths. First, it is based on a birth cohort with detailed anthropometric and body fatness measurements collected by trained dietitians from age 2 throughout childhood, allowing for the identification of distinct adiposity classes. Second, we used a variety of adiposity markers to gain a more comprehensive understanding of body fatness and its potential effects. Finally, breast density was measured using DXA scans at an optimal time, before the participants' first pregnancy and after they had completed puberty, a period when breast density is at its highest, which enhances the validity of our findings. In conclusion, our results suggest that higher adiposity during childhood, whether measured by BMI, waist circumference (WC), or percentage of fat mass by bioimpedance, is inversely correlated with %FGV. Additionally, we observed a positive association between contemporary adiposity markers and AFGV, particularly around the time of the DXA evaluation. Further research is required to better understand the relationship between childhood obesity, breast density (BD), and breast cancer (BC). Future studies should explore the role of fat mass distribution, specifically accounting for visceral and subcutaneous fat, in relation to BD. It is crucial to note that these findings should not be interpreted as promoting childhood obesity to reduce breast cancer risk. Public health recommendations should focus on a balanced approach to managing childhood obesity. Supplementary Information Supplementary file 1.
