Background Kidney cancer is one of the top ten most common cancers in the United States (U.S.) [ 1 ]. Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is the most common type, which accounts for over 80% of kidney cancer [ 2 , 3 ]. Nationally and globally, RCC incidence rates are increasing [ 3 , 4 ]. This trend coincides with the obesity epidemic, 3 and nearly 30% of RCC incidence is attributable to higher body mass index (BMI) [ 5 ]. Recent epidemiologic studies demonstrated that a greater lifetime excess body weight significantly increases the risk of various cancer types, including RCC [ 6 – 9 ]. Studies have also shown that exposure to excess weight in early life increases the risk of RCC [ 10 , 11 ]. Despite the well-described association of obesity with RCC risk, patients who have obesity (body mass index, BMI ≥ 30 kg/m 2 ) at diagnosis have a more favorable prognosis than patients with normal weight [ 12 , 13 ]. This counterintuitive relationship is commonly referred to as the obesity paradox. Possible explanations of this obesity paradox include imprecise measures of adiposity, notably in racial and ethnic groups, not including effect modifiers or confounders (e.g., metabolic comorbidities), timing of BMI measures, and molecular heterogeneity [ 14 – 16 ]. Previous research attempted to address the obesity paradox using pre-diagnostic scans for more precise measurement of adiposity and skeletal muscle but varied in study design and findings [ 17 ]. While many studies show a negative effect of low skeletal muscle cross-sectional area, the associations of abdominal adiposity with RCC pathology remain unclear [ 17 ]. A study in Europe showed that higher visceral adipose tissue (VAT) cross-sectional area and higher VAT relative to total adipose tissue (rVAT) were associated with lower odds of advanced RCC [ 18 ]. Other studies found that rVAT was associated with high-grade and advanced-stage RCC [ 19 , 20 ]. In a U.S. study on clear cell RCC, the results for VAT were inconclusive. Higher VAT cross-sectional area was associated with poorer disease-free survival, but not with advanced-stage, high-grade, or overall survival [ 21 , 22 ]. On the other hand, low skeletal muscle density (SMD) and high VAT radiodensity (VATD) were associated with advanced-stage, high-grade, and poor survival, even after mutually adjusting for all body composition variables. In addition, these studies did not include patients from diverse backgrounds with higher RCC burden, as well as higher prevalence of obesity and obesity-related comorbidities, such as Hispanics, American Indian (AI), and non-Hispanic Black (NHB) patients, compared to non-Hispanic White (NHW) [ 23 – 25 ]. Differences in the prevalence of obesity and obesity-related risk factors may explain these disparities. Because almost all patients undergo imaging assessment pre-operatively, body composition can potentially be an inexpensive and noninvasive tool for risk stratification. However, body composition may vary across racial and ethnic groups [ 26 – 28 ], and the effect of adiposity on cancer risk, tumor characteristics, and outcomes may also vary across racial and ethnic groups [ 29 , 30 ]. Further evaluation of the use of body composition for risk stratification is necessary in diverse patient populations. This study investigated variation in cross-sectional body composition measurements across racial and ethnic groups and other factors (i.e., sex, comorbidities, and neighborhood socioeconomic characteristics) and assessed associations between each cross-sectional body composition measure and diagnosis with clear cell histology, high grade, and advanced stage in a diverse cohort. We hypothesized that VAT would be associated with more aggressive RCC pathological characteristics. VAT surrounding organs has local and systemic consequences and is believed to be a driver of obesity-related health risks [ 31 ]. Based on this as well as previous study showing associations between high VAT and aggressive pathological characteristics, we hypothesized that (1) higher cross-sectional area of VAT would be associated with diagnosis of high-grade, advanced-stage, and clear cell subtype (more aggressive, obesity-associated subtype), and (2) patients from racial and ethnic groups with a high RCC burden would have higher VAT. This study also explored whether the radiodensity of each adipose tissue type and skeletal muscle (IMATD, VATD, SATD, and SMD), which may reflect adipose and muscle quality, was associated with diagnosis of clear cell, high grade, and advanced stage. Methods Materials This is a cross-sectional study of RCC patients who underwent partial or radical nephrectomy between 2006 and 2022 at two academic institutions, the University of Arizona Banner Medical Center Tucson (BUMCT) and Roswell Park Comprehensive Cancer Center (Roswell Park). At BUMCT, electronic medical records of RCC patients were retrospectively reviewed as described previously to collect demographic, clinical, and pathological data and to identify eligible patients [ 32 , 33 ]. The eligibility to be included in this study was adult patients with pathology confirmed RCC without prior diagnosis of other cancer and with computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance (MR) scans available for research projects ( n = 134, 80 patients with CT and 54 patients with MR scans). At Roswell Park, de-identified demographic, clinical, and pathological data were obtained from the Roswell Park Cancer Registry and electronic medical records. We aimed to include as many patients from minoritized racial and ethnic groups as possible. Only CT scans were obtained and used at Roswell Park ( n = 134). At each site, BMI (kg/m 2 ) at the time of diagnosis (or surgery) was extracted from medical records. Approval of the research project was obtained from the Institutional Review Boards of the University of Arizona and Roswell Park. BMI was parameterized as a dichotomous variable [patients with obesity (BMI 30) vs. without obesity (BMI ≥ 30) or four categories as BMI 18.5–24.9, 25–29.9, 30–34.9, and ≥ 35 based on World Health Organization classification. Patients with BMI ≥ 40 represented the smallest group ( 15%) in this patient population and were categorized with patients with BMI 35–39.9. Zip code of patients’ residence was linked to Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) developed by Centers for Disease Control and Prevention [ 34 ]. SVI measures neighborhood-level socioeconomic challenges, and the scores range from 0 (less vulnerable) to 1 (most vulnerable) as social and economic challenges in the neighborhoods increase. Hispanic group includes patients of any racial background with Hispanic ethnicity, while patients from non-Hispanic backgrounds were grouped based on their racial background (i.e., NHW, NHB, and AI). Body composition analysis Using preoperative ( 6 months prior to surgery) computed tomography ( n = 214) or magnetic resonance ( n = 54) scans, we measured cross-sectional area (cm 2 ) and radiodensity in Hounsfield Units (HU; CT scans only) of intermuscular, visceral, and subcutaneous adipose tissue as well as skeletal muscle. A single CT or MR scan slice at the inferior edge of, or immediately caudal to, the third lumbar vertebrae (L3) was selected. Adipose tissue and skeletal muscle were segmented using sliceOmatic image analysis software (version 6, TomoVision, Quebec, Canada). For CT scan segmentation, minimum and maximum thresholds were set between − 190 and − 30 for intermuscular adipose tissue (IMAT) and subcutaneous adipose tissue (SAT), between − 150 and − 50 HU for VAT, and between − 29 and + 150 HU for skeletal muscle following prior body composition studies [ 21 , 35 , 36 ]. For MR scan segmentation, minimum and maximum thresholds were manually set by the trained analyst according to standardized protocol based on image signal intensity to accurately delineate and capture all regions of interest. To assess reproducibility, an expert repeated segmentation for a subset of MR scans (17 of 54 MR scans, 31%), and agreement across body composition measures was excellent with Intraclass Correlation Coefficient ranging 0.961–0.999). We also assessed differences in median body composition measurements between CT and MR scans. Because we found differences in VATI but no consistent differences in other body composition measurements or no correlation with RCC subtype, grade, or stage, and because use of CT versus MR depended on insurance coverage and other health conditions rather than RCC subtype, grade or stage, we combined the results from two scan types in our analyses. Area measurements were normalized for height squared (cm 2 /m 2 ) to calculate intermuscular, visceral, subcutaneous and total adipose tissue indices (IMATI, VATI, and SATI), and skeletal muscle index (SMI). Total adipose tissue index (TATI) was calculated by adding IMAT, VAT, and SAT and dividing by height squared. Relative size of VAT (rVATI or percent VAT) to total adiposity was calculated (rVATI = VATI/TATI × 100). Primary exposure variables assessed in this study were each body composition indices (IMATI, VATI, SATI, and SMI). To further investigate the adiposity and skeletal muscle combined effect, we created body composition phenotypes. Body composition phenotypes were defined by combining sex-specific medians for each adiposity index with SMI (high vs. low). For each adiposity index, we created four categories, (1) low adiposity and high SMI (reference), (2) high adiposity and high SMI, (3) low adiposity and low SMI, and (4) high adiposity and low SMI. In addition, radiodensity for each adipose tissue type and skeletal muscle (IMATD, VATD, SATD, and SMD) was evaluated. After removing outliers (e.g., HU values outside of set range the specific adipose tissue and skeletal muscle due to poor scan quality), we included 198 patients for the radiodensity analysis. Our primary exposure variable of interest was VAT, while exploring associations of other body composition measurements. Outcome variables This study assessed associations of body composition measures with diagnosis of aggressive pathological characteristics (clear cell histology, high grade, and advanced stage). Diagnosis with clear cell histologic subtype is associated with obesity, and clear cell subtype is more aggressive than other RCC subtypes [ 37 , 38 ]. Grade 3 or 4 tumors were considered as high grade, while stages III and IV were considered as advanced stages. When patients had bilateral RCC, pathology information from the first surgery was used. Statistical analysis Characteristics of patients were described using median and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables and percentages for categorical variables. Differences in body composition measurements and BMI between racial and ethnic groups were tested using Mann–Whitney U test. Non-parametric tests were used to examine correlations between BMI, body composition measurements, and SVI. Logistic regression analyses were used to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Body composition indices were modeled as continuous variables and separately as categorical variables. We report body composition indices as a categorical variable, and the second quartile (Q2) was selected as the reference group because the associations between body composition indices and diagnosis with high grade and advanced stage were non-linear, potentially with J-shaped associations. For radiodensity, the first quartile (Q1) was used as the reference group. Linear trend was also examined treating quartiles of body composition indices, radiodensity, and rVAT as continuous variables. The adjusted models included demographic and clinical variables that were significantly associated with outcome variable in unadjusted model, such as age, sex, race and ethnicity, SVI, comorbidities (hypertension and diabetes), study site, histology (not for diagnosis with clear cell subtype), and smoking status. We also followed prior body composition studies for model building, and BMI was included in the model to test the independent association of BMI [ 39 ]. For the analysis of body composition indices, all four indices, IMATI, VATI, SATI, and SMI, were mutually adjusted to test the independent effect [ 21 , 40 ]. Similarly, analysis of body composition radiodensity included IMATD, VATD, SATD, and SMD in the same model. Sensitivity analyses were performed by removing patients with SAT cutoff from the scans and patients with MR scans as well as further adjusting for scan type. For analysis of IMAT, VAT, and SAT phenotype, the adiposity measurements not used to define phenotype were included in the model for mutual adjustment of body composition variables. We considered p 0.05 as statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using SAS version 9.4. Results Characteristics of patients A total of 268 patients, including 56.7% NHW, 25.4% Hispanic, 7.1% AI, 6.3% NHB patients, were included (Table 1 ). In the total sample, median age was 62 years (IQR 16.0), 68.7% were male. The prevalence of comorbidities, smoking, and histological subtypes varied across racial and ethnic groups. Compared with NHW patients, Hispanic, NHB, and AI patients were more likely to have diabetes and both diabetes and hypertension. Smoking was less common in Hispanic patients (66.2% vs. 41.4% never smoked in Hispanic and NHW patients). Clear cell subtype was more common in Hispanic (95.6%) and AI (94.7%) patients, while papillary subtype was more common in NHB patients (58.8%). Patients from minoritized racial and ethnic backgrounds, as well as patients with diabetes and both diabetes and hypertension, were more likely to come from high SVI neighborhoods ( Supplementary Table 1 ). High-grade and advanced-stage RCC was more common in AI (47.4%) compared with NHW (30.7%) patients, but the differences for grade and stage across racial and ethnic groups were not statistically significant. Associations of body composition with RCC pathological characteristics Compared with patients with non-clear cell subtypes, patients with clear cell subtype had higher median IMATI (5.6 vs. 4.8, p = 0.05) and SATI (89.1 vs. 69.0, p = 0.004) and lower median SMD (32.3 vs. 41.6, p 0.001) ( Supplementary Table 5 ). In multivariable logistic regression analysis, higher IMATI, Quartile 4 (Q4) compared with Q2, was associated with increased odds of clear cell subtype (OR 6.93, 95% CI 1.74–27.58; p for trend = 0.01) (Table 3 ). Higher SMD (Q4 vs. Q1) was associated with reduced odds of clear cell (OR 0.10, 95% CI 0.02—0.49, p for trend 0.001). In the same model, Hispanic ethnicity was associated with increased odds of diagnosis with clear cell RCC (OR 8.27, 95% CI 1.98—34.8) ( Supplementary Table 6 ). Obesity increased the odds of diagnosis of clear cell RCC, but the association was not significant. Moreover, higher rVATI was associated with clear cell RCC (Table 4 ). In the analysis of all patients combined, each rVATI quartile increase was associated with approximately 50% increased odds (OR per quartile 1.53, 95% CI 1.00–2.33) of clear cell diagnosis. In both sexes, rVATI was associated with increased odds of clear cell subtype. For analysis of IMAT, VAT, and SAT phenotype, the adiposity measurements not used to define phenotype were included in the model for mutual adjustment of body composition variables Discussion This study of diverse RCC patients investigated the variation in body composition and showed that various factors, such as race and ethnicity, neighborhood characteristics, and comorbidities, are correlated with body composition. Associations between body composition, particularly VAT, and pathological characteristics were also examined. Overall, greater abdominal adiposity and lower skeletal muscle mass were associated with aggressive pathology. Specifically, higher IMAT was associated with clear cell histology and advanced-stage RCC. Higher SATI was associated with high grade, and higher rVATI was associated with clear cell subtype, whereas low SMI and low adiposity phenotypes were associated with high-grade RCC. High VATD was also associated with high-grade RCC, and low SMD was associated with the clear cell subtype. Factors correlated with body composition variation in RCC patients and their potential effect on associations of body composition with pathological characteristics and oncologic outcomes are unknown. Strong correlation between BMI and body composition is consistent with another study of RCC patients, demonstrating that BMI may partially capture variation in adiposity [ 21 ]. We investigated variation in BMI and body composition across racial and ethnic groups because previous studies have shown that BMI and body composition can have distinct associations with cancer outcomes across racial and ethnic groups [ 29 , 41 , 42 ]. In this study, BMI did not capture the heterogeneity in adiposity and muscle distribution across racial and ethnic groups. Although BMI was similar across groups, several body composition measures differed significantly, indicating that BMI alone may not reflect important differences in adipose tissue and muscle compartments. Moreover, studies have shown that neighborhood social factors are associated with obesity in cancer patients [ 43 – 45 ]. In our study, we also observed that neighborhood factors and comorbidities such as diabetes and hypertension, which established risk factors for RCC mortality, were correlated with one another and with body composition [ 46 ]. These findings suggest that further studies are necessary to investigate whether differences in body composition impact RCC outcomes in diverse patient populations, with analyses that account for social factors and comorbidities. In this study, greater abdominal adiposity was associated with aggressive pathology (e.g., high IMAT with clear cell and advanced stage, SATI with high grade, and higher rVATI with clear cell subtype). We hypothesized that VAT may be associated with more aggressive RCC pathological features. In studies of metabolic diseases, a greater VAT volume was associated with higher circulating levels of C-reactive protein, triglycerides, leptin, and fatty acid binding protein 4 (FABP-4), but lower levels of adiponectin and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [ 47 , 48 ]. High adiposity may promote inflammation in adipose tissue, including perinephric adipose tissue surrounding the kidney, creating a tumor microenvironment that supports tumor aggressiveness and progression [ 49 ]. However, the relationship between VAT cross-sectional area and RCC aggressive pathology is still inconclusive [ 18 – 21 , 50 ], and the current study similarly provides mixed findings. rVATI was associated with obesity-related aggressive subtype (clear cell), which is consistent with a previous study by Wang et al., whereas rVATI was associated with reduced odds of high-grade RCC [ 51 ]. This study also showed that increasing SATI was associated with high-grade pathology. Maurits et al. have shown that increasing SAT was associated with higher grade pathology in female patients [ 18 ], but many other studies did not find any association between SAT and aggressive pathology [ 19 – 21 ]. Differences in patient populations, lifestyle, and exposures to various risk factors may have contributed to these inconsistent findings. Also, cross-sectional measurement of body composition of a single L3 slice may be an imperfect proxy for the adiposity surrounding the kidneys, which could have had more direct impacts on tumor biology [ 52 ]. The independent association of BMI with high-grade RCC also suggests that there may be additional factors not included in this study influencing the diagnosis of high-grade RCC. Our study also provides evidence suggesting a potential relationship between sarcopenia or cachexia and aggressive pathology. First, lower skeletal muscle mass (low SMI and low adiposity phenotypes) was associated with high-grade RCC. Patients with low SMI and low adiposity phenotype were older and had lower BMI than patients with other body composition phenotypes. Second, high IMAT is indicative of decreased muscle strength and function and impaired metabolic health related to aging and linked to systemic and tissue level inflammation [ 53 – 55 ]. In this study, high IMATI was associated with clear cell subtype, and low IMATI was associated with reduced odds of advanced stage. However, the association between high IMATI and high SMI phenotype and advanced stage suggests further investigations are necessary to understand the relationship between IMAT and skeletal muscle. Additionally, rVAT was inversely associated with high grade, but instead of high VAT having a protective effect, patients with sarcopenia or cachexia may have lower VAT along with skeletal muscle. This is consistent with our findings showing a low SMI and low VATI phenotype associated with high-grade RCC. In our previous study of the same patient populations, consistent with reverse causation, we found that high BMI at diagnosis had increased odds of diagnosis with high-grade and advanced-stage RCC, and patients who experienced unintentional weight loss, which is indicative of cachexia, had higher odds of high grade and advanced stage [ 56 ]. Preoperative weight loss is also associated with poor survival [ 57 – 59 ]. Screening for sarcopenia and cachexia may be important to identify high-risk RCC patients. However, because of the cross-sectional study design, we cannot determine causality, that is, whether sarcopenia increases the risk of high-grade RCC or whether more severe disease leads to cachexia. Repeated measurements of body composition or BMI were not available. Prospective studies with longitudinal body composition or BMI data are needed to determine whether sarcopenia and preoperative body composition are associated with survival. This study also explored the relationship of adipose tissue radiodensity and SMD with pathological characteristics. We found that high VATD was associated with advanced stage, which is consistent with previous studies showing an association of high VATD with high grade, advanced stage, and poor survival [ 21 , 22 ]. High adipose tissue radiodensity indicates smaller adipocyte (depleted or altered lipid content) and fibrosis with the presence of immune cells in adipose tissue [ 60 – 62 ]. On the other hand, we found an inverse association between SATD and stage. Lower adipose tissue radiodensity is associated with larger adipocyte weight or size of each adipose type [ 63 ]. Lower adipose tissue radiodensity is linked to increased cardiometabolic risk and insulin resistance [ 64 , 65 ]. Higher SMD was also associated with reduced odds of the clear cell subtype. Higher SMD (or skeletal muscle attenuation) indicates healthier muscle quality and is associated with muscle strength, while lower SMD is associated with higher lipid contents in muscle fibers and diabetes [ 66 – 68 ]. Adipose tissue radiodensity and SMD are also associated with circulating levels of metabolic and inflammatory biomarkers [ 64 , 69 , 70 ]. Moreover, diabetes is also associated with a diagnosis of clear cell subtype [ 71 ]. Taken together, adipose tissue and skeletal muscle radiodensity may reflect underlying biological conditions related to aggressive pathology, and some of the biological conditions may be common in RCC, cardiovascular, and diabetes risk. Future studies need to further investigate the relationships of kidney tumors with adipose tissue surrounding the kidney as well as skeletal muscle, accounting for comorbidities. There are some limitations in this study. First, the sample size, especially patients from racial and ethnic minoritized groups, was small. We could not perform race and ethnicity-stratified analysis to examine the differences in associations across racial and ethnic groups. Even though VATI measurements differed between CT and MR scans, we used MR scans to include as many patients from minoritized racial and ethnic groups as possible. The analysis results from the model further adjusting for scan type were very similar to the results without scan type in the model, and VATI was not associated with pathological characteristics. We observed several associations between body composition measures and pathological characteristics, but CIs were often wide for quartile-based comparisons. We chose to use quartiles to better capture non-linear relationship between body composition measures and pathological characteristics after restricted cubic spline models, indicating non-linear relationships but poor model performance with wide CIs. Additionally, a small number of patients had non-clear cell histologic subtypes. These estimates and results should be interpreted cautiously. Second, the retrospective collections of data enabled us to include a larger number of patients than we could by prospectively recruiting patients for this study within a given study period, but we were not able to collect data on potential confounding factors, such as individual level socioeconomic status, dietary patterns, and physical activity. Moreover, there are several sources of potential biases that may have affected our study findings. Inclusion of patients depended on availability of scans. When imaging assessments were performed in health care systems different from where the patients were treated, those scans were sometimes inaccessible. SAT was sometimes underestimated because scans did not cover the entire cross-sectional area of the torso for some patients. Because our focus was on VAT, we included as many scans as possible but excluded SAT and skeletal muscle measurements when cross-sectional areas of both SAT and SM were incomplete. In these cases, only VAT was used. Intravenous contrast administration may influence radiodensity measurements, but information on contrast use was not available in this study. Finally, this was a cross-sectional study design, and we cannot determine causality between body composition and aggressive pathological characteristics. A major strength of this study is its comprehensive characterization of body composition, including patients from minoritized racial and ethnic groups, such as patients from Hispanic, AI, and NHB populations, who were previously underrepresented, despite the heavy burden of kidney cancer, obesity, and obesity-related comorbidities observed in these populations [ 72 ]. In addition to including patients from diverse backgrounds, we aimed to improve the generalizability of our study findings by including patients treated at two institutions from different states and healthcare settings. Unlike many previous studies that restricted analysis to clear cell RCC and single adipose depots using data from a single institution, we jointly evaluated associations of VAT, SAT, IMAT, and skeletal muscle with pathological characteristics in all RCC subtypes using data from two healthcare institutions [ 17 , 21 , 73 ]. We also examined radiodensity to capture both the quantity and quality of abdominal compartments in relation to tumor features. Furthermore, this is the first RCC study to use body composition phenotypes while also incorporating neighborhood-level socioeconomic and clinical factors. This approach enhances the clinical and public health relevance of our findings and helps to position body composition as a potential biomarker of RCC pathological aggressiveness and risk stratification. In conclusion, abdominal adiposity was associated with more aggressive RCC pathology in this study, although sarcopenia and cachexia may have complicated the relationship between adiposity and aggressiveness. Prospective studies in diverse patient populations that incorporate socioeconomic, longitudinal body composition, and/or BMI data, and established RCC risk factors are needed to elucidate how abdominal adiposity and sarcopenia/cachexia jointly influence RCC progression and survival.
