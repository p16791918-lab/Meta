1 Introduction Despite being one of the most preventable and treatable cancers, cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer in women globally as well as in the United States [ 1 ]. According to the American Cancer Society's estimates, in 2026, more than thirteen thousand new cases of cervical cancer will be diagnosed in the US alone and more than four thousand women die from the disease annually [ 2 ]. Disparities exist with regards to geographic distribution, with patients from the Southeastern region of the US experiencing poorer outcomes when compared to individuals from other parts of the nation [ 3 , 4 ]. Over the last few decades, the incidence and mortality rates of cervical cancer have declined due to effective population‐based screening programs and HPV vaccines [ 5 ]. However, a recent study indicated that age‐standardized incidence rates of cervical cancer increased significantly in the 30–39 years early‐onset age group in the most recent time period and not in the older‐onset age groups [ 6 ]. The prevalence of multiple chronic conditions is increasing in the US adult population, specifically in southeast US [ 7 ]. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) defines conditions that last a period of 1 year or longer and which require ongoing medical attention as being chronic in nature (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, arthritis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)) [ 8 ]. More than 129 million American adults have at least 1 chronic condition with more than 68 million adults having 2 or more chronic conditions simultaneously [ 7 ]. The prevalence of chronic conditions is higher among women (28.4%) than men (25.9%) [ 9 , 10 , 11 ]. Various studies have shown that a higher comorbidity count is associated with a lower quality of life and survival among cancer patients [ 12 , 13 , 14 ]. Some of the most widely used scales include the Charleson Comorbidity Index (CCI) and the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index (ECI). These scales have not been used previously with regard to predicting survival outcomes for cervical cancer. While Alabama ranks third in the nation in the incidence and deaths from cervical cancer, it also reports the third highest prevalence of multiple chronic conditions (MCC) in the US, with over 60.1% of adults over 18 years reporting MCCs [ 10 , 15 ]. Prior studies have demonstrated that comorbidities adversely affect survival outcomes across multiple cancer types; however, the extent to which comorbidity burden influences survival among cervical cancer patients remains poorly characterized. Considering the growing presence of comorbidities among adults in the last two decades and their high prevalence in the southeast of the US [ 16 ], our study aims to evaluate the association between comorbidity burden and survival among young and old women with cervical cancer and to compare the prognostic performance of the CCI and ECI. 2 Methods 2.1 Study Design/Population In this retrospective clinical cohort, patients with cervical cancer diagnosed between January 1, 2011 and December 31, 2021, within the University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB) hospital system and the National Cancer Institute (NCI)‐designated O'Neal Comprehensive Cancer Center (CCC) were included. Eligible patients were identified from the CCC tumor registry using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision (ICD9/10) HNC diagnosis codes. Cancer histology was confirmed using International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD‐O‐3) histology codes and review of physician notes in the electronic medical records (EMRs). 2.2 Data Sources Demographic, clinical, and comorbidity data were extracted from the EMR system through the UAB Informatics for Integrating Biology and the Bedside (i2b2) platform which is a NIH‐funded informatics infrastructure. Extracted variables included age at diagnosis, self‐reported race, cancer stage at diagnosis, comorbid conditions, dates of diagnosis and follow‐up encounters, and vital status. All data used for analysis were de‐identified prior to investigator access. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained at UAB for all study procedures (IRB‐300000131). 2.3 Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria Patients with confirmed diagnosis of cervical cancer and complete data on comorbidities, age at diagnosis, cancer stage, and race were included in the study. Patients with missing or incomplete information for these key variables were excluded. Analyses were restricted to patients who self‐identified as non‐Hispanic Black or non‐Hispanic White, as sample sizes for other racial or ethnic groups were insufficient to support the statistical estimates. For the five‐year survival analysis, we included patients diagnosed with cervical cancer between January 1, 2011 and December 31, 2018 to allow adequate follow‐up. For the two‐year survival analysis, patients diagnosed between January 1, 2011 and December 31, 2021 were included. 2.4 Outcome and Predictor Variables The primary outcome of the study was overall 5‐year mortality with a secondary analysis of 2‐year survival. Survival time was calculated from the date of cervical cancer diagnosis to the date of death or last documented clinical encounter. Patients who did not experience death within the specified follow‐up period were censored at 5 or 2 years, respectively. Thus, for five‐year survival, patients who did not experience the primary outcome (i.e., death) within five‐years, their follow‐up window was censored at 5 years. Similarly, for two‐year survival, patients who did not experience the primary outcome within 2 years, their follow‐up window was censored at 2 years. The primary predictors of interest were comorbidity burden as measured by the two indices—the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) and the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index (ECI), calculated using comorbidities and weights based algorithms, as previously validated [ 17 ]. Comorbidity information were identified using ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 diagnosis codes (Tables S1 and S2 ) from 1 year prior to through 1 year post initial diagnosis of cervical cancer. Based on extant literature, CCI scores (1—CCI = 0, 2–1 ≤ CCI ≤ 4, 3—CCI ≥ 5) and ECI scores (1—ECI = 0, 2–1 ≤ ECI ≤ 2, 3—ECI ≥ 3) were categorized into 3 categories. Cancer stage was categorized into three categories based on similarity in the treatment modalities recommended (1‐ Stage I; 2—Stage II and Stage III; 3—Stage IV). Age at diagnosis was categorized as 50 years (early‐onset/young) and ≥ 50 years (late‐onset/old), consistent with NCI definitions. Self‐reported race and cancer stage were included as covariates in all adjusted analyses. 2.5 Statistical Analysis The two comorbid indices, Charlson and Elixhauser, were calculated for cervical cancer patients and a 5‐year (main outcome) and 2‐year overall survival rate was estimated using Kaplan–Meier methods. Two‐year survival analyses were considered based on the number of events (i.e., majority of cancer patients in this clinical cohort died in the first 2 years). Unadjusted Cox proportional hazard models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% CIs for risk of mortality for CCI, ECI, race, stage, and age group (young vs. old). Multivariable Cox proportional hazard models included comorbidity index (CCI or ECI) as the main predictor, adjusting for race, age, and cancer stage at diagnosis. For each comorbidity index, adjusted HR and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Proportional hazard and linearity assumptions were assessed using martingale and deviance residuals [ 18 , 19 ]. Stratified analyses were conducted to compare the cervical cancer survival probability by CCI and ECI status in early and late onset of cervical cancer patients. An alpha threshold of p 0.05 was used to define statistical significance. SAS version 9.4 was used for all data analysis ([ 20 ]. SAS/STAT 15.3 User's Guide. Cary, NC: SAS Institute Inc .). Further, the predictive ability of CCI models vs. ECI models was assessed using Harrel's C‐statistics [ 21 , 22 ]. 3 Results 3.1 Five‐Year Survival Of the 556 eligible cervical cancer patients for five‐year survival analysis, 291 women were 50 years and 265 were ≥ 50 years (Figure 1 ). Both higher scores of CCI and ECI were associated with lower survival and the predictive performances were comparable. These findings underscore the important role of comorbid conditions in influencing cervical cancer outcomes, beyond established prognostic factors such as cancer stage. Studies have shown that comorbidities negatively impact outcomes for cancer patients; however, quantifying this relationship remains challenging [ 13 , 14 , 23 ]. Our study addresses this gap, specifically in the southeast region of US, where comorbidity prevalence is higher than in other regions. Notably, the association between high comorbidity burden and mortality was particularly pronounced among younger women ( 50 years). This finding is clinically meaningful, as younger cervical cancer patients are often expected to have more favorable outcomes. Though this study was novel in terms of the cancer type (cervical) and geographic location, the findings align well with those from earlier studies which focused on different malignancies across populations. Both the indices analyzed have shown strong predictive power of mortality and survival among prostate cancer patients [ 24 ], lung cancer patients [ 25 ], and colorectal cancer patients [ 26 ]. Secondly, we also observed minimal difference in the predictive performance of these two widely used indices. Extent studies do align on to this finding, with these widely used indices being largely comparable [ 27 , 28 ]. Moreover, in instances when studies did report a difference, the effect size was marginal [ 29 , 30 ]. Given their similar performance in this study, either index may be appropriate for risk stratification and adjustment in cervical cancer outcomes research. The findings from our study have some pertinent takeaways for practitioners and providers, as well as for patients living with cervical cancer. Providers may benefit from integrating comorbidity burdens more when tailoring therapies and treatment regimens for cervical cancer patients. Comorbidities should also be considered when outlining the prognoses for patients and could be used to model predictive tools which can predict outcomes like readmissions. At a broader level, comorbidity‐informed risk stratification may also support health system planning, including resource allocation and development of predictive models for outcomes such as readmissions and long‐term survival. Similarly, patients may similarly benefit from increased awareness of their comorbidity status. Many of the comorbidities most routinely found among cervical cancer patients respond well to lifestyle changes. These include conditions like obesity, hypertension, diabetes, etc. among others. Our findings reinforce the association between comorbidity burden and poorer survival and suggest that integrated management of chronic conditions may contribute meaningfully to improved outcomes among cervical cancer survivors. Extent literature has shown that mortality among cervical cancer patients is higher than the national average in the state of Alabama in particular, and the Southeastern US as a whole [ 31 ]. The higher comorbidity burden experienced by patients from this region may help explain a portion of this discrepancy. Though our findings do not show a significant difference between mortality by race, our cohort was limited to patients who could access care consistently at an urban academic hospital. This may potentially underrepresent populations facing greater barriers to access [ 32 ]. We also conducted a sub‐analysis excluding stage IV patients and still observed a significant association between comorbidity status and survival. This excluded the survival bias which could potentially be introduced by higher mortality rates among stage IV patients. Like most studies, our study has several important limitations. Firstly, as a purely quantitative, retrospective study, we could only establish correlations and establishing causal linkages was beyond our scope. Comorbidities were identified within a window extending up to 1 year after cancer diagnosis to account for delays in documentation, which may introduce some misclassification. Second, both the CCI and ECI include solid and metastatic tumors themselves as comorbidities. This can lead to potentially magnifying their predictive potential. However, to limit this issue, we conducted sensitivity analyses excluding these comorbidities and both the indices were still strongly associated with higher rates of mortality among cervical cancer patients (data not shown). Third, cause‐specific mortality and competing risks could not be evaluated due to data limitations, and overall mortality was therefore used. While five‐year survival analyses were restricted to patients with sufficient follow‐up, consistent findings across two‐ and five‐year analyses support the robustness of our results. Fourth, findings from this single‐center study may not be fully generalizable to other regions or care settings. Finally, we acknowledge that the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) was originally developed to predict 1‐year mortality. However, numerous studies have subsequently demonstrated its validity in predicting long‐term survival, including 2‐ and 5‐year mortality, across multiple cancer populations. For example [ 33 ] showed that higher CCI scores were independently associated with markedly worse 5‐year overall survival among patients undergoing surgery for non–small cell lung cancer. Similarly [ 34 ] reported that the age‐adjusted CCI was a strong independent predictor of both short‐ and long‐term outcomes, including 5‐year survival, in patients with gastric cancer. Additional population‐based studies have confirmed the prognostic utility of the CCI and its variants for 5‐year survival in head and neck, gastrointestinal, and other cancer types [ 35 ]. We used the original unweighted Elixhauser Comorbidity Index (ECI) rather than weighted variants such as the van Walraven or AHRQ formulations because existing weighting schemes were developed primarily to predict short‐term or in‐hospital mortality using administrative data and may not generalize to longer‐term cancer survival outcomes, including the 2‐ and 5‐year mortality endpoints examined in this study [ 36 , 37 ]. The unweighted ECI provides a transparent measure of overall comorbidity burden that has demonstrated robust performance across diverse populations and outcomes without reliance on externally derived, outcome‐specific weights [ 17 ]. In addition, because a primary objective of this study was to compare the prognostic performance of the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) and the ECI, using their original formulations ensured conceptual and methodological comparability, allowing differences in predictive performance to reflect structural differences between indices rather than weighting strategies. The strong and consistent associations observed between the unweighted ECI and both short‐ and long‐term mortality support its appropriateness for longitudinal cervical cancer survival analyses. Future studies with larger, multicenter cohorts and longer follow‐up are warranted to confirm these findings and to further examine differences between comorbidity indices. Mixed‐methods approaches may also help identify structural and patient‐level barriers to comorbidity management and cancer care. Such work may inform interventions and policies aimed at improving survival outcomes for women with cervical cancer, particularly in high‐burden regions.
