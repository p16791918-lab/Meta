LEARNING OUTCOMES Upon completing this article, readers will be able to: Describe sex-specific and site-specific associations between occupation and cancer risk identified in a large-scale Japanese case–control study. Identify occupational groups associated with significantly increased or decreased risks of major cancers after adjustment for lifestyle-related factors. Interpret observed occupational cancer gradients in light of socioeconomic context and potential detection-related bias. Occupational factors are well-established contributors to cancer risk and represent an important component of the global burden of disease, with a wide range of workplace-related exposures and conditions implicated across multiple cancer sites. 1 , 2 However, despite this robust evidence for specific exposures, epidemiologic findings based on aggregated or broadly defined occupational categories remain heterogeneous and fragmented. 3 Much of the existing literature has focused on individual agents or high-risk industries, whereas relatively few studies have comprehensively evaluated site-specific cancer risks across a wide spectrum of occupations. 4 Another important limitation of prior research is the insufficient consideration of sex differences and contextual factors in occupational cancer epidemiology. 5 Although biological and exposure-related differences between men and women are increasingly recognized, large-scale analyses stratifying cancer risk by sex across detailed occupational categories and multiple cancer sites remain limited. 6 , 7 Moreover, even within the same detailed occupational category, men and women may experience distinct exposure profiles, work tasks, and psychosocial stressors, leading to differential cancer risk patterns. 8 , 9 In addition, many incidence studies have not adequately integrated the roles of socioeconomic status (SES) and detection-related processes into the interpretation of occupational risk estimates. 10 For cancers with established screening modalities, observed occupational gradients may partly reflect differences in healthcare access and screening uptake rather than differences in underlying disease occurrence, particularly given the higher participation in optional screening among higher-SES and white-collar populations. 11 , 12 Japan provides a distinctive context in which to examine these issues. Historically, the Japanese labor market has been characterized by long-term employment patterns, with many workers remaining in the same occupation for extended periods, thereby accumulating sustained occupational exposures. 6 This employment structure supports the use of the longest-held occupation as a pragmatic proxy for cumulative occupational exposure in epidemiologic studies. Furthermore, Japan’s Industrial Safety and Health Law mandates annual health examinations for employees, embedding health surveillance within the workplace. 13 This system creates occupation-specific patterns of cancer detection; for example, employees of large corporations—often in white-collar occupations—are more likely to undergo comprehensive health checkups that include a wider range of optional or opportunistic cancer screening tests. In contrast, workers in smaller enterprises or blue-collar sectors may rely primarily on statutory examinations with more limited screening content. 14 Notably, the most prominent difference is the frequent inclusion of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing in comprehensive health checkups offered by large corporations, whereas statutory examinations do not include PSA or other cancer-specific screening tests. These features make the Japanese setting particularly informative for examining occupational cancer risk while accounting for lifestyle-related behaviors and detection-related processes. This study aimed to systematically evaluate associations between detailed occupational categories and cancer risk across multiple anatomical sites using data from the Inpatient Clinico-Occupational Survey of the Rosai Hospital Group (ICOD-R). Leveraging this large, multicenter hospital database, we conducted sex-specific analyses to characterize occupational patterns of both overall cancer risk and site-specific cancer risk. By interpreting observed associations in light of behavioral factors, socioeconomic context, and potential detection-related processes, this study seeks to provide a comprehensive, hypothesis-generating epidemiologic assessment of occupational cancer disparities in the Japanese workforce. METHODS Study Design and Setting This multicenter, hospital-based matched case–control study utilized data from the ICOD-R, an ongoing database administered by the Japan Organization of Occupational Health and Safety (JOHAS) since 1984. The ICOD-R systematically collects clinical, occupational, and lifestyle information from inpatients admitted to 34 Rosai Hospitals, which are general hospitals operated by JOHAS and located across Japan. These hospitals serve the general population and are not limited to occupational disease patients. The design, methodology, and findings derived from this database have been described in previous publications, 15 – 21 which have demonstrated associations between occupational factors and various noncommunicable diseases. Clinical data were obtained from physician-completed inpatient discharge summaries for each hospitalization. Attending physicians recorded up to 7 final diagnoses per admission, which were coded using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10). Information on occupational history and lifestyle factors was collected through standardized, interviewer-administered questionnaires conducted by trained personnel at each participating hospital. Completion rates were 100% for clinical data and approximately 75% for occupational history data, with missing information addressed in the statistical analysis. This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient before the completion of the questionnaires until 2015. From 2016, the consent acquisition method was changed to broad consent (opt-out). This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement (see Supplemental Digital Content 1, https://links.lww.com/JOM/C483 ). The authors of this study were granted access to the dataset under a research agreement with JOHAS. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Tokai University School of Medicine, Kanagawa, Japan (protocol number: 24RC046) and of JOHAS (protocol number: BY2024005). Cases and Controls Cases were defined as patients aged 40–74 years who were admitted to participating hospitals between April 1, 2005 (fiscal year 2005) and March 31, 2023 (fiscal year 2022) and received a primary definitive diagnosis of malignant neoplasm, classified using ICD-10 codes C00–C99 (n = 146,994). The age range was selected to ensure sufficient variability in occupational exposure history for analysis. Overall cancer risk (all cancers combined) was evaluated using this definition, and site-specific analyses were conducted for cancers of the lung (C34; n = 14,194), esophagus (C15; n = 3179), stomach (C16; n = 18,808), colorectum (C18–C20; n = 23,256), liver (C22; n = 6561), biliary tract (C23–C24; n = 2423), pancreas (C25; n = 4746), bladder (C67; n = 5803), prostate (C61; n = 16,761), and breast (C50; n = 15,778). To restrict the analysis to incident cancers, patients with a history of malignancy were excluded based on ICD-10 code Z85. For individuals with multiple hospital admissions during the study period in which incident cancer was diagnosed, only the first admission was considered, and such individuals were excluded from eligibility as controls. Controls were randomly selected from patients admitted to the same hospital in the same calendar year and within the same 5-year age strata as the corresponding case. Five-year age strata were used for matching, a standard approach in case–control studies that balances the precision of confounding control with practical feasibility, and is consistent with previous ICOD-R studies. 15 – 21 Eligible controls had no history of cancer and no cancer-related hospitalization during the study period and were admitted for conditions not considered to be associated with occupational exposures in previous ICOD-R-based studies. 18 – 21 Common reasons for hospitalization among controls included infectious and parasitic diseases (A00–B99); diseases of the eye and ear (H00–H95); diseases of the circulatory system (I00–I99); diseases of the digestive system (K00–K95); diseases of the blood and immune system (D50–D89); endocrine, nutritional, and metabolic diseases (E00–E89); diseases of the genitourinary system (N00–N99); diseases of the musculoskeletal system (M00–M99); and factors influencing health status and contact with health services (Z00–Z99). A 1:2 case-to-control matching ratio was adopted, consistent with previous ICOD-R studies, 15 – 21 as a pragmatic balance between statistical efficiency and data availability within the matched strata. In the analysis of all cancers combined, 89.3% of cases were matched to 2 controls, whereas in site-specific cancer analyses, the vast majority of cases were matched at a 1:2 ratio. Exposure Assessment Participants reported their current occupation and up to 3 prior occupations, along with the ages at which each employment period began and ended. Occupational information was coded according to the Japan Standard Occupational Classification (JSOC) issued by the Ministry of Internal Affairs and Communications of Japan. 22 To account for revisions to the JSOC during the study period, the JOHAS developed a unified occupational coding scheme comprising 81 categories (including homemakers, students, unemployed, and unclassifiable occupations) that harmonized classifications across survey years. This unified scheme corresponds broadly to the submajor (2-digit; 43 groups) and minor (3-digit; 130 groups) categories of the International Standard Classification of Occupations 2008 (ISCO-08). For each participant, the longest-held occupation among the reported jobs was used as the primary exposure variable. Analyses focused on detailed occupational categories to capture heterogeneity in work tasks and exposure profiles. Homemakers, students, unemployed individuals, and those with unclassifiable occupations were included in the baseline characteristics to describe the overall hospital population but were excluded from all logistic regression analyses to ensure comparisons were restricted to the active workforce. Detailed descriptions of occupational categories and corresponding case counts for each cancer site are provided in Supplemental Digital Content 2, https://links.lww.com/JOM/C484 . Covariate Information on lifestyle and health status was collected via questionnaires at admission. Smoking status and alcohol use were categorized as never, former, or current. Diagnosis of hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and dyslipidemia was defined as physician-diagnosed disease based on self-report, including current use of medications (yes/no). Obesity was defined as a body mass index of 25 kg/m 2 or higher (yes/no). Shift work in the longest-held occupation was defined as engagement in shift work (yes/no). History of hazardous chemical-handling work was defined as work requiring mandatory special medical examinations for hazardous substances, including organic solvents, specified chemical substances, lead, or dust (yes/no). Statistical Analysis The analytical workflow followed a sequence of steps: case–control identification, matching, and exposure assignment (as detailed above), followed by conditional logistic regression modeling, including multiple imputation for missing covariates, pooling of estimates, and correction for multiple comparisons, as described below. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals for overall cancer (all cancers combined) and site-specific cancers were estimated using conditional logistic regression with multiple imputation. General clerical workers, a single occupational category in the unified JSOC coding scheme, were used as the reference group because they were the most prevalent occupational category and were considered to have relatively low exposure to occupational hazards. Minimally adjusted models consisted of conditional logistic regression accounting for the matching factors, including age category (5-year strata), hospital (34 hospitals), and year of admission. The final multivariable models were additionally adjusted for smoking status, alcohol use, type 2 diabetes, obesity, engagement in shift work in the longest-held occupation, and history of hazardous chemical-handling work. Because approximately 1% of participants had missing data for smoking status or alcohol use, missing values were addressed using multiple imputations by chained equations. 23 Five imputed datasets were generated and combined using the Rubin rules. To account for multiple comparisons, P values were adjusted using the false discovery rate approach based on the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. The significance level was set at α = 0.05, and all P values were 2-sided. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). Overall and Site-specific Cancer Risks by Detailed Occupation Figure 1 presents sex-specific associations between detailed occupational categories and cancer risk among men, including overall cancer risk (all cancers combined) and site-specific cancers. Overall cancer risk provides an integrated measure of cancer susceptibility within each occupation, reflecting the cumulative influence of occupational and lifestyle-related factors that may act predominantly as cancer promoters. 8 For all cancers combined, lower ORs were consistently observed among professional and white-collar occupations, including doctors, dentists, veterinarians, and pharmacists, as well as teachers, whereas higher ORs were more frequently observed among manual, service, and transport-related occupations. These patterns suggest that occupational contexts characterized by physical labor, irregular work schedules, or potential chemical exposures may be associated with an increased overall burden of cancer risk among men. For site-specific cancers, distinct organ-specific patterns were observed, reflecting potential interactions between occupational exposures and tissue-specific carcinogenic processes. Lower risks of lung, esophageal, stomach, and colorectal cancers were consistently observed among professional occupations, whereas elevated risks of lung, colorectal, and liver cancers were commonly observed among workers in sales, food and drink preparation, customer service, motor vehicle driving, construction, civil engineering, metal products, and carrying occupations. Certain associations appeared to be site-specific, such as an elevated risk of biliary tract cancer among wooden products workers and increased bladder cancer risk among architects, civil engineers, surveyors, musicians, and chemical products workers. Notably, prostate cancer risk was lower than that of general clerical workers across many detailed occupational categories, a pattern that may reflect occupational differences in health-seeking behavior and cancer detection rather than differences in underlying disease occurrence. For overall cancer risk (all cancers), variation across occupational categories was less pronounced among women than among men, consistent with a generally weaker association between occupation and total cancer burden. Despite the limited variation in overall cancer risk, several site-specific associations were observed. Electro-mechanic assembly workers exhibited significantly elevated risks of lung, biliary tract, and stomach cancers. Elevated risks of stomach cancer were also observed among office appliance operators, merchandise sales workers, domestic hygiene service workers, apparel products workers, and jewelry products workers. Lower risks of colorectal cancer were observed among teachers, artists, designers, photographers, and film operators; quasi-sales workers; domestic support service workers; care service workers; and agricultural workers. For breast cancer, heterogeneous occupational patterns were observed; notably, public health nurses, midwives, nurses, other health care workers, and care service workers consistently showed significantly lower risks compared with general clerical workers. DISCUSSION This large-scale, multicenter case–control study using the ICOD-R database provides a comprehensive, hypothesis-generating overview of occupational disparities in cancer risk across the Japanese population. By examining both overall cancer risk (all cancers) and site-specific cancer risks across a wide range of detailed occupational categories, this study offers insight into how occupational contexts may differentially influence the cumulative burden of cancer as well as organ-specific carcinogenic processes. Across analyses, we observed clear sex differences in occupational cancer risk patterns. Among men, occupational variation was evident not only for site-specific cancers but also for overall cancer risk, suggesting that certain occupational contexts may be associated with an increased or decreased total burden of cancer. Among women, variation in all cancer risk across occupations was less pronounced, with occupational differences emerging primarily at specific cancer sites. These contrasting patterns underscore the importance of considering sex-specific occupational contexts, work tasks, and related behavioral and detection-related factors when interpreting associations between occupation and cancer risk. One plausible explanation for these sex-specific contrasts is differences in the duration and intensity of occupational exposures. In the present dataset, the median duration of the longest-held occupation was substantially longer among men than among women (32 vs. 21 years), suggesting a greater cumulative opportunity for occupational exposures among men. Prolonged exposure duration may be particularly relevant to overall cancer risk, which reflects the integrated effects of multiple occupational, behavioral, and environmental factors acting over time. Shorter cumulative exposure histories among women, however, may attenuate occupation-related differences in overall cancer burden, even when site-specific associations are present. In addition to exposure duration, occupational exposure profiles often differ by sex even within the same occupational categories. Epidemiologic studies have shown that men are more likely to experience higher levels of physical demands and chemical exposures, whereas women more frequently encounter psychosocial stressors, repetitive tasks, or lower-intensity exposures. 9 , 21 Consistent with these patterns, prior large-scale occupational cohort studies have reported more frequent and broader associations between occupation and cancer risk among men, with fewer and more site-specific associations observed among women. 9 , 24 Together, these considerations provide important context for interpreting why occupational variation extended to overall cancer risk among men but remained largely confined to specific cancer sites among women in the present study. In the analysis of detailed occupational categories, doctors and teachers—both high-SES occupations—consistently demonstrated lower risks across multiple cancer sites, a pattern that contributed to the lower overall cancer risk observed among men in these occupations. These findings are consistent with previous evidence suggesting that higher health literacy and educational attainment are associated with greater adherence to cancer screening and preventive behaviors, which may facilitate earlier detection and reduced cancer risk. 25 Legal workers, another high-SES occupational group, exhibited elevated risks of liver and pancreatic cancers in this study. This divergence suggests that socioeconomic advantage does not uniformly confer protection against cancer risk, pointing to a paradoxical pattern among certain high-SES occupations in which occupation-specific factors may offset the benefits typically associated with higher socioeconomic position. 26 Prior studies have documented that legal professionals experience substantial occupational stress, burnout, and a higher prevalence of substance use, including alcohol misuse, as coping responses to long working hours and high job demands. 27 Although alcohol use was adjusted for in the present study, standard categorical measures are unlikely to fully capture binge drinking patterns or cumulative lifetime exposure, leaving room for residual confounding. 28 Methodological work in occupational epidemiology further indicates that incomplete measurement of complex exposures and behaviors may obscure or attenuate true associations. 29 These findings highlight that cancer risk among high-SES occupations is shaped not only by socioeconomic position, but also by occupation-specific cultures, stress profiles, and health-related behaviors. Prostate cancer exhibited an inverse occupational gradient, with lower risks observed among blue-collar workers, including those in construction and manufacturing, compared with white-collar occupations. This pattern represents a site-specific deviation from the broader occupational patterns observed for overall cancer risk and is unlikely to reflect a true biological protective effect. 27 , 28 Rather, it is more plausibly explained by differences in cancer detection related to screening practices. Prostate cancer incidence is strongly influenced by PSA testing. 30 , 31 In Japan, white-collar workers—particularly those employed by large corporations—are more likely to undergo comprehensive medical checkups (Ningen Dock), in which PSA testing is frequently included, whereas blue-collar workers more commonly rely on statutory health examinations that do not include PSA testing. 32 As a result, asymptomatic and early-stage prostate cancers are more likely to be detected among white-collar workers, inflating observed incidence in these groups. Taken together, the inverse occupational gradient observed for prostate cancer in the present study is best interpreted as a manifestation of detection bias driven by differential screening practices, rather than evidence of reduced biological risk among blue-collar workers. Differences in access to health checkups and healthcare utilization may also influence occupational gradients for other screen-detectable cancers. Among women, we observed site-specific elevations in stomach cancer risk in several detailed occupations, including sales, service, and selected manufacturing jobs. Prior occupational studies likewise suggest that stomach cancer risk can vary across women occupational groups, with excess risks reported in some service- and production-related occupations. 33 Because gastric carcinogenesis is strongly influenced by Helicobacter pylori infection and related precancerous conditions, observed occupational differences may at least partly reflect variation in underlying infection burden, diet, and other correlated social or workplace determinants rather than workplace exposures per se. In addition, the work organization typical of sales and service jobs—such as constrained break times and irregular eating schedules—could plausibly influence gastric symptoms or healthcare-seeking behaviors. However, these pathways cannot be directly evaluated in the present dataset and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. A second notable site-specific finding among women was the lower ORs for breast cancer observed among nurses, despite the long-standing concern that night shift work may increase breast cancer risk. Reviews of the epidemiologic evidence indicate that any excess risk—when observed—tends to be most apparent after prolonged durations of night work (eg, 20–30 years) and that the overall evidence has been judged limited and sensitive to bias and confounding. 34 Consistent with this, a systematic review focusing on nurses reported that higher risks were primarily observed among those with prolonged or intensive rotating night shifts. 35 Importantly, because shift work was explicitly adjusted for in our models, the estimated association for nurses likely reflects other features of nursing work beyond shift schedules. One plausible explanation is higher occupational physical activity—such as prolonged standing, walking, and patient handling—relative to clerical reference occupations; prospective evidence suggests that greater occupational physical activity is associated with a reduced risk of breast cancer. 36 Nonetheless, incomplete measurement of shift intensity and duration, screening behaviors, and other unmeasured correlates of occupation could also contribute to the observed pattern. Strengths and Limitations The major strength of this study lies in the use of the ICOD-R, a large, nationwide, multicenter database with a standardized collection of clinical diagnoses, occupational histories, and key lifestyle factors. The inclusion of over 140,000 incident cancer cases and more than 270,000 matched controls provided sufficient statistical power to evaluate associations across multiple cancer sites and detailed occupational categories, including sex-specific analyses. Occupational information was systematically coded using a unified scheme harmonized across survey years and aligned with international occupational classifications, enhancing internal consistency and comparability. The use of the longest-held occupation offered a pragmatic proxy for cumulative occupational exposure in the Japanese labor context, where long-term employment within a single occupation has historically been common, particularly among male workers. Furthermore, the matched case–control design, combined with conditional logistic regression, adjustment for major lifestyle-related confounders, multiple imputation for missing covariates, and correction for multiple comparisons, strengthened the internal validity of the findings. The explicit consideration of detection-related bias, particularly in the interpretation of prostate cancer results, further supports the robustness of the study’s conclusions. Although Japan’s community-based cancer screening program offers screening for colorectal, gastric, breast, cervical, and lung cancers to all residents regardless of employment status, differential access to employer-based comprehensive health checkups, particularly PSA testing for prostate cancer, may have contributed to detection-related differences across occupational groups. However, because this study is hospital-based and captures cancers diagnosed at the point of inpatient admission, the influence of screening-related detection differences is likely attenuated for most cancer sites. Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, this was a hospital-based case–control study, and although controls were selected from the same hospitals and admission periods as cases, and previous ICOD-R studies have shown that the occupational distribution of controls approximates that of the general population, selection bias, including Berkson’s bias, cannot be entirely excluded. However, sensitivity analyses excluding controls with selected disease categories were conducted to assess potential selection bias, and the results remained materially unchanged. Second, occupational exposure assessment relied on self-reported job histories and the longest-held occupation as a pragmatic proxy for cumulative exposure, which is commonly used in the Japanese labor context. However, this approach does not capture task-level heterogeneity, short-term high-exposure jobs, or cancers with shorter latency. Accordingly, the observed associations should be interpreted as occupation-level patterns rather than agent-specific causal effects. Third, although major lifestyle-related confounders were adjusted for, information on several potentially important factors—including dietary habits, physical activity, H. pylori infection, cancer screening behaviors, and detailed alcohol consumption patterns—was unavailable. In particular, alcohol use was categorized broadly and did not capture cumulative intake, binge drinking patterns, or alcohol use as a stress-coping behavior, which may vary systematically by occupation. Prior research in Japan suggests that occupational stress and alcohol-related behaviors are closely intertwined among workers with liver disease, which could not be fully captured in the present study. 37 Finally, limited statistical power in some female or less common occupational categories may have reduced the ability to detect modest associations and may have resulted in unstable risk estimates. The findings may not be generalized to nonhospitalized populations or to settings with different labor structures and healthcare systems. CONCLUSION In this large, nationwide case–control study, we identified substantial occupational disparities in cancer risk across the Japanese workforce, with pronounced sex differences and distinct patterns for overall and site-specific cancers. Associations were more evident among men and varied by occupational category, socioeconomic position, and cancer site, while several findings—particularly for prostate and breast cancer—highlighted the importance of detection-related and behavioral factors in interpreting occupational gradients. These findings provide a comprehensive, hypothesis-generating epidemiologic framework for understanding occupational cancer disparities in Japan. The observed associations should be viewed as identifying priority areas for further investigation, particularly studies incorporating detailed exposure assessment, cancer screening information, and prospective designs.
