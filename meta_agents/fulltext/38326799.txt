Introduction Non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including diabetes, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and chronic kidney disease, represent a growing global health burden 1 . Despite advances in therapeutic strategies that have improved survival rates, the quality of life among affected individuals often remains severely compromised due to the persistent and progressive nature of these conditions 2 , 3 . Given the difficulty of achieving full cures, early detection and intervention have become pivotal strategies to mitigate disease progression and improve long-term health outcomes 4 – 6 . While early detection is essential, current diagnostic pathways for most NCDs remain largely dependent on hospital-based environments, specialized instruments and trained professionals to ensure testing accuracy and safety. Specifically, blood tests, medical imaging, and biopsies are commonly used in NCDs diagnosis and evaluation. Blood tests and biopsies require venous blood or tissue specimen collection through needle puncture, followed by standardized storage and timely laboratory processing. Imaging-based examinations (e.g., CT, ultrasound) rely on standardized acquisition protocols and expert interpretation, and some may involve radiation exposure. Such dependence increases financial costs and healthcare resource demands, and limits the feasibility of frequent or preventive screening in community settings. As a result, developing accessible, non-invasive approaches that support low-burden disease risk assessment outside clinical environments is necessary. Recent developments in non-invasive biosignal acquisition and machine learning technologies offer promising avenues toward addressing these challenges 7 – 11 . Image-based approaches, including facial and tongue imaging, have been investigated as potential indicators of metabolic and systemic alterations associated with internal organ dysfunctions 12 – 17 . Physiological waveform signals such as pulse waveforms and electrocardiographic (ECG) signals have been used to model cardiovascular and metabolic risk, with prior studies reporting promising performance in detecting diseases such as nephropathy and diabetes 18 , 19 . In addition, wearable monitoring devices 20 have further broadened non-invasive health assessment approaches. These studies indicate that non-invasive physiological signals can reflect disease-related changes and may support disease risk evaluation. Although individual non-invasive approaches show promising potential, many studies (e.g., ECG measurements) still rely on trained personnel to enable standardized acquisition, which can be difficult to implement in regions with limited clinical capacity. In addition, some wearable-based strategies are primarily designed for continuous individual monitoring and may be difficult to operationalize for population-level screening. These considerations motivate non-invasive, self-service acquisition as a practical route to broaden access. However, self-service acquisition introduces technical challenges, including variability in user operation, inconsistent environmental conditions, and the absence of clinical supervision. These factors complicate signal standardization and increase the difficulty of developing robust and generalizable models. Besides, relying on a single physiological signal may fail to capture the heterogeneous manifestations of chronic diseases, resulting in limited predictive performance when applied to complex, systemic diseases 21 – 23 . To address these limitations, multimodal fusion techniques have emerged to integrate complementary information from diverse physiological signals and improve the robustness of disease risk prediction 24 – 27 . Nevertheless, most existing multimodal studies focus on prediction of a single specific disease, with limited exploration of whether such strategies generalize across multiple chronic conditions. Cross-disease risk evaluation requires signals that capture heterogeneous physiological alterations across multiple organ systems. Facial imaging reflects morphological and microvascular characteristics associated with metabolic status 28 , tongue imaging has been linked to systemic and circulatory changes 29 , and pulse waveforms provide dynamic hemodynamic information 30 . Importantly, these modalities are amenable to guided, non-invasive self-acquisition, making them suitable for scalable community deployment. In this study, we present an interactive Multimodal disease risk Evaluation System (iMES) that integrates facial, tongue, and pulse data into a unified prediction framework. Our system is based on a self-service, non-invasive, portable platform that consists of a visual capture device and a pulse capture device. We employ an interactive neural network to integrate these complementary data, and apply a downstream classifier for disease risk assessment. Model interpretability is assessed using Shapley additive explanation (SHAP) analysis, and statistical significance is evaluated through repeated paired comparisons. In a real-world cohort, we demonstrate the feasibility of this approach across several common chronic diseases, providing evidence that multimodal fusion enhances prediction accuracy and robustness. Our goal is not to replace clinical diagnosis, but to complement existing clinical workflows by enabling accessible, prevention-oriented screening in community settings. Methods Design of non-invasive devices iMES is a self-service, non-invasive platform designed for early risk screening of common NCDs. The iMES platform (Fig. 1 a) adopts a compact, movable design, comprising a main body approximately 1 meter in length, 0.75 meter in width, and 1.3 meters in height. It integrates a fixed display screen, a visual capture module, a pulse acquisition module, and a desktop-mounted keyboard and mouse interface. The entire system is mounted on casters, allowing easy relocation and self-service without the need for professional assistance. The visual capture device (Fig. 1 b) is a cabinet-style module equipped with an integrated visible-light camera and embedded illumination system. The semi-enclosed structure partially shields the imaging area from ambient environmental light, reducing the influence of external lighting variation during self-service operation. Two built-in light sources positioned at fixed 45 ∘ angles (upper and lower) ensure consistent and uniform illumination across users. Prior to image acquisition, color correction is performed using the internal reference color chart. During data collection, users are seated and instructed to rest their chin on a designated support within the device. The integrated chin support helps standardize head positioning and camera-to-face distance across users of varying heights. Facial images are captured by a single keystroke operation, requiring users to maintain a neutral expression and steady posture. For tongue imaging, users are asked to extend their tongue fully. Representative examples of the captured facial and tongue images are shown in Fig. 1 c and Fig. 1 d, respectively. Geometry and imaging path of the visual capture device can be found in Supplementary Fig. 1 . The pulse acquisition module (Fig. 1 e) consists of a palm-sized device based on pressure sensors, fixed onto the radial artery of the left wrist using an adjustable elastic band. After setting the device on the left wrist, users can control the capture process independently via keyboard and mouse. The device records continuous pulse waveforms over a 3-minute period while users remain seated and relaxed. While capturing the user’s pulse signal, a signal quality assessment method is applied to evaluate the signal quality in real-time to ensure the reliability of the obtained signal. Only waveform segments that satisfied predefined quality criteria were retained for subsequent analysis. Representative pulse signal acquisition and single waveform extraction steps are illustrated in Fig. 1 f. Detailed components of the pulse capture device can be found in Supplementary Figs. 2 – 4 . A demonstration of the self-service pulse data capture procedure is shown in Supplementary Video 1 . For multimodal data interaction, iMES employs a main-modality guided feature fusion strategy, where facial features serve as the primary modality, supplemented by auxiliary features from tongue and pulse signals. After modality-specific standardization, feature-level fusion is performed based on a canonical correlation analysis (CCA)-based framework to optimize cross-modality interactions 31 . The fused feature representations are subsequently used to train a suitable classifier for disease risk evaluation. The overall system architecture and processing pipeline are depicted in Fig. 1 g. Design of data processing and feature extraction As shown in Fig. 1 g, data are processed and features are extracted from acquired data before they are input into interactive fusion. The processing methods and feature extraction algorithms are different and designed based on the characteristics of the different data modalities. Face image processing and feature extraction Facial images were captured using a SONY 3-CCD video camera flanked by two D65 fluorescent tubes, with the lighting angle fixed at 45° ensuring standardized illumination (see Supplementary Methods 1 ). Before capturing facial images, the device camera is color-calibrated using a standard color reference chart (see Supplementary Fig. 5 ). Each image was stored in bitmap format with a resolution of 640 × 480 pixels. Five 64 × 64 pixel blocks centered around the face were selected for feature extraction (see Supplementary Fig. 6 ). The RGB image was first transformed into the CIELAB color space, and each pixel was classified by comparing its LAB value to six predefined color values (see Supplementary Fig. 7 ) using Euclidean distance. The ratio of each color category over the total pixels was computed in each block, yielding a 30-dimensional color histogram feature vector of five blocks. In parallel, a 2D Gabor filter bank was applied to extract local texture information from each block; both individual texture values and their means were computed, resulting in a 6-dimensional texture feature vector. Altogether, each face image was encoded as an 36-dimensional feature vector (30 color + 6 texture features) combining color and texture representations. Details of the extraction procedures are provided in Supplementary Methods 2 . Tongue image acquisition and processing Tongue images were acquired under the same camera and lighting system used for face images, and saved at a resolution of 640 × 480 pixels. A segmentation algorithm was applied to isolate the tongue region from the background. Eight 64 × 64 pixel blocks on the segmented tongue area were selected (see Supplementary Fig. 8 ), and similar to facial processing, LAB color transformation and Euclidean comparison against 12 predefined tongue-color categories were used to derive color features (see Supplementary Figs. 9 – 10 ). Since the majority of the tongue area is red, the ratio of each color category is calculated only based on the whole tongue rather than blocks, resulting in 12-dimensional tongue color features. Texture features were extracted from the eight blocks using 2D Gabor filters, and averaged to obtain a 9-dimensional texture representation. Additionally, 13 geometric features capturing tongue shape and contour were computed, including width, length, length-width ratio, center offset, and normalized areas derived from inscribed shapes. The final tongue feature vector comprised 34 dimensions (12 color + 9 texture + 13 geometric features). Details of the feature definitions and processing are provided in Supplementary Methods 3 . Pulse signal acquisition and processing Pulse signals were recorded continuously for three minutes using a tri-sensor acquisition system, consisting of a semiconductor strain gauge for dynamic pulse wave detection ( a c dynamic ), a foil strain gauge for static contact pressure ( d c static ), and a pressure feedback sensor for real-time monitoring and control 18 . Signals were digitized at a sampling rate of 500Hz using a 12-bit analog-to-digital converter. A two-stage signal quality assessment framework was implemented to detect amplitude-related artifacts and morphological irregularities including baseline drift and inconsistent cycle periodicity. Only high-quality segments that passed all quality criteria were retained for analysis and were subsequently segmented into individual cardiac cycles. A comprehensive set of quantitative features was extracted. Temporal descriptors included peak-to-peak amplitude, pulse width at half-maximum, rise and decay durations, and waveform entropy. Frequency-domain features were derived from power spectral density estimates and encompassed the dominant frequency, spectral centroid, and relative energy distribution across physiologically relevant frequency bands. Morphological features captured structural characteristics of the pulse waveform, such as the timing and distribution of inflection points, as well as clinically relevant indices including the augmentation index and stiffness index. All features were standardized across the dataset to ensure comparability prior to model training. Details of the signal quality criteria, and feature extraction procedures are provided in Supplementary Methods 4 and Methods 5 . After processing and extracting features from unimodal data, sex and age information are concatenated with modality features before being passed to the classifiers. This aims to provide real-world distributions of age and sex to help improve the model’s performance. In practice, the sex feature is coded by “0” (female), “2” (male), and “1” (unknown). The age value is directly concatenated if provided or marked as “0” if unknown. SCN offers a computationally efficient and theoretically grounded approach to model learning, particularly suited for small to medium-sized datasets. Its non-iterative nature and adaptive capacity also render it promising for deployment in embedded and portable screening systems. Benchmark models To investigate the discriminative power of multimodal fused features, several classifiers are applied to evaluate the risk evaluation performance across nine disease evaluation tasks. The classifiers included logistic regression (LR) 36 , support vector machine (SVM) 37 , k-nearest neighbors (KNN) 38 , random forest (RF) 39 , and multilayer perceptron (MLP) 40 , covering both linear and non-linear decision boundaries as well as distance-based and ensemble-based paradigms. Feature importance analysis To interpret the contribution of individual features to model predictions, we applied SHAP to the output of the SCN classifier, using the predicted probability of the disease class as the target. SHAP values were computed on the test set using the KernelExplainer. The input features to the classifier consisted of 50-dimensional vectors from the “Tongue Face” fusion branch, two demographic variables (sex and age), 36 raw facial features, and 50-dimensional vectors from the “Pulse Face” fusion branch, resulting in a total of 138 input dimensions. Each SHAP value quantifies the marginal contribution of a feature to the prediction outcome. The top 25 features with the highest mean absolute SHAP values were selected for visualization. Dataset characteristics This study utilized a dataset comprising 2,423 participants, including both healthy individuals (n = 338) and participants diagnosed with NCDs. The disease groups included diabetes ( n = 403), fatty liver disease (n = 320), dyslipidemia (n = 269), and other 6 diseases. Each participant was assigned a single disease label by licensed physicians based on a combination of clinical assessment, biochemical indicators, and diagnostic imaging. Data were collected between January 2014 and March 2015 at Guangdong Provincial Hospital of Chinese Medicine in Guangzhou, China. All diagnoses were assigned based on standardized clinical guidelines and biochemical criteria that remain consistent with current diagnostic practices for non-communicable diseases. Implementation details and parameter settings All model training, evaluation, and feature processing were implemented in Python 3.10.9 using the PyTorch (v2.2.2) Numpy (v1.24.2), and scikit-learn (v1.3.0) libraries. The experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3050 GPU (8 GB memory), a 12th Gen Intel Core i7-12700 CPU, and 32 GB RAM running Windows 11. CUDA version 11.7 was used to enable GPU acceleration for deep learning modules. The dataset was partitioned into training and testing subsets using a fixed 70:30 split, stratified across disease-positive and healthy samples for each classification task. To assess robustness and reproducibility, we repeated the stratified splitting procedure 10 times using paired repetitions, and all compared models within each repetition shared the identical training/testing split to ensure fair comparison. For feature preprocessing, each modality’s features were normalized using parameters computed from the training set and then applied to the test set. This ensured consistent scaling across modalities while preventing information leakage. Multimodal fusion was performed using the proposed interactive CCA module. The fusion training process was terminated once the improvement in canonical correlation loss dropped below 0.0001, which is a sign of model convergence. The resulting fused features of all fusing epochs were then passed to the classification models for downstream disease prediction. For all classifiers, hyperparameters were tuned via grid search over predefined search spaces (see Supplementary Methods 6 and Supplementary Table 1 ). For each task, the hyperparameter configuration with the highest accuracy was selected as the final setting of the classifier in this task. The overall performance of the classifiers was evaluated and compared by their average test performance across all nine tasks. Among the classifiers, MLP model was trained using early stopping based on validation loss, while other models used default training settings without early stopping. Characteristics of samples and diseases All participants were evaluated by licensed clinicians from Guangdong Provincial Hospital of Chinese Medicine in Guangzhou, China. Each participant was assigned a single disease label by physicians based on individuals’ medical histories and comprehensive clinical evaluations, including physical examination, biochemical assays, imaging, and standardized diagnostic criteria. Only one disease diagnosis was assigned per individual to ensure mutually exclusive class labeling for training clarity. The following nine disease categories were used as labels for model training and evaluation: Diabetes All cases in this category are type 2 diabetes mellitus. Diagnosis was established if any of the following criteria were met: (1) fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥7.0 mmol/L; (2) 2-hour plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L during a 75g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT); or (3) glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥6.5%. Fatty liver All cases in this category are non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. This disease was identified via imaging modalities that indicates hepatic steatosis affecting more than 5% of hepatocytes, excluding significant alcohol intake or other causes. Chronic kidney disease Defined by an estimated glomerule filtration rate (eGFR) 60mL/min/1.73m 2 , or markers of kidney damage for at least three months. Hyperlipidemia Hyperlipidemia refers to elevated serum lipid levels, particularly low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) ≥4.1 mmol/L and triglycerides ≥2.3 mmol/L. In this study, hyperlipidemia was considered a distinct category from dyslipidemia, and was typically diagnosed when either LDL-C or triglycerides exceeded reference thresholds without major changes in HDL-C. Hypertension Diagnosed by systolic blood pressure ≥140mmHg or diastolic pressure ≥90mmHg on repeated clinic visits. Arrhythmia Diagnosed via ECG, with atrial fibrillation indicated by absence of P waves and irregular R-R intervals; 24-hour Holter monitoring used for intermittent cases. Hyperuricemia Defined as a serum uric acid level 420 μ mol/L in males or 360 μ mol/L in females, measured in a fasting state. Dyslipidemia Defined by total cholesterol ≥6.2 mmol/L, LDL-C ≥4.1 mmol/L, triglycerides ≥2.3 mmol/L, or HDL-C 1.0 mmol/L (males) / 1.3 mmol/L (females). Lung cancer Diagnosis was based on imaging modalities (e.g., low-dose CT) and pathological biopsy, with staging classification performed with TNM system. Statistics and Reproducibility For evaluating the performance of our disease risk evaluation system, we utilized a standard train-test splitting strategy. Each dataset was split into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%). This split ensures that the model is trained on a substantial portion of the data while maintaining a separate dataset for fair evaluation. This splitting procedure was repeated 10 times with different random seeds. For each repetition, all compared models were trained and evaluated using exactly the same training and test sets to ensure a fair comparison. No data were excluded from the analyses. Model performance was primarily evaluated using classification accuracy, which served as the key metric for hyperparameter selection, fusion dimension optimization, and performance benchmarking across tasks. Additional metrics including precision and recall were also reported to provide a comprehensive view of classifier behaviors. For comparisons between unimodal, concatenated, and fused feature representations, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the corresponding area under the curve (AUC) were used as evaluation metrics. All experiments were conducted with fixed random seeds to ensure reproducibility. Each of the nine disease-specific classification tasks was treated as an independent experimental unit, and model hyperparameters were selected individually for each task using testing performance (see Supplementary Table 1 ). This design enables fair and robustness evaluations without additional repeated runs, and demonstrates the stability of the proposed fusion and classification pipeline across diverse tasks. Statistical comparisons between models were conducted using paired two-sided t -tests on repetition-level mean performance metrics. The paired design ensured that performance differences were assessed under identical data splits. A significance level of α = 0.05 was adopted. Where appropriate, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were additionally performed to confirm robustness against potential deviations from normality. SHAP values were computed to evaluate feature contributions across modalities in different classification tasks. For demographic feature ablation analysis, paired statistical tests were conducted to assess performance differences between models with and without age and sex features. Results Comparison between unimodal and multimodal fusion models To evaluate the effectiveness of our interactive multimodal fusion strategy, we compared the classification performance of unimodal and fused models under different primary modality settings, respectively. For each of the three input modalities (facial images, tongue images, and pulse signals), we constructed a single-modality baseline model using the SCN classifier, and compared it with a fusion model where the respective modality served as the main modality in the fusion process. As illustrated in Fig. 2 c, multimodal fusion improves classification performance across most modalities. When facial features served as the main modality, the average accuracy improved from 89.25% to 92.64% after fusion (paired tests, p 0.001), representing a 3.39% improvement. Tongue-based models exhibited a similar improvement, with accuracy increasing from 84.33% to 86.85%, corresponding to a 2.52% gain ( p 0.001). Pulse-guided fusion resulted in a marginal change, with accuracy increasing from 77.44% to 77.49%, which is only a 0.05% difference and did not reach statistical significance ( p = 0.48). Although the mean accuracy difference of the pulse-guided fusion model is not statistically significant, the fusion model exhibited reduced variance across repetitions. These results suggest that while facial features alone yielded the highest unimodal accuracy, fusion significantly improved performance even for strong primary modalities. Furthermore, tongue and pulse modalities also benefited from the complementary information introduced through fusion, reducing inter-task performance variance and boosting overall discriminative power. These results demonstrate the effectiveness provided by adding additional modalities through the fusion algorithm. Overall, the observed gains confirm that interacting complementary signals from multiple physiological sources substantially enhances classification robustness and generalization, particularly in real-world settings where individual modalities may be noisy or incomplete. Impact of output dimension on model performance To investigate the effect of fused feature dimensionality on model performance, we evaluated the average classification accuracy, precision, and recall across nine disease tasks under four diverse output dimension settings (20, 50, 100, and 200). These output dimensions correspond to the length of the fused feature vectors generated by the multimodal fusion module. As shown in Fig. 2 d, performance improves from an output dimension of 20 to 50 and remains relatively stable at higher dimensions. Across 10 repeated experiments, the model achieves its highest average accuracy 92.64% ( ± 0.47%) at dimension 50 and 92.61% ( ± 0.42%) at dimension 100. Precision reaches 92.87% ( ± 0.67%) at dimension 50 and slightly increases to 93.01% ( ± 0.55%) at dimension 100, while recall achieves its highest mean value of 95.00% ( ± 0.71%) at dimension 50. While dimension of 200 yields comparable accuracy of 92.19% ( ± 0.38%), it does not offer further improvements and slightly reduces recall. In contrast, the lowest dimension 20 results in a modest decrease in accuracy of 91.93% ( ± 0.48%) with precision of 92.31% ( ± 0.61%) and recall of 94.40% ( ± 0.75%). These results suggest that an output dimension of 50 provides an optimal balance between feature expressiveness and model efficiency. Notably, model performance remains relatively stable across a wide range of dimensions, indicating that the fusion representation is robust to dimensionality fluctuations as long as the dimension is large enough to integrate multimodal information. This stability facilitates deployment flexibility, allowing developers to trade off between computational cost and classification performance based on application needs. More performance comparison of output dimensions can be found in Supplementary Table 2 – 5 . Performance of different classifiers after multimodal fusion To verify the robustness and generalization ability of the multimodal fusion features, we evaluated multiple classifiers trained on the fused feature set. Specifically, LR 36 , SVM 37 , KNN 38 , RF 39 , MLP 40 , and SCN 33 are evaluated in terms of classification accuracy, precision, and recall across nine disease evaluation tasks in 10 repeated experiments. All classifiers were optimized via grid search over their respective hyperparameter spaces to ensure a fair and unbiased performance comparison on the same training and testing datasets. As shown in Fig. 2 e, SCN consistently achieves the highest average classification, achieving a mean accuracy of 92.64% ( ± 0.47%), a mean precision of 92.87% ( ± 0.71%), and a mean recall of 95% ( ± 0.74%). SVM and MLP followed, with mean accuracies of 92.01% ( ± 0.43%) and 91.32% ( ± 0.65%), respectively. Logistic regression and random forest achieved mean accuracies of 90.63% ( ± 0.76%) and 90.87% ( ± 0.63%), while KNN showed comparatively lower performance (88.35% ± 0.68%). Notably, all classifiers achieved mean accuracies exceeding 88%, indicating that the fused feature representations are generally discriminative and well-suited for multi-disease classification tasks. More details of baseline metrics comparisons can be found in Supplementary Table 2 – 5 , and the comparison of SCN with SOTA classifier can be found in Supplementary Table 6 . Among all classifiers, SCN consistently demonstrates superior predictive capability across all nine disease categories, suggesting strong generalization of the fused representations. Paired statistical tests conducted on repetition-level mean accuracies confirmed that SCN significantly outperformed all baseline classifiers ( p 0.01). In addition to predictive performance, SCN offers practical advantages crucial for real-world deployment, including rapid training, fast inference, and a compact model size, owing to its incremental supervised random configuration mechanism that eliminates the need for gradient-based backpropagation 41 . These characteristics render SCN particularly suitable for integration into lightweight, self-service diagnostic devices. Based on its superior predictive accuracy, efficiency, and hardware compatibility, SCN was selected as the final classification model for all subsequent analyses. Discussion This work demonstrates that body-surface multimodal signals, including pulse waveforms, facial images, and tongue images, can be integrated through an interactive fusion framework for scalable disease risk assessment. Unlike conventional approaches that rely on unimodal features or clinician-guided acquisition, iMES captures physiologically meaningful signals through a self-service, non-invasive platform, achieving accurate and interpretable risk evaluation across diverse chronic conditions. With appropriate validation, such tools could support preventive care, particularly where professional resources are limited. A key insight from this study is that physiological and morphological signals convey complementary diagnostic cues. Their predictive strength varies by disease: facial images contributed significantly to diabetes detection, likely due to the known correlation between facial features and insulin resistance 47 , while tongue images were particularly effective for liver-related conditions, consistent with traditional medical knowledge 48 . By aligning modalities in a shared latent space, iMES captures cross-modal interactions not accessible through unimodal analysis alone. The success of our fusion framework highlights the importance of physiologically informed modality design 49 . Among the three modalities, facial features showed the strongest individual predictive ability, and adding pulse and tongue data further improved classification by providing complementary information. SHAP 43 analysis shows that fusion-derived features play an important role in model predictions, supporting the effectiveness of cross-modal integration. The self-service design of iMES removes the need for trained professionals during data acquisition but also introduces considerations for signal quality control. The platform employs controlled illumination and color calibration to ensure stable imaging conditions, though ambient light in uncontrolled environments may still introduce some variability. On-screen instructions and automated quality checks are provided to guide user operation and detect acquisition errors. Routine sanitation of shared surfaces using disposable or replaceable chin-rest pads and periodic sensor calibration by maintenance personnel are also required. These practical factors should be considered when evaluating long-term deployment. This study has several limitations that should be considered to handle clinical realism. The data were collected at a single hospital over a limited time period, and external validation on multi-site cohorts covering diverse populations, skin tones, and age groups is needed to confirm generalizability. In addition, the current evaluation focused on single-disease classification for each participant. Although the classifiers are not mutually exclusive and can in principle operate independently for each condition, further research under multi-label situations are necessary to deal with comorbidities challenge. Furthermore, our healthy and diseased groups exhibit different age distributions. Though this is common in chronic disease research, additional studies are needed to better understand how age-related factors contribute to model predictions. Finally, while the moderate class imbalance in our cohort was preserved to reflect real-world screening scenarios, developing alternative strategies such as class-weighted objectives or calibrated decision thresholds may be beneficial for addressing more severe class imbalances cases. In summary, iMES integrates facial, tongue, and pulse signals through interactive multimodal fusion for non-invasive disease risk evaluation across nine common NCDs. Cross-modal interaction learning improves performance over unimodal baselines, producing interpretable fused representations across disease categories. Though current study has certain practical limitations to be implemented on scalable community, the experimental results support the effectiveness of the proposed framework for scalable disease risk stratification. As healthcare systems increasingly focus on disease risk evaluation and prevention, self-service, non-invasive platforms like iMES could become a useful component of community-based health infrastructure.
