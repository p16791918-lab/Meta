## Introduction

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer and a leading cause of cancer death among women in the United States.¹⁻² Incidence and mortality vary across racial and ethnic groups, and these differences have been documented over several decades.¹ Non-Hispanic White women have historically been reported to have among the highest overall invasive breast cancer incidence rates, while non-Hispanic Black women have higher mortality, a younger average age at diagnosis, and a larger share of triple-negative tumors.³⁻⁶ These patterns have been attributed to differences in subtype distribution, stage at presentation, screening, and access to care.⁴⁻⁶ Incidence estimates by race and ethnicity are used for cancer surveillance, etiologic research, and the planning of prevention and early-detection programs, and are usually reported for a small number of broad racial and ethnic categories.

Standard racial and ethnic categories are broad, and a number of studies have noted that aggregate groupings can mask variation within them.⁷⁻⁸ The umbrella term "Asian American, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander" (AANHPI) covers populations whose reported incidence rates differ severalfold, from relatively low rates in some Southeast Asian groups to rates closer to those of White women in others.⁷,⁹⁻¹³ Reported rates for Hispanic/Latina women also differ by country of origin.⁸,¹⁴⁻¹⁵ For American Indian and Alaska Native (AI/AN) women, incidence has been described as regionally variable, and rates from registries that rely on observer-assigned race have been noted to be affected by racial misclassification; studies using linkage to Indian Health Service records or dedicated tribal registries have reported higher rates than unlinked sources.¹⁶⁻¹⁸ Receptor-defined molecular subtypes are also distributed differently across groups, and the reported patterns for triple-negative disease do not necessarily follow the ordering seen for overall incidence.⁴,⁶,¹⁹⁻²⁰

Disaggregated incidence estimates are reported across a range of sources. National cancer statistics reports and individual registry-based studies have described rates for specific subgroups, but they draw on different cancer registries (the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results [SEER] program, the National Program of Cancer Registries, IHS-linked and state or regional registries), cover different time periods and age-standardization schemes, and use varying White comparator definitions.²,²¹ Because many of these studies rely on overlapping underlying data, estimates from the same source are not statistically independent, which is relevant when they are pooled. To our knowledge, these disaggregated estimates have not been brought together in a single systematic review with screening for overlap, risk-of-bias appraisal, and certainty grading. A systematic review is suited to assembling such scattered estimates under standardized extraction and appraisal.

We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of racial and ethnic differences in invasive breast cancer incidence among women in the United States, expressed as incidence rate ratios relative to non-Hispanic White women. We focused on disaggregated AANHPI and Hispanic-origin subgroups, AI/AN populations by region, and major receptor-defined molecular subtypes. To limit the influence of overlapping data, we selected one representative estimate per registry family for the main analysis, assessed risk of bias with an adapted Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, and graded certainty within a prespecified GRADE-informed framework, with the aim of providing a disaggregated summary of the available evidence.
