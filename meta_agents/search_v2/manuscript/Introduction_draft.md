## Introduction

Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer and a leading cause of cancer death among women in the United States.¹⁻² Its burden is not shared evenly across the population: incidence and mortality differ substantially by race and ethnicity, and these differences have persisted for decades.¹ Non-Hispanic White women have historically had among the highest overall invasive breast cancer incidence rates, whereas non-Hispanic Black women experience higher mortality, an earlier average age at diagnosis, and a disproportionate burden of aggressive tumor phenotypes—most notably triple-negative breast cancer.³⁻⁶ Because these disparities reflect differences in tumor biology, stage at presentation, screening, and access to care, accurate incidence estimates by race and ethnicity are foundational to surveillance, etiologic research, and the equitable planning of prevention and early-detection programs.⁴⁻⁶

A central limitation of the standard racial and ethnic classification, however, is that broad aggregate categories conceal substantial within-group heterogeneity.⁷⁻⁸ The umbrella term "Asian American, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander" (AANHPI) spans populations whose incidence rates differ several-fold, from very low rates in some Southeast Asian groups to rates approaching or exceeding those of White women in others; a single aggregate rate is therefore uninformative and can obscure both high- and low-risk subgroups.⁷,⁹⁻¹³ Hispanic/Latina women likewise differ in incidence by country of origin, so that pooled "Hispanic" rates average across distinct populations.⁸,¹⁴⁻¹⁵ American Indian and Alaska Native (AI/AN) incidence is regionally heterogeneous and has historically been undercounted in registries that rely on self-reported or observer-assigned race; accurate rates require linkage to Indian Health Service enrollment or dedicated tribal registries.¹⁶⁻¹⁸ Overlaid on all of these is a subtype axis—receptor-defined molecular subtypes are unevenly distributed across groups, and disparities in triple-negative disease in particular do not track the overall-incidence ordering.⁴,⁶,¹⁹⁻²⁰

Despite an extensive primary literature, disaggregated incidence estimates remain fragmented. They are scattered across individual cancer registries (the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results [SEER] program, the National Program of Cancer Registries, IHS-linked and state or regional registries), across non-overlapping time periods and age-standardization schemes, and across studies that use different White comparator definitions.²,²¹ Many registry-based estimates draw on the same underlying SEER or NPCR data, so that naïve pooling would double-count non-independent observations rather than add information. To our knowledge, these disaggregated estimates have not been systematically assembled, screened for overlap, appraised for risk of bias, and graded for certainty within a single synthesis.

We therefore conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of racial and ethnic differences in invasive breast cancer incidence among women in the United States, expressed as incidence rate ratios relative to non-Hispanic White women. We gave particular attention to disaggregated AANHPI and Hispanic-origin subgroups and to AI/AN populations by region, and to major receptor-defined molecular subtypes, selecting one representative estimate per registry family to preserve independence, assessing risk of bias with an adapted Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, and grading certainty within a prespecified GRADE-informed framework. By standardizing extraction, provenance tracking, and appraisal across a heterogeneous evidence base, we aimed to provide a transparent, disaggregated synthesis to inform surveillance and equitable breast cancer control.
