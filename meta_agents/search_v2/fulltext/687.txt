TITLE: Urbanization and breast cancer incidence in North Carolina, 1995-1999

Urbanization and Breast Cancer Incidence in North Carolina, 1995–1999
SUSAN A. HALL, PHD, JAY S. KAUFMAN, PHD, ROBERT C. MILLIKAN, PHD,
THOMAS C. RICKETTS, PHD, DALE HERMAN, MSPH, AND DAVID A. SAVITZ, PHD
PURPOSE: Breast cancer incidence rates are reported to be higher in urban compared with rural areas in
the United States. We investigated the relationship between urbanization and breast cancer in North
Carolina (1995–1999), and considered hospital characteristics as an explanation.
METHODS: We calculated age-adjusted in situ and invasive female breast cancer incidence rates stratiﬁed
by race, urbanization (Urban Inﬂuence Codes), and the presence of a hospital with a cancer registry and
cancer program approval in a county.
RESULTS: For white women, incidence rate ratios (IRRs) comparing the most urban with the most rural
counties were 1.60 for in situ and 1.18 for invasive cancer. For non-white women, IRRs were 1.27 and 0.99,
respectively. IRRs for incidence in registry hospital counties versus those without were all O 1.00 and
differences were greater for in situ cancer than invasive. For most strata, urban excesses were attenuated
when further stratiﬁed by registry hospital status.
CONCLUSIONS: For most strata, we observed excess incidence in urban counties, but it appeared to be
explained through the urban preponderance of registry hospitals. Counties with these hospitals may have
higher incidence because of increased detection. Area hospital characteristics should be considered when
evaluating geographic patterns of breast cancer incidence.

INTRODUCTION
Female breast cancer incidence rates have been reported to
be higher in urban areas compared with rural areas in the
United States since 1955. Past studies have found urban ele-
vations with relative risks ranging from 1.06 to 1.83 (1–16).
Only one estimate showed higher rates in rural areas, among
African-Americans only (6). The urban excess has also been
observed among males with breast cancer (17) and among
females in Canada (18), European countries (17, 19–24),
Costa Rica (25), and Japan (17).
Higher rates in urban areas such as Long Island, New
York, have been used as a justiﬁcation for studying
environmental pollution as a cause of breast cancer (26).
Higher urban incidence may be the result of exposures,
behaviors, or reproductive patterns more common in urban
areas that elevate risk. However, higher rates in urban areas
could arise from differential ascertainment due to higher
rates of detection in urban areas, and consequently, higher
reported incidence.
Quality of reporting has been considered as an explana-
tion for the urban excess of breast cancer in six studies (1–3,
10–12). Most found that quality in rural areas was poorer by
their indicator (percent of incident cases found by review of
death certiﬁcates) (1–3, 10, 12). One study noted a potential
reporting bias by ‘‘registry hospital,’’ noting that larger
hospitals reported more completely, but did not conclude
that this bias explained the urban excess (5). In a 1995 study,
American hospitals with the largest cancer caseloads were
found to more completely report their cases to the
Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Pro-
gram than hospitals with smaller cancer caseloads. Also, the
percentage of cases missed and unreported to SEER was
lower for hospital cancer registries compared with case-
ﬁnding by the central cancer registry for the SEER region
(27).
In North Carolina, a greater proportion of urban than
rural hospitals are certiﬁed by the American College of
Surgeons’ (ACOS) Commission on Cancer as ‘‘Approved
Cancer Programs.’’ Such hospitals are required to have an
in-house cancer registry (28). Our study was undertaken to
investigate whether breast cancer incidence rates in urban

MATERIALS AND METHODS
We conducted an ecologic study of female breast cancer
incidence in the state of North Carolina, using county as the
unit of analysis. Our study was approved by the Institutional
Review Board of the School of Public Health at the
University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. We used
incident case count data supplied by the North Carolina
Central Cancer Registry; incident cases were North
Carolina resident women with a ﬁrst case of invasive or in
situ breast cancer diagnosed between January 1995 and
December 1999. For the purposes of this study, breast cancer
incidence always refers to detected cases of breast cancer, not
the true incidence of all cases.
Cancer registry data included age, race, county of
patient’s residence at diagnosis, year of diagnosis, and stage
at diagnosis. There were 28,830 cases of breast cancer over
the study years. We excluded 841 (2.9%) women with
missing data on race (75), stage at diagnosis, (777), or both
(11), leaving 27,989 cases for analysis. We stratiﬁed on race
as the incidence of breast cancer is known to vary by race
(30). Race was either self-reported or health-care-provider
recorded; collection methods varied by county and facility.
We used yearly population estimates provided by the State
Demographer in order to calculate race and stage-speciﬁc
incidence rates, averaged over the 5-year period. Rates were
age-adjusted by the direct method to the year 2000 U.S.
Standard Population and expressed per 100,000 women of
the same race. Races were grouped as white and non-white,
as reliable population denominators from the U.S. Census
for smaller racial groups (in our data, American Indians and
Asians) were not considered by the National Program of
Cancer Registries to be reliable (31). We combined these
groups with non-whites so that they could still contribute to
the analysis although they accounted for less than 1% of all
cases. Stage-speciﬁc rates were calculated as in situ and
invasive (deﬁned as localized, regional, and distant stage
combined).
Urbanization level was measured using the county’s 1993
Urban Inﬂuence Code (UIC) based on the 1990 U.S.
Census. This nine-level scale deﬁnes non-Metropolitan
counties by their adjacency or non-adjacency to a Metro-
politan Area, and the size of the largest communities within
them (29). This scale is particularly useful for health
research as the size of the largest town often deﬁnes whether
hospitals are located within them, and adjacency may
inﬂuence access to care (32). We used the full scale and
a scale grouped as follows: 1–2 Z Metropolitan Area;
3–6 Z Non-Metropolitan, adjacent to a Metropolitan
Area; and 7–9 Z Non-Metropolitan, non-adjacent to a
Metropolitan Area. Our rationale for this grouping was the
assumption that among non-Metropolitan counties, non-
adjacency to a Metropolitan Area may represent reduced
availability of health care services for residents in these areas
compared with non-Metropolitan, adjacent areas. The UIC
deﬁnition takes into account Metropolitan Areas in
adjacent states in their assignment of metropolitan status.
Rates were calculated for the entire state and for counties
grouped by urbanization level. Incidence rate ratios (IRRs)
were calculated comparing the most urban to the most rural
group, and the most urban to the second most rural group.
Because our study uses reported incidence and is not based
on sampling of the NC population in the speciﬁed period,
every reported case is (theoretically) captured. Thus, we
did not calculate conﬁdence intervals (which represent
sampling variability) for our rates or proportions (33).
We also considered incidence rates by hospital type
(having a hospital-based registry, or not), race, stage and
grouped UIC. The presence of a hospital-based registry in
a county was determined by whether or not any hospital(s)
in a county was approved by the ACOS’ Commission on
Cancer in 2002 (28). Such hospitals were designated
‘‘registry hospitals’’ in this analysis, and each North Carolina
county (N Z 100) was assigned a yes/no variable indicating
its presence. A county map was created that included the
simultaneous geographic distribution of urbanization level
(grouped UIC) and registry hospital status for each county
(34). IRRs and incidence rate differences between registry
and non-registry counties were calculated. We used the non-
parametric Wilcoxon’s rank sum to test the null hypothesis
that registry and non-registry rates were a random partition
of the same underlying distribution of rates.

Registry Hospital Analysis
Registry hospitals were more likely to be located in
Metropolitan counties (Fig. 1). As of 2002, there were 31
ACOS certiﬁed registry hospitals in North Carolina, and 21
(67.7%) were Metropolitan in location. Eight (25.8%) of
the registry hospitals were in non-Metropolitan, adjacent
counties, and only two (6.5%) were in the most rural
counties.
We observed higher in situ breast cancer incidence rates
in counties with registry hospitals compared with counties
without registry hospitals (Table 2). IRRs were 1.48
(comparing registry to non-registry county) for whites and
1.53 for non-whites, considering all of North Carolina.
Patterns were similar for invasive breast cancer, although
differences were of smaller magnitude than for in situ disease
(IRRs Z 1.25 for whites and 1.18 for non-whites). For both
in situ and invasive incidence in both racial groups, the
excess persisted in analyses stratiﬁed by urbanization level
(UIC groups).

DISCUSSION
We conducted an ecologic study to determine if an urban
excess of breast cancer incidence was present in North
Carolina for our study years, and, if so, to consider the role of
hospital characteristics as a potential explanation. We
found that greater urbanization, as represented by the
grouped UIC, was related to higher in situ breast cancer
incidence in white and non-white women and higher
invasive incidence among white women but not non-white.

Both invasive and in situ breast cancer incidence rates for
both races were elevated among counties with registry
hospitals, and with the exception of in situ disease among
whites, urban elevations in rates were attenuated within
strata deﬁned by the presence of registry hospitals.
We are aware of no prior studies that consider breast
cancer incidence by hospital characteristics. Variation in
breast cancer incidence by urbanization level has not been
previously studied in North Carolina, a state with a relatively
large rural population (ranked sixth in the nation by the
Census for proportion rural in 1990) (35). Prior ecologic
studies of cancer incidence have varied with respect to the
measurement of urbanization. Many American studies
considered incidence as a function of population density,
or have used a dichotomous deﬁnition of urban and rural
areas (1–7, 9–11, 14, 36). Population density may perform
poorly as a proxy for urbanization in large counties with
a locus of population clustered around a single city (37),
while dichotomous deﬁnitions may poorly capture hetero-
geneity within rural areas (32, 38). A recent study of cancer
incidence using cancer registry data in Texas found residual
heterogeneity within non-Metropolitan Area rates when
non-dichotomous urbanization scales such as the UIC or the
similar Urban-Rural Continuum Code were applied (39).
Our use of a non-dichotomous measure also revealed
unique racial patterns in incidence at the very rural end of
the scale. Among non-white women, invasive incidence
was highest in the most rural UIC groups compared with the
other two groups. Also, although white incidence exceeded
non-white incidence generally in this study, non-white
incidence was higher than white incidence only in most
rural UIC group, for both stages. Compared with urban non-
whites, rural non-whites are of lower socioeconomic status
in North Carolina (40). We hypothesize that these rural
women may have especially beneﬁted from federal funding
programs for screening of low-income women in the 1990s,
reﬂected in a ‘‘catch-up’’ in rural incidence rates (41).

hospitals was higher than incidence in counties without
registry hospitals for all of our strata, although some rates
were based on small case counts. The presence of a registry

hospital (data not shown). Registry hospitals are required to
have mammography available in-house or by referral, and
mammography must meet the standards set by the American
College of Radiology or the State Public Health Department
(28). The magnitude of the differences between registry and
non-registry hospital rates we observed was larger for in situ
than invasive cancers. However, these hospitals may also
ascertain and report cases more completely, so it is difﬁcult
to know if either or both explanations can account for the
registry/non-registry incidence difference.
It is possible that the presence of staff dedicated to
reporting cases in registry hospitals may result in more
complete case-ascertainment and/or more complete report-
ing of cases ascertained than non-registry hospitals. For
example, registry hospitals may be more likely to report cases
that are patients of staff physicians, but diagnosed and
treated outside the hospital setting. We cannot know this
deﬁnitively, however, without access to proprietary data
speciﬁcally containing results of studies of case ﬁnding and
completeness of reporting in registry and non-registry
hospitals.
There are other possible explanations for the excess
incidence of breast cancer in registry hospital counties.
Registry hospitals and their certiﬁcation by the American
College of Surgeons may attract more cancer patients for
care and treatment. These patients may give the address of
a friend or family member in the same county as the hospital
when traveling from rural counties, and this local, more
urban, address becomes associated with the case and
ultimately reﬂected in the incidence statistic. We are aware
of no published studies of county of diagnosis of breast
cancer versus patient’s county of residence to inform this
hypothesis. One unpublished analysis completed in the early
1990s of women in 39 North Carolina counties in the
central and eastern part of the state (that did not share
a border with another state) found that 26% of women with
breast cancer were diagnosed outside their county of
residence. In addition, the six counties that received the
highest proportion of cases from outside their county were
located in Metropolitan Areas (42). This supports the idea
that some women may travel from a non-Metropolitan
county to a Metropolitan county for diagnosis. The
expansion of ambulatory surgery centers in North Carolina
in the 1990s, however, has facilitated outpatient treatment
and diagnosis of breast cancer at a more local level, possibly
reducing the need for travel. Due to patient conﬁdentiality
we have no individual-level data in this analysis that would
allow us to consider the accuracy of patient address.
Women in counties with registry hospitals may have
higher breast cancer risk due to a different distribution of
risk factors in those counties. For example, women living
near major urban academic centers such as Raleigh-
Durham-Chapel Hill, the ‘‘Research Triangle,’’ may have
lower parity and later age at ﬁrst full-term pregnancy than
urban women elsewhere in North Carolina. We considered
a county-level variable estimating parity (the mean number
of children ever born to women aged 35–44 years) and found
it was lower in registry hospital counties (1.8) versus
counties without (2.0), suggesting that risk factors may
indeed differ (43). This relationship held within grouped
urbanization level. We also considered an ecologic measure
of socioeconomic status, but did not ﬁnd that the proportion

unknown which of the possible explanations presented

are functioning simultaneously making it difﬁcult to isolate
the relative contribution of a single explanation. There may
be additional unmeasured variables that are not captured by
our study. Among whites for in situ disease, an excess
incidence associated with the most urban counties persisted
after stratiﬁcation by hospital type, and remains unex-
plained in our analysis.
A limitation of these analyses is that the registry status of
each hospital is known only for 2002; exact certiﬁcation
dates are held in conﬁdence by the ACOS, so misclassiﬁ-
cation of registry hospital status over the study period is
possible. Over the study period, urbanization has increased
in North Carolina by population in-migration to Metro-
politan Areas (45). The most recently available 1993 UICs

Our results call for further examination of the role of the
health care availability when evaluating geographic pat-
terns of breast cancer incidence. The explanatory factors
behind higher incidence in the presence of a registry
hospital are unknown, but could include more complete
reporting due to the presence of the in-house cancer registry
and differentially greater detection due to mammographic
screening. Closer study of the exact mechanism(s) by which
registry hospitals increase incidence is warranted. We
suggest that urban excesses in reported breast cancer in
other states may be explained by simple disparities in
health care availability, a readily testable hypothesis.
This approach may be a cost-effective ﬁrst step towards
understanding geographic patterns of incidence before
embarking on more costly biologic exposure studies.
