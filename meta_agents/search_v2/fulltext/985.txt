TITLE: The risk of developing breast cancer within the next 5, 10, or 20 years of a woman's life

The Risk of Developing Breast Cancer Within the
Next 5, 10, or 20 Years of a Woman’s Life
Cyllene R. Morris, DVM, PhD, William E. Wright, PhD, Robert D. Schlag, MSc
Background:
The lifetime risk of developing breast cancer is a frequently misinterpreted statistic. Risk
projections over a shorter time period, conditioned on current age, may be less prone to
misconceptions and more relevant to populations at different ages. The purpose of this
study was to estimate the risk of developing breast cancer within the next 5, 10, or 20 years
for women currently aged 30 to 70 years in California’s four major race/ethnic groups.
Methods:
Life tables were used to obtain risk estimates based on 1993–1997 breast cancer incidence
rates from the California Cancer Registry and statewide mortality rates.
Results:
For women currently aged 50, the estimated risk of developing invasive breast cancer
within 5 years varied from 0.8% (1 in 133) among Hispanics to 1.3% (1 in 75) among
Caucasians. Risk estimates within 10 years were 2.9% (1 in 34) among Caucasians, 2.3% (1
in 43) among African Americans, 2.0% (1 in 51) among Asian/Paciﬁc Islanders, and 1.6%
(1 in 63) among Hispanics. Within 20 years, estimated risks increased to 6.6% (1 in 15)
among Caucasians, 5.0% (1 in 20) among African Americans, 3.9% (1 in 26) among
Asian/Paciﬁc Islanders, and 3.7% (1 in 27) among Hispanics. Risk estimates were 8% to
20% higher when in situ tumors were included in the calculations.
Conclusions:
Based on these estimates, the baseline risk of developing breast cancer in the next 1 or 2
decades of life varies by race/ethnicity and current age, but may be lower than the risk
perceived by most women.

Introduction
T
he lifetime risk of developing breast cancer,
frequently translated into the familiar statistic of
one-in-nine women, is commonly cited in the
health care community and the media. Notwithstand-
ing the value of lifetime risk estimates as a way to
communicate the need for breast cancer screening,
misconceptions about this frequently cited number
may be generating excessive fear and anxiety among
women. For example, in a recent population-based
survey, most respondents overestimated their average
lifetime risk of breast cancer, independent of their age
or prior exposure to information on breast cancer.1 A
community survey of mammography use found that
21% of women in their 40s perceived their personal risk
as “higher than most.”2 Another study on perceptions
of breast cancer risk found that 37% of women aged
,50 overestimated their risk of developing breast can-
cer within the next 10 years by a factor of ten or more.3
The authors suggested that the one-in-nine lifetime risk
may have been misinterpreted by women as being a
short-term probability.3 Counseling apparently did not
prevent women from overestimating their risks. In a
randomized trial of risk counseling among ﬁrst-degree
relatives of breast cancer patients, about two thirds of
women who had been counseled continued to overes-
timate their lifetime risks substantially.4 Similarly, many
women with a family history of breast cancer who
attended genetic counseling failed to recall risk ﬁgures
correctly, and 66% could not accurately recall their
own lifetime chance of developing the disease.5
Lifetime risk is deﬁned as a newborn female’s prob-
ability of eventually being diagnosed with breast cancer.
It assumes that age-speciﬁc breast cancer incidence
rates and mortality rates from all causes other than
breast cancer will remain stable over her lifetime pe-
riod. Risk projections over a shorter time period are
therefore more likely to be accurate, and may be more
meaningful to the general public. In addition, because
breast cancer incidence and mortality vary markedly by
race/ethnicity,6,7 race-speciﬁc risk estimates are more

Methods
In this report, the risk of developing cancer was taken to
mean the risk of being diagnosed with cancer. Estimates of
risk were obtained following the methodology of Feuer et
al.,8,9 based on breast cancer incidence and mortality rates
from all causes other than breast cancer in California from
1993 to 1997. Two sets of estimates were calculated: (1) for all
breast cancers (invasive plus in situ tumors) and (2) for
invasive breast cancer only. The SAS System release 6.12 (SAS
Institute Inc., Cary, NC) and DEVCAN, a computer program
available through the National Cancer Institute (NCI),8 were
used for all data analyses.
Breast Cancer Incidence and Mortality Rates
During the 1993–1997 period, 109,165 female breast cancers
were diagnosed in California and reported to the CCR as of
November 1999. Age-speciﬁc incidence rates for breast can-
cer (Table 1) and mortality rates for all causes other than
breast cancer were calculated for each race/ethnic group.
Incidence rates were based on the number of ﬁrst primary
breast cancers (invasive and invasive plus in situ cases)
diagnosed in California women from 1993 to 1997. Non–
breast-cancer mortality rates were obtained by subtracting
breast cancer related deaths from the total number of deaths
occurring in California during the period (Center of Health
Statistics, California Department of Health Services). Race-
and age-speciﬁc population estimates were obtained from the
Demographic Research Unit, California Department of
Finance.
Deﬁnition of Race/Ethnicity
Race/ethnicity for breast cancer cases, deaths, and popula-
tion estimates was grouped into four mutually exclusive
categories of non-Hispanic Caucasian, non-Hispanic African
American, Hispanic, and non-Hispanic Asian/Paciﬁc Is-
lander. Hispanic ethnicity of cases and deaths was based on
information on the medical record or death certiﬁcate. In
addition, women of any race who had a last name (or maiden
name, when present) appearing on the 1980 U.S. Census list
of 12,497 Hispanic surnames were also classiﬁed as Hispanic.

Risk Estimates
Calculations were based on a hypothetical cohort of 10
million newborn females, for whom standard multiple-decre-
ment life tables were derived.8,9 During each 5-year age
interval, females in this cohort were considered to be at risk
for two mutually exclusive events: (1) developing breast
cancer for the ﬁrst time in their lives or (2) dying of other
causes without having developed breast cancer. Mortality
among women with breast cancer did not affect the calcula-
tions, since they already had developed breast cancer. Prob-
abilities derived from statewide incidence and mortality rates
were applied to the hypothetical cohort to generate the
number of women expected to develop breast cancer or to
die of other causes without having developed breast cancer,
during each age interval. Women who survived the interval
free of breast cancer became the population at risk at the
beginning of the next age interval. The risk of developing
breast cancer within a particular age interval was estimated as
the number of expected incident cancers developing during
that interval divided by the population at risk (free of breast
cancer) at the beginning of that period.

oping breast cancer in the next 5 to 20 years of her life,
reﬂecting the fact that incidence rates increase with
age.6 However, the risk in the next 20 years for a woman
at any age was always higher than the risk in the next 10
years for a woman 10 years older. For example, the
estimated risk by age 70 for a 50-year-old woman was
higher than the risk by age 70 for a 60-year-old woman.
This is because, unlike an older woman who has already
survived and reached her current age free of breast
cancer, a woman 10 years younger is still at risk of
developing breast cancer in the next decade of her life.
Overall, risk estimates increased by 8% to 20% when
in situ cancers were included in the calculations (Table
2). As expected, such an increase in risk estimates for
the age groups included in this study paralleled the
proportion of in situ tumors among all breast cancers
diagnosed in California from 1993 to 1997.6
Discussion
The lifetime risk of developing invasive breast cancer,
albeit a powerful translational statistic, has been fre-
quently misinterpreted by the lay media and the public.
The commonly cited one-in-eight ﬁgure (or one in
nine when truncated to age 85) represents a newborn
female’s likelihood of eventually being diagnosed with
invasive breast cancer, and does not apply to women of
all ages. For women who are currently over 40, esti-
mates of the risk of developing invasive breast cancer at
some time during the remainder of their lives actually
decrease with age,6,11 and differ substantially from
estimates of lifetime risk for a newborn. The difference
in risk is due to the fact that an older woman has
already survived several decades free of breast cancer,
while a newborn is still at risk of developing breast
cancer (or dying of other causes) until she reaches that
particular age. Another frequent misconception is that
lifetime risk estimates are valid only if all women live to
a speciﬁc age (e.g., 85 or 95 years), when in reality
calculations take into account the likelihood of dying of
other causes at any age.9 In addition to these miscon-
ceptions, risk estimates are projections of current rates
into the future and are, therefore, valid only if these
rates remain stable over time. In the short term, such
an assumption may hold true, but is probably not
realistic over a long period of time. On one hand,
improvements in screening technology may inﬂate
incidence rates, since more tumors are detected at an
earlier stage of development. On the other hand, breast
cancer research may lead to more-effective prevention
strategies and, therefore, to an actual decrease in risk.
For these reasons, risk estimates based on current age
and calculated for the next decade or two may be more
accurate, less prone to misconceptions and, therefore,
more meaningful to the general population.
In this study, the risk of developing breast cancer
varied markedly by race/ethnicity, reﬂecting the al-
ready recognized race/ethnic differences in breast can-
cer rates in California and the United States.6,7,11 The
accuracy of ethnic classiﬁcation is an ever-present con-
cern in health studies that utilize medical record data,
since ethnicity assignment in medical records may
involve subjective appraisals by hospital personnel. The
possibility of some misclassiﬁcation of Hispanic ethnic-
ity in this study cannot be ruled out. However, such
misclassiﬁcation was minimized by supplementing med-
ical record information with use of the patient’s sur-
name. An earlier California study demonstrated that
use of medical records plus surname reduced the bias
observed with use of either method alone when com-
pared with self-identiﬁcation of ethnicity.10
Differences in mortality rates and in screening prac-
tices may have a substantial impact on risk estimates.
High non–breast-cancer-related mortality rates de-
crease the number of women in the life table at risk for
breast cancer. When cancer probabilities are applied to
a smaller population at risk, the expected number of
breast cancers will be accordingly smaller. Assuming
identical incidence rates, risk estimates would be lower
for a race/ethnic group with higher mortality rates,
because the number of cancers in the life table (to be
divided by the initial population at risk) would be
smaller. Mammography screening can also affect breast
cancer risk in two opposite ways. Screening may reduce
risk because prevalent cases are excluded from the
population at risk, but it may also increase the risk due
to lead time and, particularly for in situ tumors, over-
diagnosis bias.
This study is based on California statewide data, and
we do not know to what extent these risk estimates can
be extrapolated to other geographic areas in the
United States. However, interval risks of developing
invasive breast cancer for African-American and Cauca-
sian women in this study are consistent with estimates
from the NCI’s Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End
Results (SEER) program, based on data from 1995
through 1997.11 Risk estimates for Hispanic and
Asian/PI women, or for invasive/in situ tumors were

The population-based estimates of risk presented in
this report do not take into account risk factors other
than age and race/ethnicity, and represent the average
risk of breast cancer. More-complex models have been
developed that project the risk of breast cancer with
consideration of individual risk factors.12–14 One of
these models, developed by Claus et al.,12 calculates
breast cancer risk for women with a ﬁrst-degree family
history of ovarian cancer. The Gail model,13 and a
modiﬁcation of this model developed by the National
Surgical Adjuvant Breast and Bowel Project,14 account
for individual risk factors such as current age, age at
menarche, age at ﬁrst live birth, number of previous
breast biopsies, presence of atypical hyperplasia, and
number of affected ﬁrst-degree relatives. Such models
can be very useful to counsel individual women, but
have limited use for communicating risk to a broader
population. In addition, these models were developed
based on the occurrence of breast cancer in Caucasian
women only, and their validity for women of other
race/ethnic groups has not been determined.15
Clearly, age and race/ethnicity alone cannot predict
the risk of developing cancer for an individual woman,
for whom the limitations of risk projections should be
recognized. However, population-based estimates can
be used by health care providers in primary care
settings to increase awareness and encourage screening
among older women and, at the same time, put breast
cancer risk into perspective among younger women.
Based on these estimates, the baseline risk of develop-
ing breast cancer in the next 1 or 2 decades of life may
be considerably lower than the risk perceived by most
women. Use of this statistic to communicate a woman’s
average risk of developing breast cancer at her current
age may reduce the current confusion and anxiety
around this serious issue.
