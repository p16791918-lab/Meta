TITLE: Breast cancer trends among young women in the United States

BRIEF REPORT
Breast Cancer Trends Among Young Women in the
United States
Robert E. Tarone
Background: It has been suggested that exposures associated with
industrialization have increased breast cancer risk among young
women in recent decades in the United States despite data demon-
strating declining breast cancer mortality in birth cohorts born after
1945.
Methods: Trends for in situ and invasive breast cancer incidence
rates from 1975 through 2002 among white and black U.S. women
ages 20 to 49 are evaluated by decade of age using linear regression
analyses.
Results: Despite increasing rates of in situ breast cancer after 1980
reﬂecting increased use of mammography, invasive breast cancer
rates declined for both white and black women under age 50. These
declines are consistent with a decrease in birth cohort risk of breast
cancer for women born after 1945.
Conclusions: Invasive breast cancer incidence rates are not increas-
ing in young U.S. women despite increases in mammography and
trends in known risk factors (eg, reproductive factors) that would
predict increasing risk.
I
t has been reported that breast cancer risk is increasing over
time everywhere in the world and that successive birth
cohorts of women have had higher levels of risk throughout
their lives beginning early in life.1 This would seem incon-
sistent with the decreasing risk of breast cancer mortality in
young U.S. women due to a decreasing birth cohort risk for
women born after 1945.2 A possible explanation for this
inconsistency is that incidence rates reﬂect breast cancer risk
better than mortality rates.3 An increase in the age-adjusted
invasive breast cancer incidence rate for all U.S. women under
the age of 50 between years 1975 and 2002 appears to support
such an explanation.3
It can be debated whether trends in breast cancer risk
(ie, the probability that a woman develops breast cancer) are
best represented by trends in incidence rates (which reﬂect
improvements in diagnostic technology as well as risk) or
mortality rates (which reﬂect improvements in cancer detec-
tion and cancer treatment as well as risk). Any inference
about breast cancer risk based on the increase between 1975
and 2002 in the age-adjusted breast cancer incidence rate for
all U.S. women under age 50, however, must take into
account both the marked increase in the use of mammography
beginning in the early 1980s and the well-established birth
cohort pattern of breast cancer risk.4–6 The age-adjusted
breast cancer incidence rate in women under the age of 50
would be expected to increase substantially between 1975
and 2002 due to increased use of mammography even if the
underlying trend in breast cancer risk is ﬂat or decreasing
slowly. In addition, this rate would be expected to increase
somewhat between 1975 and the late 1980s due to increasing
birth cohort risk for women born before 1945. Accordingly,
the observed increase in the age-adjusted incidence rate for
women under age 50 between 1975 and 2002 is not sufﬁcient
to demonstrate increasing risk of developing breast cancer for
women born after 1945.
Secular trends for both in situ and invasive breast
cancer incidence rates in young U.S. women by decade of age
are evaluated here to determine whether these incidence rates
provide evidence of the decreasing birth cohort risk demon-
strated previously for U.S. women born after 1945 in the
analysis of breast cancer mortality rates.5,6
METHODS
Invasive and in situ breast cancer incidence rates from
the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER)
Program of the U.S. National Cancer Institute were calculated
using SEER*Stat software.7 Directly standardized age-ad-
justed rates (U.S. 2000 standard population) were calculated
separately for white and black women. Regression analyses
were performed using annual age-adjusted incidence rates in
3 10-year age groups: 20–29, 30–39, and 40–49 years of age
at time of breast cancer diagnosis. The starting years of the
regression analyses differed by age group; the recent decrease
in risk of dying from breast cancer appears to be a birth
cohort phenomenon (ie, decreasing mortality rates for women
born after 1945),5,6 and so the starting years in the analysis of
breast cancer incidence rates for the 2 older decades of age
were determined by adding the midpoints of the age intervals
to 1945. Thus, the regression analyses were based on rates
from 1975 through 2002 for the 20–29 age group, from 1980
through 2002 for the 30–39 age group, and from 1990
through 2002 for the 40–49 age group. Standard 95% con-
ﬁdence intervals (CIs) for the estimated slopes from the
regression analyses were calculated using the t-distribution.

RESULTS
Secular trends of in situ breast cancer rates provide per-
haps the best indicator of increasing mammography use. Figure
1 shows the age-adjusted in situ breast cancer incidence rates for
white and black women from 1975 through 2001. To smooth the
curves, each point represents the rate for 3 consecutive years,
and the rate is plotted at the midpoint of the 3-year period. For
both white women and black women, rates increased over 3-fold
between 1980 and 1990 and then increased approximately 2-fold
between 1990 and 2001. Increases in rates of in situ breast
cancer after 1980 were observed for every decade of age begin-
ning with the 20–29 age group (Figs. A1–A6, available with the
online version of the article). The marked increase in mammog-
raphy use reﬂected by the curves in Figure 1 would also be
expected to have a substantial impact on trends in invasive breast
cancer incidence rates.
Table 1 shows the results of linear regression analyses of
in situ and invasive breast cancer incidence rates by decade of
age for white and black women between the ages of 20 and 49.
The in situ breast cancer incidence rates increased over time for
each decade of age for both white and black women, reﬂecting
the marked increase in utilization of mammography. The SEER
invasive breast cancer incidence rates by decade of age in young
women have secular trends consistent with the birth cohort
pattern of risk observed previously for U.S. breast cancer mor-
tality rates (Figs. A7–A9, available with the online version of the
article). Despite the positive bias due to increasing mammogra-
phy use (ie, bias in terms of incidence rates representing the true
trend in underlying breast cancer risk), the invasive breast cancer
incidence rates decreased over time for each decade of age for
both white and black women (Table 1).
DISCUSSION
Regression analyses of breast cancer incidence rates by
decade of age in young women have demonstrated that breast
cancer risk has not been increasing among U.S. women born
after 1945. SEER invasive breast cancer incidence rates are
consistent with the birth cohort pattern of risk observed
previously in analyses of breast cancer mortality rates.5,6
Despite marked increases of in situ breast cancer incidence
rates (reﬂecting a substantial increase in the utilization of
mammography), invasive breast cancer incidence rates de-
creased for both white and black women born after 1945.
Evidence of a decrease in the birth cohort risk of breast
cancer has been noted for women born after World War II in
the United States, Canada, and Scotland.5,6,8,9 The decreases
of invasive breast cancer incidence rates and breast cancer
mortality rates among U.S. women born after 1945 have been

despite existing trends in most known or proposed breast
cancer risk factors that would predict increasing breast cancer
risk (eg, increasing age at ﬁrst birth, decreasing parity, and
both increased and earlier use of oral contraceptives).5,10 If,
as has been suggested, increasing industrialization also leads
to higher breast cancer risk,1 then the decreasing trends for
invasive breast cancer incidence rates and breast cancer
mortality rates among young U.S. women are even more
surprising. In fact, it follows that unknown and unexplained
factors correlated with industrialization must have exerted a
profound protective effect against breast cancer. Investigating
the paradoxically reduced breast cancer risk among young
U.S. women and identifying the unknown protective factor
(or factors) should be a major priority in cancer research.
