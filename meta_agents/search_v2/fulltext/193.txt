A B S T R A C T
Introduction: This study examined recent trends and outcomes in breast cancer among Black women under age 
forty.
Methods: Breast cancer incidence data from the National Cancer Database (2010–2022) were analyzed using U.S. 
Census data population estimates.
Results: Among 188,311 women under forty, 16.4 % were Black, compared to 11.3 % in older Black women. 
From 2010 to 2022, incidence in young Black women rose 5.3 %, compared to 19.9 % in White, 27.7 % in 
Hispanic, and 18.9 % in Asian/Pacific Islander women. Triple-negative breast cancer was more common in 
young Black women (16.3 %) vs. young Non-Black women (12.1 %). Black women under forty had 50 % higher 
odds of death (OR 1.50; 95 % CI: 1.46–1.55, p < .0001) compared to their Non-Black peers after adjusting for 
age, tumor characteristics, and treatment.
Conclusions: Young Black women have more aggressive breast cancers and worse overall survival than Non-Black 
young women, though incidence has remained stable over the past decade.
1. Introduction
Breast cancer is the most diagnosed cancer among women world­
wide, and studies have shown an increasing incidence of breast cancer in 
younger women.1,2 The definition of “young women” is subject to 
variability but many clinical trials and other studies have defined it as 
under 40 years old.3,4 Among these younger women, Non-Hispanic 
Black or African American women face a disproportionately high 
burden of the disease.5,6 This group has not only been more likely to be 
diagnosed at younger ages compared to other racial and ethnic groups, 
but also often presents with more aggressive tumor subtypes, such as 
triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), which is associated with poorer 
prognoses and limited treatment options.7 Black women, regardless of 
age, are more likely to be diagnosed at later stages and are 41 % more 
likely to die from the disease than White women, despite lower inci­
dence rates.8
A large recent study by the American Cancer Society showed that 
breast cancer incidence has been rising steadily, particularly among 
younger women. Between 2012 and 2021, breast cancer incidence 
increased by 1 % annually overall, with a sharper rise of 1.4 % per year 
in women under fifty. The study also showed that while the incidence 
rate of Black women was 5 % lower than that of White women, their 
mortality rate was 38 % higher. Black women were also more likely to be 
diagnosed at later stages and with more aggressive subtypes, such as 
triple-negative breast cancer, contributing to lower survival rates across 
all stages. Additionally, younger Black women had the highest incidence 
of breast cancer before age 40 compared to other racial and ethnic 
groups.9
Although the American Cancer Society study defined 
“young-onset breast cancer” as occurring under age fifty, this study uses 
a cutoff of less than forty years for this analysis in alignment with clinical 
trials and biologically distinct subgroups. This definition is widely used 
in breast oncology studies to capture the unique tumor biology and 
treatment considerations associated with very early-onset disease.4,10–13
To confirm these trends amongst Black women, we examined data 
from the National Cancer Data Base (NCDB). This database examines 
cancer cases and treatment from patients treated at approximately 1400 
Commission on Cancer accredited facilities across the United States.14

2. Methods
2.1. Study design
This study employed a retrospective cohort design to analyze trends 
in breast cancer diagnoses among women under and over forty years of 
age from 2010 to 2022 using data from the NCDB. The NCDB, a 
nationwide clinical oncology database, collects data from more than 
1400 Commission on Cancer-accredited facilities, representing approx­
imately 70 % of all newly diagnosed cancers in the United States.16 This 
database includes comprehensive information on patient demographics, 
tumor characteristics, treatments, and outcomes.14 The study extracted 
demographic, clinical, and pathological variables to evaluate trends in 
breast cancer diagnoses, stratified by age, race/ethnicity, and tumor 
subtype. To contextualize findings within the broader population, 
United States Census data were incorporated to standardize rates and 
calculate population-based denominators for analysis.
2.2. Study setting
The study was set in the United States, using the NCDB to evaluate 
breast cancer diagnoses across a diverse cohort of women. Data collec­
tion spanned from 2010 to 2022, encompassing urban, suburban, and 
rural healthcare settings. The NCDB's national scope provided a 
comprehensive overview of breast cancer trends across racial and ethnic 
groups, offering insights into disparities and variations in diagnosis 
patterns by age and tumor characteristics.
2.3. Participants
The study included women with newly diagnosed breast cancer of all 
racial and ethnic backgrounds who were diagnosed with breast cancer 
between 2010 and 2022. Participants included patients having a com­
bination of treatment and diagnosis at the reporting facility. Participants 
were stratified into two primary age groups: women under forty years 
and women over forty years. Annual demographic data from the United 
States Census from 2010 to 2022 were utilized to represent the total 
population and calculate standardized incidence rates. Subgroup ana­
lyses were conducted to explore trends among Black women, White 
women, Hispanic women, Asian/Pacific Islander women and women 
that identified as Non-Hispanic other.
2.4. Data sources
The primary data source for this study was the NCDB, which houses 
tumor and treatment data on newly diagnosed breast cancer patients 
treated at Commission on Cancer sites. The database includes variables 
such as patient age, race/ethnicity, tumor size, stage at diagnosis, re­
ceptor status (ER, PR, HER2), and treatment modalities. Additionally, 
United States Census data were used to standardize breast cancer inci­
dence rates and ensure population-based comparisons. United States 
Census data were obtained from the United States Census Bureau.17
2.5. Statistical methods
Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics 
of the study population, including age, race/ethnicity, tumor subtype, 
and stage at diagnosis. Incidence rates were standardized using popu­
lation data from the United States Census. Subgroup analyses examined 
differences in diagnosis rates, tumor characteristics, and trends by age 
group and racial/ethnic categories. Kaplan-Meier analysis and multi­
variable Cox proportional hazards modeling were used to assess overall 
survival between young Black women and Non-Black young women. 
Statistical significance was determined at a threshold of p < 0.05, and all 
analyses were conducted using statistical software.
3. Results
3.1. Trends in Breast Cancer Incidence in Black Women
Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of female patients diagnosed 
with breast cancer from 2010 to 2022, stratified by age group (<40 vs. 
≥40 years). Among patients under forty, the mean age at diagnosis was 
34.7 years, compared to 62.6 years in those aged forty or older. The 
NCDB captured 188,311 breast cancer cases in women under forty years 
and 4,007,073 cases in women forty and older. Within the younger 
cohort, 89.1 % were aged 30–39, while 10.9 % were under thirty.
Among breast cancer patients under age forty, 63.5 % were White, 
16.4 % Black, 11.2 % Hispanic, 6.3 % Asian/Pacific Islander, and 2.6 % 
Non-Hispanic Other. In contrast, among patients aged forty and older, 
White women comprised a larger majority (77.5 %), with lower pro­
portions of Black (11.3 %) and Hispanic (5.6 %) patients.

Table 2 presents the annual incidence rates of breast cancer cases per 
100,000 population among women aged 18–39 years, stratified by race/ 
ethnicity, between 2010 and 2022. The overall incidence increased from 
92 per 100,000 in 2010 to 107 per 100,000 in 2022, representing a 15.4 
% relative increase among all races/ethnicities. White and Black pop­
ulations exhibited an absolute increase of 4 cases per 100,000 and 1 case 
per 100,000, corresponding to a 19.9 % and 5.3 % relative change, 
respectively. Among Hispanic individuals, incidence rose from 11 per 
100,000 in 2010 to 14 per 100,000 in 2022, reflecting the largest rela­
tive increase of 27.7 %. Asian/Pacific Islander populations showed a rise 
in incidence from 20 to 23 per 100,000 during the same period, a 
relative change of 18.9 %. The Non-Hispanic Other group demonstrated 
a slight increase from 16 to 18 per 100,000, equating to a 12.2 % relative 
change. These findings highlight an overall upward trend in breast 
cancer incidence rates among young women across all racial/ethnic 
groups, with the Hispanic population experiencing the greatest relative 
increase and Black women experiencing the lowest relative increase.
Fig. 1 illustrates the incidence of breast cancer cases per 100,000 
population among Black women under and over the age of forty from 
2010 to 2022. The incidence among Black women under forty remained 
relatively stable, ranging from 24 to 26 cases per 100,000 with a total 
increase of 5.3 % over the study period. In contrast, women aged forty 
and older experienced a more pronounced increase in incidence, rising 
from 249 to 299 cases per 100,000, a relative increase of 20.2 %. The 
disparity between the two age groups persisted across all years, with 
breast cancer incidence consistently higher among older women.
3.2. Tumor characteristics in young Black Women
Table 3 compares tumor characteristics across four groups: Black 
women under age forty, Black women aged forty and older, Non-Black 
women under forty and Non-Black women aged forty and older. 
Among younger Black women, 63.6 % had hormone receptor-positive 
(ER+/PR+) tumors, slightly lower than both older Black women 
(70.8 %) and younger Non-Black women (68.9 %). HER2 positivity was 
more common among younger Black women (15.1 %) than among older 
Black women (9.3 %) but comparable to younger Non-Black women 
(16.4 %). Triple-negative breast cancer was more prevalent in younger 
Black women (16.3 %) than in both older Black women (12.7 %) and 
younger Non-Black women (12.1 %).
Younger Black women had a significantly higher proportion of 
poorly differentiated tumors (56.3 %) compared to older Black women 
(38.9 %) and younger Non-Black women (49.8 %). Well-differentiated 
tumors were less common in this group (25.6 %) versus 38.4 % in 
older Black women and 31.6 % in younger Non-Black women. The 
proportion of moderately differentiated tumors was also lowest among 
younger Black women (10.0 %).
Tumor size patterns revealed that younger Black women were more 
likely to present with larger tumors. Only 29.9 % of this group had tu­
mors ≤2 cm, compared to 47.2 % of older Black women and 36.3 % of 
younger Non-Black women. Nearly one-fifth (18.8 %) of younger Black 
women had tumors >5 cm, nearly double the proportion seen in older 
Black women (10.6 %).
Lymph node involvement was more frequent among older Black 
women (55.0 %) than younger Black women (47.4 %) but similar to 
younger Non-Black women (52.6 %). Stage at diagnosis also varied: 
70.9 % of younger Black women were diagnosed at early stage (I/II), 
compared to 80.2 % of older Black women and 77.0 % of younger Non- 
Black women. Late-stage diagnoses (III/IV) were more common in 
younger Black women (25.2 %) compared to the other groups.
3.3. Facility characteristics of young Black Women
Table 4 summarize facility type and geographic location for Black 
women under age forty, Black women aged forty and older, and Non- 
Black women under forty. Among Black women under forty, the most 
common facility type was academic/research institutions (40.9 %), 
followed by comprehensive community cancer programs (33.6 %), and 
integrated network programs (20.9 %). This distribution was compara­
ble among Black women aged forty and older. Non-Black women under 
forty had a slightly lower proportion treated at academic/research 
centers (37.5 %) and a slightly higher proportion at comprehensive 
community cancer programs (36.4 %).
Geographically, Black women under forty were most frequently 
treated in the South Atlantic region (37.9 %), followed by the Middle 
Atlantic (14.3 %) and East North Central (14.2 %) regions. This regional 
distribution was consistent across both age groups of Black women. In 
contrast, Non-Black women under forty were more widely distributed, 
with higher proportions treated in New England, the Pacific, and 
Mountain regions compared to Black women.
3.4. Overall survival of young Black Women
Table 5 and Fig. 2 summarize overall survival among Black women 
under forty compared to Non-Black women under forty, adjusting for 
clinical and treatment variables. Black women under forty had lower 
survival, with a 5-year survival rate of 81.5 % and 10-year survival of 
73.0 %, compared to 89.7 % and 82.7 %, respectively, for Non-Black 
women under forty. Multivariable analysis showed that Black women 
under forty had 50 % higher odds of death (OR 1.50; 95 % CI: 1.46–1.55, 
p < .0001) than their Non-Black peers after adjusting for age, tumor 
characteristics, and treatment. To provide additional context, Table 6
includes 5 and 10 year survival estimates for Black and Non-Black 
women both under and over age forty, highlighting the persistence of 
survival disparities across both age and racial groups.
Additional factors associated with improved survival included older 
age (30–39 vs. <30 years), which may reflect increased likelihood of 
receiving adjuvant therapies such as chemotherapy, radiation, and 
hormone therapy, all of which were independently associated with 
improved survival in our multivariable model. Conversely, higher co­
morbidity scores, larger tumors, node positivity and receipt of chemo­
therapy were associated with worse outcomes.

4. Discussion
This study provides an updated analysis of breast cancer trends, 
tumor characteristics, and cancer outcomes in Black women under forty 
years old using a large, nationally representative dataset. We found that 
young Black women remain disproportionately affected by triple nega­
tive breast cancer, a more aggressive breast cancer phenotype but the 
incidence of breast cancer among young Black women has remained 
relatively constant over the past decade. In contrast, the incidence of 
breast cancer among Hispanic, White and Asian/Pacific Islander young 
women has increased by 27.7 %, 19.9 %, and 18.9 % respectively. By 
2022, incidence rates among young Black and White women had 
converged at 26 per 100,000, a significant shift from 2010 when young 
Black women had the highest rates. Despite a relatively modest 5.3 % 
increase in incidence among young Black women, their tumor biology, 
and treatment outcomes continue to reflect deep disparities.
Our findings are consistent with recent data from the American 
Cancer Society, which report that breast cancer incidence is rising most 
rapidly in younger women, particularly among Hispanic and Asian 
American populations. The American Cancer Society study reports a 1 % 
per year increase in the incidence of breast cancer in young women 
under fifty years old and our study showed a 15 % increasing incidence 
in breast cancer among young women under forty years old over the past 
decade. The American Cancer Society also highlights that Black women 
under forty have the highest incidence of breast cancer compared to all 
other racial/ethnic groups, but face the highest mortality rates due to 
later-stage diagnoses and more aggressive subtypes.9 While White 
women continue to have the highest overall incidence, mortality rates

higher rates of triple-negative breast cancer, limited access to early 
detection, and systemic inequities in care delivery.18–20 Likewise, our 
study shows that young Black women have worse overall survival than 
young Non-Black women with a 50 % worse overall mortality despite 
adjusting for tumor characteristics and treatments. The NCDB does not 
contain any recurrence information, so we do not know if this increased 
mortality is related to breast cancer or not. However, given the young 
age of these women and the lower likelihood of other life-threatening 
conditions or co-morbidities, it is more likely these deaths were from 
breast cancer. Nonetheless, these data are very bothersome and certainly 
warrant further investigation.
The persistently high prevalence of triple-negative breast cancer 
(16.3 %) and poorly differentiated tumors (56.3 %) in young Black 
women in our cohort underscores the unique biologic profile of breast 
cancer in this population. Compared to both older Black women and 
young Non-Black women, those under forty were more likely to present 
with larger tumors, advanced stage disease, and lymph node involve­
ment, all factors associated with poor prognosis. These findings are 
supported by previous research demonstrating that African ancestry are 
independently associated with more aggressive tumor phenotypes, 
including basal-like and HER2-enriched subtypes.21,22 Our findings also 
align with studies which have demonstrated that very young age at 
diagnosis is independently associated with more adverse tumor biology 
and poorer survival outcomes.23–25 These more aggressive tumor fea­
tures also likely contribute to the worse overall survival we are seeing in 
these young Black women.
The sharp rise in breast cancer incidence among young Hispanic and 
Asian women is notable and warrants further investigation. Possible 
explanations include population changes in the United States, changes in 
reproductive behavior, obesity, hormone exposure, acculturation- 
related lifestyle shifts, and improved access to screening and diag­
nostic services in these populations. However, the relative contribution 
of these factors remains unclear. Importantly, the rise in incidence does

Facility characteristics and access to care also contributed to dis­
parities observed in our study. Younger women, particularly those 
identifying as Black, were more likely to be treated at academic or 
research institutions yet still faced higher odds of poor outcomes 
compared to their Non-Black peers. Our survival analysis showed that 
Black women under forty had 50 % higher adjusted odds of death 
compared to Non-Black women under forty. This finding suggests that 
factors beyond tumor biology such as delayed care, barriers to timely 
treatment, financial hardship, and structural racism may contribute to 
worse outcomes. While our analysis adjusted for area-based measures of 
socioeconomic status including median income, education level, and 
travel distance to the treating facility, Black women under forty 
continued to experience significantly worse survival outcomes, sug­
gesting that structural barriers beyond these factors may contribute to 
persistent disparities.
Although our study leverages the strength of the NCDB, which cap­
tures approximately 80% of all newly diagnosed breast cancers in the 
United States,15 several limitations should be noted. The NCDB does not 
capture patients treated at non-CoC facilities or outside of hospital set­
tings, and lacks granular sociodemographic variables such as income, 
education, and social determinants of health. Additionally, the NCDB 
does not capture patient-level data on family history or genetic testing, 
which limits our ability to evaluate hereditary cancer syndromes or 
BRCA-associated risk in this population. The retrospective nature of the 
study precludes causal inference however we have shown similar find­
ings to other studies of young Black women. Despite these limitations, 
our findings show some significant trends and worrisome outcomes that 
warrant future investigations to determine causes. These findings sug­
gest actionable targets for clinical and public health interventions.
5. Conclusion
Our study demonstrates that while Black women remain at high risk 
for early-onset breast cancer, the most pronounced relative increases in 
incidence are emerging among Hispanic women under forty. These 
findings highlight the need for targeted public health strategies, 
improved access to early detection and treatment, and further research 
into the biological and systemic factors contributing to these disparities. 
Addressing these challenges are critical to reducing the burden of breast 
cancer in young women and ensuring equitable care across all racial and 
ethnic groups.
