TITLE: Patterns of cancer incidence among US Hispanics/Latinos, 1995-2000

Abstract
Objective
Current and comprehensive data on cancer
incidence in US Latinos has been limited.
Methods
Using a standardized approach to uniformly
assign Hispanic/Latino race/ethnicity to cancer records,
data from 15 central cancer registries, representing
more than 85% of the US Latino population, were
included in the analysis. Average annual age-adjusted
incidence rates and standard errors were calculated for
Hispanic, non-Hispanic white and non-Hispanic black
males and females. To compare cancer incidence
among Hispanic and non-Hispanic populations, stan-
dardized incidence ratios (SIRs) also were calculated.
Results
Latino populations had overall lower inci-
dence for all cancers combined and the four leading
cancers (breast, prostate, lung and colorectal) than
non-Hispanic populations, however, cancers of lesser
impact in non-Hispanic populations (liver, gallbladder,
stomach, penis and cervix) were more commonly
diagnosed among Latinos.
Conclusions
Understanding the patterns of cancer
incidence in this diverse racial/ethnic minority group
can serve to both stimulate research into the unique
behaviors, exposures and genetics that drive cancer
risk among Latinos and to direct prevention and con-
trol efforts tailored to this population.

Introduction
The 2000 US census clearly conﬁrmed the Hispanic/
Latino population as the nation’s fastest growing
minority group. While the US population has grown
by 13% since the 1990 census, the Latino population
has grown by 58% and now comprises approximately
13% of the total US population, a proportion similar
to that of African-Americans [1]. One of the chal-
lenges to providing cancer incidence data for this
population has been the lack or inconsistency of
methods used by population-based cancer registries to
identify Latinos among reported cancer cases. Meth-
ods used to assign Hispanic/Latino ethnicity have in-
cluded
one
or
a
combination
of
the
following
approaches:
medical
record
notation,
birthplace
information, death certiﬁcates, race, visual cues or
identiﬁcation, self-identiﬁcation in research studies
used to supplement registry information, and use of
surnames and maiden names to identify Latino heri-
tage. Many registries did not use any protocol and did
not produce information about Latino populations in
their registry [2].
Therefore, in order to address the need for reliable
and comprehensive national cancer incidence data for
Latinos, it is necessary to ﬁrst develop a standardized
approach to Latino identiﬁcation in cancer registry

Materials and methods
Participating registries
In April 2003, NAACCR issued a call for data from its
US member registries, requesting they submit cancer
data for diagnosis years 1995 through 2000 for the
purpose of generating national cancer incidence sta-
tistics for Hispanic/Latino, non-Hispanic white and
non-Hispanic black populations. Fifteen population-
based central cancer registries, including those with the
largest Latino populations, met the NAACCR criteria
for data completeness and quality (i.e. silver or gold
certiﬁcation levels) for inclusion in the combined can-
cer statistics for Latino populations presented in this
report. Fourteen state central cancer registries (Ari-
zona, California, Colorado, Florida, Idaho, Illinois,
Iowa, New Jersey, New Mexico, New York, North
Carolina, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming) partici-
pated, as did one metropolitan area (Detroit, Michi-
gan). The data from these registries were used for the
comparison white and black non-Hispanic populations
as well.
NAACCR best practice guideline for identiﬁcation
of Hispanic/Latino persons
Hispanic ethnicity was assigned to the cancer cases
through the standardized use of the NAACCR His-
panic Identiﬁcation Algorithm (NHIA), the details of
which are available in the ﬁnal report of the NA-
ACCR Expert Panel on Hispanic Identiﬁcation [4].
Brieﬂy, NHIA uses a combination of NAACCR
variables, including Spanish/Hispanic origin, birth-
place, race, surname, and maiden name, to directly or
indirectly classify cases as Hispanic/Latino for analytic
purposes. Cases reported as having Spanish/Hispanic
origin (as indicated by a standard NAACCR data
element
reported
by
all
member
registries)
are
directly identiﬁed as Hispanic in the dataset. Cases
reported as non-Spanish/non-Hispanic, Spanish by
surname only, or unknown whether Spanish are
evaluated
for
possible
Hispanic
ethnicity
by
the
algorithm through indirect identiﬁcation. The ultimate
goal of the algorithm is to classify these cases as
Hispanic or non-Hispanic based on an evaluation of
the strength of the birthplace, race, and/or surname
associations with Hispanic ethnicity status. After the
individual registries have applied NHIA to their
database, cases not ultimately classiﬁed as Hispanic
are classiﬁed as non-Hispanic, leaving no cases with
‘‘unknown’’ Hispanic status, and these data are re-
ported to NAACCR. Hispanic ethnicity is assigned
regardless of race of the individual, e.g., a case having
a race code of black and a positive indicator for
Hispanic status according to the algorithm would be
assigned Hispanic ethnicity.
Statistical analysis
Rates for Hispanic, non-Hispanic white and non-
Hispanic black males and females were calculated per
100,000 population and age-adjusted by the direct
method to the 2000 US population standard. Hispanic
rates presented include all races, however, only a
small number of Hispanic cases had a race code other
than white. The incidence rates are annual averages
for the period 1995 through 2000. Estimates of the
population used for the calculation of rates for the
US for 1995 through 2000 were obtained from the
National Cancer Institute (NCI) Surveillance, Epide-
miology and End Results (SEER) program, based on
US Bureau of Census population estimates for these
years. These population estimates represent a modi-
ﬁcation of the annual time series of population esti-
mates produced by the Population Estimates Program
of the Bureau of the Census with support from the
NCI [5]. The standard error of the difference be-
tween adjusted cancer incidence rates was used to
calculate a Z (standard normal) statistic in order to
evaluate the statistical signiﬁcance of rate differences
among the populations [6]. To further quantify the
difference in incidence rates between Hispanic/Latino
and non-Hispanic males and females, standardized
incidence ratios (SIRs) were calculated by dividing
the age-adjusted incidence rate for Hispanics by the
age-adjusted incidence rate for non-Hispanic white
and non-Hispanic black populations. Corresponding
95% conﬁdence intervals for the SIRs also were
calculated.

Results
A total of 3,645,670 cancers (all invasive and bladder
in situ) were diagnosed in the 6 year study period
(1995–2000) among the central cancer registries com-
prising the NAACCR US combined areas. Of these,
306,918 (8.4%) were diagnosed among Hispanic/Latino
populations, 2,998,532 (82.2%) among non-Hispanic
white populations, and 340,220 (9.3%) among non-
Hispanic black populations. The areas covered in the
NAACCR combined cancer incidence rates used in
this study represent more than 85% of the total US
Hispanic/Latino population, however, only 45.2% of
the US non-Hispanic white population and 47.2% of
the US non-Hispanic black population is covered by
the registries providing these data.
Common cancers in Hispanic/Latino populations
The leading cancers among Latino males were prostate
(27.2% of all cancers among Latino males), lung and
bronchus (11.4%), colorectal (11.1%), non-Hodgkin
lymphoma (5.6%), and bladder (4.1%). These top ﬁve
sites accounted for 59.4% of the total cancers diag-
nosed among Latino males. The leading cancers among
Latina females were breast (29.2% of all cancers
among Latina females), colorectal (9.9%), lung and
bronchus (6.8%), cervix uteri (6.2%), and corpus uteri
(5.2%). Together, these sites accounted for 57.3% of
total cancers diagnosed among Latina females.
Comparison of common cancer sites with non-
Hispanic populations
Total cancers
For the majority of cancer sites, average annual age-
adjusted cancer incidence rates were statistically sig-
niﬁcantly lower among Hispanic males and females
than their non-Hispanic counterparts (Tables 1–3). The
overall rate for cancer among Latino males was 443.0
per 100,000, compared to 562.4 per 100,000 among non-
Hispanic white males and 661.0 per 100,000 among non-
Hispanic black males. Among Latina females, the
overall cancer rate was 313.0 per 100,000, compared to
434.9 among non-Hispanic white females and 391.7 per
100,000 among non-Hispanic black females.
Prostate cancer
Although this was the leading type of cancer diagnosed
in Latino males, the incidence rate was signiﬁcantly
lower than the corresponding rate in non-Hispanic
white (SIR = 0.88) and non-Hispanic black males
(SIR = 0.55).
Lung and bronchus cancer
This cancer was among the top ﬁve cancers diagnosed
in both Hispanic males and females; however, the
magnitude of the incidence rates was approximately
half that of the rates in their non-Hispanic counter-
parts, a difference that was statistically signiﬁcant.
Breast cancer (in females)
Breast cancer was the most commonly diagnosed can-
cer in Latina females, with an incidence rate of 89.2 per
100,000. Although a leading cancer, Latina breast
cancer rates were statistically signiﬁcantly less than
those of both non-Hispanic white females (SIR = 0.64)
and non-Hispanic black females (SIR = 0.79).
Colon cancer
Colon cancer was the second most common cancer
diagnosed among Latina females (26.3 per 100,000)
and the third most common cancer diagnosed among
Latino males (35.7 per 100,000). Incidence rates for
both Hispanic males and females were substantially
lower, however, than those of non-Hispanic males
and
females,
a
difference
that
was
statistically
signiﬁcant.
Cervical cancer
The incidence rate for cervical cancer was 1.8 times
higher among Latina females than non-Hispanic white
females, a statistically signiﬁcant difference, but was
equivalent to the incidence rate among non-Hispanic
black females.
Bladder cancer
With the exception of non-Hispanic black males
(SIR = 1.17, 95% CI = 1.12, 1.22), Hispanic incidence
rates for bladder cancer were statistically signiﬁcantly
lower than those in non-Hispanic populations.
Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL)
A common cancer in Hispanics, NHL incidence rates

than their non-Hispanic white counterparts, but slightly

Discussion
These data indicate that cancer incidence for all cancer
sites combined is substantially lower among Latino
males and Latina females than among non-Hispanic
white and non-Hispanic black males and females, due
largely to lower incidence of prostate, lung and bron-
chus, breast, and colon cancers. However, for speciﬁc
cancers including gallbladder, liver, stomach, cervical,
penile, testicular, thyroid, and brain cancers and NHL,
there are patterns of increased incidence among His-
panic populations as compared to non-Hispanic white
and/or non-Hispanic black populations. These differing
patterns of incidence for total and speciﬁc cancers have
been noted previously in studies of the epidemiology of
cancer in Hispanic populations; however, the compar-
isons have been mainly to non-Hispanic white popu-
lations [7–10]. Seeing several distinct patterns when
comparing the cancer experience of Hispanic popula-
tions to non-Hispanic black as well as non-Hispanic
white populations (i.e., Hispanic rates higher than non-
Hispanic rates; Hispanic rates higher than non-His-
panic white but equivalent to non-Hispanic black;
Hispanic rates intermediate between non-Hispanic
white and non-Hispanic black) gives added dimension
to considering the etiology of and prevention strategies
for these cancers in these populations.
One pattern seen in these data is the increased
incidence of liver and gallbladder cancers in Latino
males and Latina females and of penile cancer in La-
tino males. Although the greatest excess for these
cancers is seen in comparison to non-Hispanic white
males and females, Hispanic rates are also higher than
those of non-Hispanic black males and females. A
variety of tumors occur in the liver, but the most
commonly diagnosed type is hepatocellular carcinoma
(HCC). Unlike many cancers, there are well-estab-
lished risk factors for HCC, including chronic infection
with hepatitis B and C viruses, alcohol consumption
and cigarette smoking, dietary exposure to aﬂatoxins,
low intake of antioxidant vitamins and selenium, high
intake of iron, long-term ingestion of inorganic arsenic
and occupational exposure to thorotrast and vinyl
chloride [11, 12]. For gallbladder cancer among North
American populations in general and Mexican Amer-
icans in particular, the single strongest risk factor
identiﬁed to date is a history of cholesterol gallstones, a
condition more prevalent in women than men. How-
ever, the risk factors for these stones (obesity, multiple
pregnancies,
low
plasma
high
density
lipoprotein
(HDL), female hormones and insulin resistance) do
not account for all observed incidence, leaving a pos-
sible role for genetic susceptibility. In addition, there is
accumulating evidence for a role for chronic infection
in the development of gallbladder cancer [13, 14].
Studies of the etiology of penile cancer generally
implicate sexual transmission of an infectious agent,
with human papillomavirus (HPV) a likely candidate
[15]. The elevated rates of both penile and cervical
cancers are consistent with that hypothesis. Higher
rates of liver, gallbladder and penile cancers in His-
panics are likely due to a higher prevalence in this
population of one or more of the identiﬁed risk factors.
A second interesting pattern seen in these data was
that for stomach and cervical cancers. For these can-
cers, incidence rates are higher in Latinos than in non-
Hispanic white populations but lower or equivalent to
those in non-Hispanic black populations. In recent
years, biological as well as epidemiological evidence
has clearly established the role of HPV as the sexually
transmitted agent in the etiology of cervical cancer, to
the point of meriting public health action, possibly
through HPV testing and vaccination in high risk
populations [16, 17]. Similarly, an infectious agent,
Helicobacter pylori, has been implicated in the etiology
of stomach cancers within the last decade [18, 19].
Higher infection rates for both HPV and H. pylori
have been noted in black and Hispanic populations
when compared to white populations [20, 21].
A ﬁnal pattern noted in these data was for those
cancer sites where the highest rate was seen among
non-Hispanic white males and females, the lowest rate
was among non-Hispanic black males and females, and
an intermediate rate was seen for Hispanic males and
females. The cancers exhibiting this incidence pattern
included bladder, brain, melanoma, testicular, thyroid
and NHL. Risk factors for these cancers, where known,

are quite diverse and include a mix of environmental
and occupational factors [22–26]. Interestingly, how-
ever, there is a consistent socio-economic gradient
seen, with higher income associated with higher risk,
for brain, NHL and testicular cancers; this gradient
may also exist for thyroid cancers, but is less well
established.
While NHIA is the best standardized method
available to date for identifying Hispanic ethnicity
indirectly
in
population-based
cancer
surveillance
programs, some limitations should be noted. The
indirect method of the algorithm relies heavily on the
Spanish surname list from the 1990 census as well as
birthplace and other information (such as maiden
name) recorded in the medical record. In general, use
of surname without additional information such as self-
reported ethnicity, maiden name, or birthplace may
result in misclassiﬁcation. Use of the surname list may
not be appropriate for populations in areas of the US
that were once occupied by Spain and have adopted
Hispanic surnames but are not actually Hispanic by
inheritance. Similarly, someone with a reported birth-
place of a country of largely Hispanic population may
not personally have a Hispanic heritage. Additionally,
the surname portion of the algorithm has been found
to be overly sensitive in some counties having sparse
Hispanic populations. NHIA has since been modiﬁed
such that registries can tailor the surname portion to
address this problem for counties that meet a set
threshold for percent Hispanic population. The mag-
nitude of the misclassiﬁcation resulting from these
scenarios, however, is likely to be small and should not
overly impact the interpretation of these data. One
purpose of studying cancer incidence by race/ethnicity
is to reveal differences in disease risk among distinct
populations and, ultimately, to identify risk factors
associated with different cultures. NHIA aids in this
goal by capturing individuals living in a heavily His-
panic environment as the ones of interest when esti-
mating the cancer burden of Hispanics.
Despite some potential limitations in the algorithm,
the use of a standardized approach to the identiﬁcation
of Hispanic/Latino individuals in population-based
cancer registry data for a large proportion of the US
Hispanic/Latino population is a strength of this study.
The size of the Hispanic/Latino population included in
the study area should result in more reliable estimates
of cancer incidence than has been available previously.
As these data suggest, the pattern of cancer incidence
among the Hispanic population differs sharply from
that of other racial groups in the United States. Cancer
prevention among the Hispanic population will require
culturally-relevant
strategies
that
can
address
a
complex mix of environmental, cultural and socio-
economic risk factors. Of particular interest is the
higher incidence among Hispanic men and women of
cancers having an infectious etiology. This could be a
reﬂection of either the impact of recent immigrants on
this population or the overall lower socio-economic
conditions of this segment of the US population—or
both. The picture will be clearer, and more complex,
when the uniform NAACCR algorithm for assigning
Hispanic ethnicity to population-based cancer inci-
dence data begins to provide national cancer statistics
for Hispanic subgroups with potentially unique etiol-
ogies (e.g. Mexican, Cuban, South and Central Amer-
ican, Puerto Rican) and for Hispanic populations in
differing regions of the United States.
