TITLE: Changes in cancer incidence patterns among a northeastern American Indian population: 1955-1969 versus 1990-2004

. . . . . Cancer . . . . .
Changes in Cancer Incidence Patterns Among
a Northeastern American Indian Population:
1955-1969 Versus 1990-2004
Martin C. Mahoney, MD, PhD;1,2 Puthiery Va, MS;1 Adrian Stevens, MPH;3 Amy R. Kahn, MS, CTR;4
and Arthur M. Michalek, PhD1,2
ABSTRACT: Purpose: This manuscript examines shifts
in patterns of cancer incidence among the Seneca Nation
of Indians (SNI) for the interval 1955-1969 compared to
1990-2004. Methods: A retrospective cohort design was
used to examine cancer incidence among the SNI during 2
time intervals: 1955-1969 and 1990-2004. Person-years at
risk were multiplied by cancer incidence rates for New
York State, exclusive of New York City, over 5-year
intervals. A computer-aided match with the New York
State Cancer Registry was used to identify incident
cancers. Overall and site-speciﬁc standardized incidence
ratios (SIRs = observed/expected × 100), and 95%
conﬁdence intervals (CIs), were calculated for both time
periods. Results: During the earlier interval, deﬁcits in
overall cancer incidence were noted among males (SIR =
56, CI 36-82) and females (SIR = 71, CI 50-98), and for
female breast cancers (SIR = 21, CI 4-62). During the
more recent intervals, deﬁcits in overall cancer incidence
persisted among both genders (males SIR = 63, CI 52-77;
females SIR = 67, CI 55-80). Deﬁcits were also noted
among males for cancers of the lung (SIR = 60, CI 33-98),
prostate (SIR = 51, CI = 33-76) and bladder (SIR = 17,
CI = 2–61) and among females for breast (SIR = 33, CI =
20-53) and uterus (SIR = 36, CI = 10-92). No cancer
sites demonstrated increased incidence. Persons ages
60-69 years, 70-79 years, and ages 80+ years tended to
exhibit deﬁcits in overall incidence. Conclusions: Despite
marked changes over time, deﬁcits in overall cancer
incidence have persisted between the time intervals
studied. Tribal-speciﬁc cancer data are important for the
development and implementation of comprehensive cancer
control plans which align with local needs.
S
tudies examining cancer incidence
patterns among American Indians (AIs) have
generally been limited to data for restricted
time intervals and/or have been restricted
to selected geographic regions which either
have a large numbers of AIs and/or a population-based
cancer registry.1-9
While some commonalities exist among members
of the 500-plus federally recognized tribal groups, each
community embodies a unique set of experiences,
environments and cultures resulting in distinct patterns
of illness and deaths.10 Compared to the majority
population, American Indians tend to be younger, less
educated, have more poverty, and lower household
incomes,11 as well as a lower life expectancy.12
Despite a population of American Indians and
Alaska Natives (ANs) which exceeds 4 million in the
United States,13 a relatively limited number of
publications have examined cancer patterns. Large
databases maintained by the Indian Health Service
(IHS) and the Surveillance, Epidemiology and End
Results (SEER) program of the National Cancer
Institute can be used to study incidence patterns.
However, IHS data are limited to AIs who are eligible
for and receive Indian Health beneﬁts, while SEER is
limited to selected geographic regions. In recent years
IHS records have been linked to state-based cancer
registries through the National Program of Cancer
Registries to correct racial misclassiﬁcation. In general,
cancer patterns for speciﬁc AI groups are difﬁcult to
compare due to tribal heterogeneity, variable sizes of
tribal groups, and variable time periods examined.
The present manuscript examines shifts in patterns
of cancer incidence within the Seneca Nation of Indians
(SNI) for the interval 1955-1969 compared to 1990-2004.

Methods
Study Cohort. The cohort consisted of enrolled
members of the Seneca Nation of Indians who were
alive during any of the study years, which substantially
expanded the Seneca cohort beyond that in an earlier
study.4 The Seneca are members of the Iroquois
Confederacy, which ranks as the 9th largest tribal group
in the United States.11
While the concept of “rural” deﬁes description
based on a single deﬁnition, features such as
geographic location, population size, and density of
inhabitants can be reviewed to characterize a
community.14 The SNI maintain 2 reservation territories
in southwestern New York State. These reservations are
located about 50 miles (the Cattaraugus Reservation)
and 70 miles (Allegany Reservation) from the nearest
urbanized area in Buffalo, N.Y., and the majority of SNI
members reside on or in proximity to these reservation
areas. The current population (2000 Census) on each of
these reservation areas is fewer than 2,500 persons,
which is consistent with the US Census Bureau
classiﬁcation as rural. Population density is 69.9
persons per square mile on Cattaraugus and 30.2
persons per square mile on Allegany.
Study Design. We utilized a retrospective cohort
design to examine patterns of cancer incidence during
1955-1969 and during 1990-2004. While cancer
incidence among the SNI was assessed between 1955
and 2004, we focused on incidence during the periods
1955-1969 and 1990-2004 so as to contrast cancer
patterns during two distinct time intervals. These
intervals avoided the inclusion of calendar years during
the 1970s and 1980s when the Seneca greatly expanded
the availability of reservation-based medical centers. A
dynamic cohort was used with individual cohort
members added through births and removed at time of
death. Membership was based on tribal roll book
listings maintained by the Tribal Clerk’s Ofﬁce. This
research was approved by the SNI and also by our local
institutional review board.
Each cohort member contributed 1 person-year to
the appropriate sex and age category for each complete
year of survival and 0.5 person-years during their year
of birth or year of death. Endpoints for person-year
calculations were: (1) date of death (or age 99 years, if
date of death was unknown), or (2) survival thru
December 31, 2004. Sex-speciﬁc person-year totals for
the study cohort were accumulated over ten 5-year
intervals (1955-1959, 1960-64, 1965-1969,. . ., and
1995-2004) and were partitioned into 5-year age
categories (0-4 years, 5-9 years,. . ., 80-84 years, and
85-99 years). Cohort members who developed a
malignancy during the study period continued to
contribute person-years at risk, allowing for the
potential development of multiple primary
malignancies.
Person-years at risk among the study cohort were
multiplied by corresponding sex-speciﬁc, age-speciﬁc,
site-speciﬁc and calendar year-speciﬁc cancer incidence
rates for New York State, exclusive of New York City.
Expected cancers were calculated during 5-year periods
based on the mid-point of each interval (eg, calendar
year 1957 for the interval 1955-1959, and 2002 for the
interval 2000-2004), based on data from the New York
State Cancer Registry (NYSCR) and Census
enumerations of the state population, exclusive of New
York City, during corresponding years. Expected cancer
cases were summarized across each of the three 5-year
time intervals of each 15-year period (eg, 1955-1969 =
1955-1959, 1960-1964 and 1965-1969).
Incident cancers among the SNI were identiﬁed
through a computer match of cohort members with
data ﬁles maintained by the NYSCR during each of the
years included in the study. Cancer has been a
reportable condition in New York State since 1940,
when the NYSCR was established. Matching criteria
included last name, ﬁrst name (ﬁrst 4 characters),
gender, year of birth (±5 years) and county of
residence. All possible matches were manually checked
for conﬁrmation using other available data and allowed
for accurate determination of true matches. During the
study interval, cancer cases were classiﬁed at the
NYSCR using various versions of the International
Classiﬁcations of Diseases (ICD) (eg, ICD-7, ICD-8,
ICD-9, ICD-10 and ICD-oncology, 2nd and 3rd editions).
The NYSCR provided information on incident cancers
diagnosed among cohort members using the
topography, histology and behavior codes from the
ICD for Oncology, Third Edition, US Interim Version
2000.15
Analyses. Standardized incidence ratios (SIRs)
(based on the ratio of [observed cases/expected cases]
multiplied by 100), were calculated for the intervals
1955-1969 and for 1990-2004, along with 95% conﬁdence
intervals (CIs) based on a Poisson distribution.16 A
point estimate was considered to differ signiﬁcantly
from expected if its 95% CI did not include 100.

person-years of observation during the 1955-1969
interval and 83,266 person-years of observation during
the 1990-2004 interval. The shape of the population

(n = 26, 22%), colorectal cancers (n = 19, 16%) and

comparable data on cancer incidence patterns among
AIs dating back to the earlier period. The cancer deﬁcits
during earlier years have generally been attributed to
consumption of a native diet, natural living and
premature deaths. Throughout the 1970s and 1980s the
Seneca experienced considerable positive changes,
including the emergence of reservation-based medical
centers. Interestingly, data from national surveys noting
greater rates of cancer risk factors among AIs would
have suggested elevated cancer incidence.11,17,18
A prior examination of cancer incidence patterns
among the Seneca Nation from 1955-1984, noted
signiﬁcantly lower overall cancer incidence for both
genders, as well as for cancers of the lung and bladder
among males and among females for cancers of the
colon, breast, and bladder.4 Deﬁcits in lung cancer
incidence were noted among males for both study
intervals, along with a deﬁcit of male bladder cancers
between 1990 and 2004, which might suggest lower
rates of smoking compared to the general population of
New York State. In contrast, the incidence of lung
cancer among Seneca females did not differ from
expected. Deﬁcits in lung cancer incidence have been
noted among male but not among female AIs,5,8,9
although others have noted lung cancer deﬁcits among
both genders.6,19,20 Also, considerable regional variation
in AI and AN lung cancer incidence patterns has been
acknowledged.17,20,21 Data on health behaviors among
the SNI are not well described. National survey data
show that current smoking rates among AI/AN exceed
that of non-Hispanic whites17,18 but may not be
representative of the Seneca.
Disease patterns for cancer sites amenable to early
detection did not follow any clear pattern. Colorectal
cancer incidence among the SNI did not vary
signiﬁcantly by time period or gender. National
surveys indicated that rates of colorectal cancer
screening among AI/ANs lag behind those for
non-Hispanic whites during more recent calendar
years.17,18 Similar to the observation of breast cancer
deﬁcits in other AI groups5,6,8,17,19 deﬁcits in breast
cancer incidence were noted among the SNI for both
time intervals. Breast cancer incidence rates vary by age
group, race/ethnicity, and multiple other risk factors
such as family history, reproductive factors (eg, late age
at ﬁrst live birth), breastfeeding history, menstrual
history (eg, early age at menarche, late menopause),
obesity, level of exercise, alcohol consumption, and use
of exogenous hormones.22,23 While detailed information
on fertility and reproductive patterns are not available,
US Census data suggest that current fertility rates
among AI/AN women are higher compared to
non-Hispanic white women11 which may partially
explain the lower rate of breast cancer. Contemporary
rates of mammography among AI/AN women (69%
within the last 2 years) tend to be slightly lower than
among white women (76%).17
The SIR for prostate cancer increased from 36 (n = 2
cases) to 51 (n = 24 cases) between the 2 time periods.
While this suggests an apparent difference, it was not
large enough to be statistically signiﬁcant. National
data indicate that AI/AN males tend to complete
prostate speciﬁc antigen testing less often compared to
non-Hispanic white males (49% vs 58%, respectively).17
There is some evidence to suggest the occurrence of
more advanced disease among Seneca during the more
recent years. Unfortunately, staging data was not
available for the earlier period. Decreased prostate
cancer incidence has also been observed in other AI
groups.5,6,8,17,19,24
Females demonstrated an excess of liver cancer
between 1990 and 2004. While hepatobiliary cancers
have been generally increased among AI
populations,3,5,6,8-10,17,25 it is difﬁcult to assess the
signiﬁcance of this observation in the present study
based on a limited number of cases. Multiple risk
factors for liver cancer have been identiﬁed and the
presence of these risk factors among these cases is
unknown. Moreover, it is possible that some of these
cases could have represented metastatic disease.
This study was unable to assess information on
cancer risk behaviors, lifestyle exposures,
environmental exposures, genetic factors and/or
occupational exposures. While the robust size of the
Seneca Nation facilitated tribal-speciﬁc analyses,
interpretation of several of the point estimates for
cancer incidence remains a challenge, especially across
gender, time interval and site groupings. Since the
NYSCR did not include cases among New York City
residents before 1976, it is possible that incident cancer
cases may have been under-reported prior to statewide
case reporting. Unique strengths of this study were the
use of tribal roll books to identify tribal members thus
eliminating the issues of racial misclassiﬁcation, the
sizable number of person-years of observation accrued
and matching the cohort against NYSCR records; also
the NYSCR is a longstanding member of the National
Program of Cancer Registries. The retrospective cohort
design is particularly well suited to this application.
The deﬁcits in cancer noted among the SNI in older
age categories is noteworthy since the analytic
approach adjusts for differences in the age distribution
of the study population despite increasing numbers of
persons reaching age 50 years and beyond during the
1990-2004 study interval. It remains possible that cohort
members, who were at high risk of eventually
developing cancer, could have succumbed to other

Observational studies are important in documenting
health disparities and aid in focusing resource
allocation and/or policy changes to improve health
outcomes. Approaches emphasizing both primary
prevention (eg, smoking prevention/cessation, healthy
body weight) and secondary prevention (eg, screening)
are likely to generate signiﬁcant changes in disease
outcomes as well as improvements in quality of life.
Tribal-speciﬁc data on cancer patterns are important for
the development and implementation of
comprehensive cancer control plans focused on
primary, secondary and tertiary cancer prevention
strategies which align with local needs.
