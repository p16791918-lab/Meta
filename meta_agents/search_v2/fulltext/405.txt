TITLE: A trend analysis of breast cancer incidence rates in the United States from 2000 to 2009 shows a recent increase

BRIEF REPORT
A trend analysis of breast cancer incidence rates in the United
States from 2000 to 2009 shows a recent increase
Ningqi Hou • Dezheng Huo
Received: 21 November 2012 / Accepted: 28 January 2013 / Published online: 28 February 2013
 Springer Science+Business Media New York 2013
Abstract
Recent reports have shown that the breast cancer
incidence rate in the US stabilized after a sharp reduction in
2002 and 2003. It is important to continue monitoring breast
cancer incidence rates according to age group, race/ethnic-
ity, estrogen receptor (ER) status, and tumor stage. Age-
standardized breast cancer incidence rates were calculated
using data from the surveillance, epidemiology, and end
results 18 registries from 2000 to 2009, for 677,774 female
breast cancer patients aged 20 and above. Jointpoint
regression models were used to ﬁt a series of joined straight
lines on a log scale to annual age-standardized rates. The
incidence rates of all breast cancer signiﬁcantly increased for
non-Hispanic blacks from 2005 to 2009 (annual percentage
change,
APC = 2.0 %,
p = 0.01)
and
Asian/Paciﬁc
Islanders from 2000 to 2009 (APC = 1.2 %, p = 0.02).
Since 2004, incidence rates in women aged 40–49 years
signiﬁcantly increased for most racial/ethnic groups (overall
APC = 1.1 %, p = 0.001). The incidence rate of carcinoma
in situ signiﬁcantly increased in all racial/ethnic groups, with
an APC range from 2.3 to 3.0 % (p \ 0.005). The localized
breast cancer incidence signiﬁcantly increased in non-His-

2005 (APC by race: non-Hispanic whites 1.5 %, non-His-
panic blacks 4.3 %, Asian/Paciﬁc Islanders 1.7 %, and
Hispanics 1.8 %; all p values \0.05), while ER-negative

non-Hispanic whites—3.9 %, non-Hispanic blacks—3.7 %,
Asian/Paciﬁc Islanders—1.5 %, and Hispanics—4.3 %; all
p values \0.05). Recently the incidence of breast cancer
appears to be increasing in certain subgroups, including ER-
positive, early-stage breast cancers, in particular among non-
Hispanic blacks and Asian/Paciﬁc Islanders. Further studies
are warranted to examine possible reasons for these changes,
such as changes in mammography screening methods and
risk factors prevalence.

Background
There was a sharp decrease in breast cancer incidence from
2002 to 2003 in the United States, evident in white
women C50 years of age and for estrogen receptor (ER)
positive tumors. This has been attributed to the reduced use
of hormone replacement therapy (HRT) after the July 2002
publication from the Women’s Health Initiative [1–4].
Following the sharp decrease, it was reported that a
decreasing trend continued into 2005 [5, 6]. The most
recent publications observed that the decline in cancer
incidence ceased [7], and the rate stabilized from 2004 to
2008 [8], with ER-positive breast cancer being projected to
increase [9]. In this brief report, we examined the breast
cancer incidence rates from 2000 to 2009 in the United
States, using the updated data from 18 registries of the
Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER)
program. We present the rates by age group, race/ethnicity,
estrogen receptor status, and stage of tumor, in order to
inform the underlying reasons for the changes.

Statistical analysis
We restricted the analysis to women aged 20 years or
above, and categorized age groups as 20–39, 40–49, 50–69,
and 70? years. We categorized race/ethnicity subgroups as
non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, Asian/Paciﬁc
Islander, Hispanic, and others (data not shown due to small
sample size).
Estrogen receptor status was missing for nearly 20 % of
the records. Since hormone receptors are important bio-
markers for breast carcinoma and a large portion of missing
data can be a major source of bias in rate calculation, we
employed the multiple imputation (MI) method to account
for the missing values of ER status. Using the chained
equation methods [11, 12], we generated 15 complete
datasets with ER, progesterone receptor status, age, race/
ethnicity, histological type, grade, SEER registry, year of
diagnosis, tumor size, number of positive lymph nodes,
SEER historic stage, vital status, and survival time in the
imputation models.
Age-adjusted incidence rates were calculated based on
the 2000 US standard population. We used the Joinpoint
Regression Program 3.5.4 to ﬁt a series of joined straight
lines on a log scale to the trends of annual, age-adjusted
rates, with the ﬁtted slopes indicating annual percentage
change (APC) [13]. All statistical analyses were performed
using STATA 12.0, with the exception of the use of SAS
9.3 to read in the original SEER datasets.
Results
The combined SEER registries from 2000 through 2009
included 677,774 female breast cancer patients 20 years or
older (Table 1). The proportion of missing ER status has
decreased from 33.9 % in 2000 to 10.0 % in 2009. Figure 1
shows the trend of incidence rates of all breast cancer in the
United States between 2000 and 2009, stratiﬁed by race/
ethnicity. The rate for non-Hispanic whites decreased sig-
niﬁcantly from 246.9 per 100,000 in 2000 to 228.8 per
100,000 in 2005 (APC = -1.8 %, p = 0.01), but then rose
to 240.6 per 100,000 in 2009 (APC = 1.3 %, p = 0.11). The
incidence rate for non-Hispanic blacks was stable between
2000 and 2005 (APC = 0.2 %, p = 0.7), but signiﬁcantly
increased from 206.0 per 100,000 in 2005 to 224.9 per
100,000 in 2009 (APC = 2.0 %, p = 0.01). Throughout the
decade, the incidence rate increased signiﬁcantly for Asian/
Paciﬁc Islanders (APC = 1.2 %, p = 0.02), but remained
stable for Hispanic women (APC = 0.41 %, p = 0.3).
Taking tumor stage into consideration, Fig. 2 shows that
the incidence of in situ breast cancer signiﬁcantly increased
in all racial/ethnic groups while for non-Hispanic whites, the
rate only increased after 2005 (APC ranges from 2.3 to 3.0 %
by racial/ethnic group, p values \0.01). Localized breast
cancer signiﬁcantly increased in non-Hispanic blacks
(APC = 1.3 %, p = 0.004) and Asian/Paciﬁc Islanders
(APC = 1.2 %, p = 0.03). Regional and distant cancers
signiﬁcantly decreased in non-Hispanic whites from 2000 to
2004 (APC = -2.5 %, p = 0.02) and in Hispanics from
2000 to 2009 (APC = -1.1 %, p = 0.006).

For both non-Hispanic blacks and Asian/Paciﬁc Island-
ers, the overall increase in breast cancer incidence appears
to be driven by an increase in early-stage diseases (in situ
and localized breast cancer), while the incidence of late-
stage (regional and distant) breast cancer remained stable
through the decade of 2000–2009. The discordant trend
observed in non-Hispanic whites is possibly due to the
marked reduction in the use of hormone replacement
therapy in this racial group [14]. For Hispanics, although
the incidence of all breast cancer appears stable between
2000 and 2009, the incidence of early-stage breast cancer
signiﬁcantly increased, while the incidence of late-stage
breast cancer signiﬁcantly decreased.
The trends for breast cancer subtype split in opposite
directions, with ER-positive tumor incidence increasing
while ER-negative tumor incidence decreasing in almost
all age and racial/ethnic groups in recent years. This
observation conﬁrms the trend reported by earlier studies
[7, 9]. Although the decreasing rate of ER-negative breast
cancer is considerable and signiﬁcant in all age groups,
ER-breast cancer remains the highest among the blacks
reported in this and previous studies [15]. On the other
hand, the ER-positive cancer rate is increasing the fastest in
blacks compared to all other racial groups, regardless of
age. Therefore, it is likely that the molecular characteristics
of breast cancer are shifting among the blacks, with fewer
triple-negative
subtype
cancers diagnosed
over
time.
However, comparing to other racial/ethnic groups, non-
Hispanic blacks have the highest regional/distant breast
cancer incidence throughout the decade, suggesting they
still have the worst prognosis.
It is possible that the changes in incidence rates, espe-
cially estrogen receptor positive breast cancer, are due in
part to improvements in cancer screening methods and,
therefore, advance in early detection. The full-ﬁeld digital
mammography has replaced screen-ﬁlm mammography in
most breast screening programs [16]. Digital mammogra-
phy has better sensitivity [17] particularly in detecting ER-
positive and early-stage breast cancer such as ductal car-
cinoma in situ [18, 19], and was reported to be more
accurate in women under the age of 50 years [20]. On the
other hand, however, it is unlikely that the overall trends of
incidence rates are due to changes in mammography
screening rate since mammography use has not substan-
tially changed from 2000 through 2010 [21–23], although it
has increased by large magnitudes in small groups with
growing populations, such as new immigrants and Asian
Americans [23]. Moreover, the U.S. Preventive Service
Task Force stopped recommending routine screening for
women age 40–49 in November 2009 [24, 25], which
means the data we show here were not inﬂuenced by this
guideline change. Increases in the incidence rate of
ER? tumor in women younger than 40 may reﬂect that the
risk factor proﬁle in younger generation have shifted over
the years, but we could not postulate an exact reason.
This study has several strengths and limitations. We
conducted a descriptive analysis of population-based can-
cer incidence rates from 18 SEER registries with high
quality data, representing 28 % of the US population. We
imputed ER status because there is signiﬁcant proportion of
missing data in this variable, particularly for older patients
and minorities. The database was large enough for us to do
subgroup analysis by multiple factors including age of
diagnosis, racial/ethnicity, stage of tumor, and ER status,
providing detailed information on incidence trends in small
subgroups. As discussed above, part of the incidence
changes may be explained by different screening technol-
ogy, yet we have limited information to quantify this
inﬂuence. Moreover, the incidence rates of 2001–2009
were based on populations extrapolated from the 2000 US
Census, so the error in the extrapolation might bias the rate
estimates. Finally, interpretation of recent breast cancer
trends is limited since this study is descriptive only.
In conclusion, with the updated SEER registry data up to
2009, the recent incidence of breast cancer appears to be
increasing in certain subgroups, including non-Hispanic
blacks, Asian/Paciﬁc Islanders, early-stage breast cancer,
and estrogen receptor positive breast cancer. Further
studies are warranted to examine possible reasons for these
changes, such as changes in mammography screening
methods and risk factors prevalence.
