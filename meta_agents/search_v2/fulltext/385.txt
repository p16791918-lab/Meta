ABSTRACT 
Introduction.  Cancer is the leading cause of death among 
Asian American, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander 
(AA&NHPI) populations, who are among the most diverse 
and rapidly growing racial/ethnic groups in the United 
States. Despite this population’s diverse socioeconomic, 
cultural, and immigration backgrounds, little is known 
about the within-group disparities in breast cancer stages at 
diagnosis. This study aims to identify AA&NHPI subgroups 
at heightened risk for advanced breast cancer by analyzing 
detailed incidence rates and diagnostic stages across these 
diverse subgroups.
Methods.  Patient data spanning from 2004 to 2020 were 
extracted from the National Cancer Database, focusing on 
individuals newly diagnosed with breast cancer. Individuals 
were disaggregated into specific AA&NHPI subgroups by 
self-reported country of origin. Ordinal logistic regression, 
adjusting for clinical and sociodemographic factors, was 
used to calculate adjusted odds ratios (AORs). Higher 
adjusted odds ratios signify more advanced stage diagnoses.
Results.  Of 3,001,173 patients with breast cancer, 99,128 
(3.30%) identified as AA&NHPI. Compared with NHW 
patients, AA&NHPI patients were younger (median age, 
NHW: 62 years versus AA&NHPI: 57 years, p < 0.001) and 
more likely to be uninsured (NHW: 1.50% vs. AA&NHPI: 
2.60%, p  <  0.001). The largest AA&NHPI subgroups 
were Filipino (n = 21,666, 21.9% of AA&NHPI), Chinese 
(n = 21,303, 21.5%), Indian/Pakistani (n = 20,295, 20.5%), 
Vietnamese (n = 6680, 6.74%), and Korean (n = 7776, 
7.84%). In aggregate, AA&NHPI patients were less likely 
to present with advanced stage at diagnosis relative to 
NHW patients (AOR 0.89, 95% confidence interval [CI] 
0.88–0.90, p < 0.001). However, AA&NHPI disaggre-
gation demonstrated that Hawaiian (AOR 1.15, 95% CI 
1.09–1.22, p < 0.001), Hmong (AOR 1.70, 95% CI 1.28–
2.27, p < 0.001), Laotian (AOR 1.37, 95% CI 1.17–1.61, 
p < 0.001), and Pacific Islander patients (AOR 1.24, 95% 
CI 1.17–1.32, p < 0.001) were more likely to present at a 
more advanced stage relative to NHW patients. Relative to 
Chinese Americans (the reference AA&NHPI group), all 
AA&NHPI ethnic groups had higher odds of advanced-
stage diagnosis with the highest odds in Hmong (AOR 2.41, 
95% CI 1.81–3.20, p < 0.001), Laotian (AOR 1.89, 95% CI 
1.61–2.20, p < 0.001), Pacific Islander (AOR 1.71, 95% CI 
1.60–1.82, p < 0.001), and Hawaiian (AOR 1.57, 95% CI 
1.48–1.67, p < 0.001) patients.

Conclusions.  Our study characterizes disparities in the 
stage of breast cancer at presentation across disaggregated 
AA&NHPI groups, with notably advanced diagnoses in 
patients of Hmong, Laotian, Pacific Islander, Hawaiian, and 
Kampuchean descent. These findings underscore the need 
for disaggregated research exploring both risk factors and 
barriers to early diagnosis, as well as culturally tailored 
interventions that involve community leaders and leverage 
cultural norms to improve cancer screening and diagnosis.
Cancer remains the leading cause of death for Asian 
American and Pacific Islander (AA&NHPI) populations 
in the United States, accounting for more than 25% of all 
deaths.1 The AA&NHPI population, encompassing 22 mil-
lion individuals and 7% of the U.S. population, is one of 
the most diverse and fastest-growing groups in the nation.2 
This heterogeneity spans ethnic, cultural, historical, and 
socioeconomic dimensions, with roots spanning the Indian 
subcontinent and East and Southeast Asia, each with distinct 
immigration histories and patterns.2 Notably, the greatest 
wealth gap of any group in the United States lies within the 
AA&NHPI community, with a median household income of 
$119,000 among Indian Americans compared with $44,000 
among Burmese Americans.3 Disparities are evident in edu-
cational attainment as well, with 9% of Bhutanese Ameri-
cans having a college degree, in contrast to 56% of Korean 
Americans.4 This great heterogeneity in social determinants 
of health may hold profound implications for the incidence, 
detection, and management of cancer within AA&NHPI 
subgroups.
AA&NHPI patients with breast cancer exhibit distinct 
patterns of disease compared with their non-Hispanic White 
counterparts owing to social determinants of health as well 
as unique genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors.5 
Young Chinese, Japanese, and Korean Asian Americans 
exhibit higher risks of hormone receptor-positive breast 
cancer compared with non-Hispanic White Americans, for 
example.6 Despite significant progress in characterizing 
breast cancer patterns in the AA&NHPI population, research 
has largely treated them as a monolithic group.
Recent studies have underscored the importance of dis-
aggregating data to understand disparities in diseases, such 
as prostate, lung, colon, cervical, and skin cancers, among 
AA&NHPI populations.7-11 This approach, which examines 
data by specific subgroups rather than broad racial catego-
ries, highlights the unique characteristics and needs of these 
diverse groups. Although increasingly recognized, histori-
cally, many studies have only analyzed data by race, which 
does not fully capture the diversity within the AA&NHPI 
communities. Moving toward a more detailed disaggrega-
tion can uncover important disparities in stage of disease at 
presentation.
METHODS
Data Source and Patients
The National Cancer Database (NCDB) is a nationwide, 
hospital-based cancer registry with a methodology that has 
been described previously. The extensive sample size of the 
NCDB allows for the analysis to be broken down by specific 
Asian American populations.12,13
Our study evaluated a retrospective cohort analysis of 
female patients diagnosed with breast cancer between 2004 
and 2020. The demographic scope of our study encom-
passed female patients self-identifying as non-Hispanic 
White, Thai, Asian Indian or Pakistani, Chinese, Vietnam-
ese, Korean, Japanese, Filipino, Hawaiian, Laotian, Kam-
puchean, or Hmong. Of note, Indian and Pakistani were 
grouped together, as early years in the NCDB did not disag-
gregate these distinct subgroups. Because of the relatively 
small sample sizes of certain subgroups, we combined sev-
eral Pacific Islander categories—including Micronesian, 
Chamorro, Guamanian, Polynesian, Tahitian, Samoan, Ton-
gan, Melanesian, Fiji Islander, and New Guinean—into a 
single analytical cohort, while we recognize the broad diver-
sity and cultural differences within this subgroup.14 Patients 
of Hawaiian descent were analyzed separately owing to a 
sufficiently large sample size within this subgroup.
Clinical and Sociodemographic Covariates
The outcome of interest was cancer stages at diagno-
sis, ranging from 0 (preinvasive/DCIS) to 4 (metastatic). 
We adjusted all models for clinical and sociodemographic 
covariates, including race/ethnicity, age, treatment facility 
type, median household income from the patient’s ZIP code 
as a socioeconomic status indicator, the percentage of adults 
without a high school diploma in the patient’s ZIP code as an 
educational attainment measure, Charlson–Deyo comorbid-
ity score, insurance status, and diagnosis year. For income 
and education, we divided the data into quartiles according 
to the 2012 American Community Survey, corresponding to 
each patient’s ZIP code.
Statistical Analysis
Pearson’s chi-squared test and Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum 
test were used to compare categorical and continuous vari-
ables between different racial subgroups. Ordinal logistic 
regression was used to analyze the odds of patients pre-
senting with increasingly advanced stages of breast cancer, 
adjusting for quantitative and categorical independent vari-
ables. We calculated two sets of adjusted odds ratios (AORs) 
and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). In the first model, we 
compared AA&NHPI racial subgroups to non-Hispanic

White patients to assess their likelihood of presenting with 
more advanced cancer stages. In the second model, focusing 
on the AA&NHPI subgroups, we used Chinese patients as 
the reference group, because they are the largest AA&NHPI 
subgroup in the United States. The entire population was 
categorized by receptor status—HR+ with HER2−, HER2+ 
regardless of estrogen/progesterone receptor status, and 
triple negative—and the analysis was then rerun for each 
group. We conducted these analyses by using Stata version 
16.1 (StataCorp, College Station, TX) and R statistical soft-
ware. The institutional review board exempted this study, 
because it used deidentified data.
RESULTS
Baseline Characteristics
Table 1 depicts clinical and sociodemographic char-
acteristics of NHW patients and aggregated AA&NHPI 
subgroups. Of the 3,001,173 patients included in the 
study, 99,128 were in the aggregate AA&NHPI group. 
AA&NHPI patients were younger relative to NHW patients 
(median age, NHW: 62 years vs. AA&NHPI: 57 years, 
p < 0.001). AA&NHPI patients had higher rates of Med-
icaid (12% vs. 4.8%, p < 0.001), private insurance (60% 
vs. 52%, p < 0.001), and uninsured status (2.6% vs. 1.5%, 
p < 0.001) compared with NHW patients, whereas Medi-
care coverage was lower among AA&NHPI patients (23% 
vs. 40%, p < 0.001; Table 1). While NHW patients were 
most frequently treated at a Comprehensive Community 
Cancer Program (41%), AA&NHPI patients most often 
received treatment at an academic or research center (36%, 
p < 0.001). AA&NHPI patients were more represented in 
the highest zip-code level income bracket compared with 
their NHW counterparts (54% vs. 36%, p < 0.001). How-
ever, AA&NHPI patients were also more often residing in 
zip codes with low high school graduation rates (16% vs. 
11% for NHW, p < 0.001). Additionally, AA&NHPI patients 
were slightly more likely to present with zero comorbidi-
ties (86% vs. 85%, p < 0.001). The largest AA&NHPI sub-
groups were Chinese (n = 21,303, 21.49% of AA&NHPI), 
Filipino (n = 21,666, 21.86%), Indian/Pakistani (n = 20,295, 
20.47%), Vietnamese (n  =  6680, 6.74%), and Korean 
(n = 7776, 7.84%). In aggregate, AA&NHPI patients pre-
sented more frequently with stage 0 disease (AA&NHPI: 
24% vs. NHW: 19%, p < 0.001).
Table 2 presents a comparison of baseline characteristics 
of NHW and AA&NHPI groups, disaggregated into specific 
ethnic populations. Hmong and Laotian patients presented 
with stage 4 disease most frequently among all populations 
studied (9.3% and 6.9% respectively; Fig. 1). Hmong and 
Laotian patients were diagnosed at the youngest median age 
(52 and 53, respectively). Hmong (24%) and NHW (27%) 
patients were least likely to be treated in an academic or 
research program (p < 0.001 for all comparisons).
Hmong (21%) and Laotian patients (28%) were least 
likely to live in the highest zip-code level income bracket, 
whereas Hmong (35%) and Kampuchean (35%) patients 
were most likely to live in zip codes with low rates of high 
school education. Hawaiian patients were the least likely to 
have no documented comorbidities (75%). Indian/Pakistani 
patients had the highest rate of uninsurance (4.5%), while 
Kampuchean patients had the highest rate of Medicaid (29%) 
(p < 0.001 for all comparisons).
Additionally, any hormone receptor status, HER2-posi-
tive was highest in Kampuchean (15%) and Laotian (13%) 
patients (p < 0.001). Hormone receptor-positive, HER2-neg-
ative was most prevalent among Hawaiian (34%), Japanese 
(30%), and NHW (30%) patients (p < 0.001). Triple-nega-
tive breast cancer rates were highest among Hmong (6.8%) 
and Laotian (7.5%) patients (p < 0.001).
Disparities in Stage at Presentation
Taken in aggregate, AA&NHPI patients were less likely 
to present with advanced stage at diagnosis (AOR 0.89, 95% 
CI 0.88–0.90, p < 0.001) relative to NHW patients (Sup-
plementary Table 1). However, individual AA&NHPI popu-
lations displayed disparities in stage at presentation upon 
disaggregation.
In the primary analysis comparing AA&NHPI groups 
to non-Hispanic White patients (Table 3), Chinese (AOR 
0.70, 95% CI 0.69–0.72, p  <  0.001), Filipinos (AOR 
0.90, 95% CI 0.88–0.93, p < 0.001), Japanese (AOR 0.78, 
95% CI 0.76–0.81, p < 0.001), Koreans (AOR 0.93, 95% 
CI 0.90–0.97, p = 0.001), and Vietnamese (AOR 0.90, 
95% CI 0.86–0.95, p < 0.001) were less likely to pre-
sent at advanced stages compared with non-Hispanic 
White patients (Fig. 2). Conversely, Hawaiian (AOR 1.15, 
95% CI 1.09–1.22, p < 0.001), Hmong (AOR 1.70, 95% 
CI 1.28–2.27, p < 0.001), Laotian (AOR 1.37, 95% CI 
1.17–1.61, p < 0.001), and Pacific Islander (AOR 1.24, 95% 
CI 1.17–1.32, p < 0.001) patients were more likely to pre-
sent at advanced stages compared with non-Hispanic White 
patients (Table 3).
In the subgroup analysis with Chinese patients as the 
referent, all analyzed subgroups were more likely to pre-
sent at advanced stages compared with Chinese patients 
(Table 3; Fig. 3). The five ethnic groups with the high-
est adjusted odds ratios (AOR) were: Hmong (AOR 2.41, 
95% CI 1.81–3.20, p < 0.001), Laotian (AOR 1.89, 95% 
CI 1.61–2.20, p < 0.001), Pacific Islander (AOR 1.71, 95% 
CI 1.60–1.82, p < 0.001), Hawaiian (AOR 1.57, 95% CI 
1.48–1.67, p < 0.001), and Kampuchean (AOR 1.45, 95% 
CI 1.25–1.68, p < 0.001).

DISCUSSION
In this study of 3 million female patients with breast 
cancer, we found increased odds of advanced diagnosis for 
Hawaiian, Hmong, Laotian, and Pacific Islander populations 
in stage at presentation even after adjusting for a range of 
clinical and socioeconomic factors, including income, edu-
cation, and comorbidity scores. Disaggregation revealed that 
all studied groups were more likely to present with a more 
advanced stage than Chinese patients, who served as the 
reference AA&NHPI group.
Our findings align with previous studies on disparities in 
breast cancer stages among aggregated AA&NHPI popula-
tions. Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) 
studies found that Japanese patients were more likely to be 
diagnosed at earlier stages compared with non-Hispanic 
white patients, for example.15,16 Our work expands these 
findings to smaller groups, such as Hawaiian, Hmong, Lao-
tian, and Pacific Islanders, who are often overlooked in 
research.17-20

Insights from a focus group study with 66 Samoan church 
members highlight the effectiveness of culturally aligned, 
church-based cancer awareness programs.38 The study found 
strong support within the Samoan community for training 
pastors to frame cancer not as a spiritual illness but as a 
physical ailment that can be treated. This approach shifts the 
perception of cancer away from being seen as an incurable 
spiritual weakness. Future interventions for Pacific Islander 
communities can learn from this model by involving spir-
itual and community leaders in health messaging. Leverag-
ing the community’s hierarchical structure and deep spiritual 
beliefs, collaborations with religious institutions and exist-
ing community structures can enhance the acceptance and 
impact of health programs.
Finally, focus groups with Native Hawaiian cancer 
survivors highlighted the importance of Native Hawaiian 
traditions, such as support from friends, family, commu-
nity members, and spiritual beliefs, in coping with cancer 
diagnosis and treatment.39 The success of the PILI ‘Ohana 
Project (POP), a community-based participatory research 
initiative addressing obesity and diabetes disparities, dem-
onstrates the effectiveness of culturally adapted health 
programs. POP utilized images of familiar cultural foods, 
recruited community peer educators, and emphasized family 
and community involvement.40
Community-level initiatives, such as cost-free screen-
ing services, mobile clinics, and educational programs, can 
enhance access for those facing financial or logistical chal-
lenges. For example, the Hawai’i Breast and Cervical Cancer 
Control Program (BCCCP) significantly increased screening 
rates among Micronesian women from 18% to 90% through 
tailored educational materials and personalized outreach.41 
Clinically, it is essential to prioritize cultural competency 
training for healthcare professionals and enhance diversity 
in physician training to better serve AA&NHPI patients.42 
Additionally, hospitals should train more interpreters to 
ensure patients and families can effectively communicate 
their beliefs and values.43 On a federal level, interven-
tions should include compensation for lost wages and costs 
incurred for screenings. Furthermore, investing in broadband 
infrastructure and programs like the Affordable Connectivity 
Program is crucial to improving telehealth access.44

eral ethnicities under the Pacific Islander umbrella term, as 
well as the grouping together of Indian/Pakistani popula-
tions, which may mask critical cultural and socioeconomic 
differences within these diverse subgroups. Second, this 
study cannot establish causality with regards to the drivers 
of disparities in breast cancer stage at diagnosis owing to its 
retrospective nature. Third, nativity status may be associated 
with cancer outcomes, as has been shown for many immi-
grant groups in California but is unfortunately not available 
in the NCDB.23 Lastly, while our study accounts for several 
covariates, including income, education, and comorbidity 
scores, full clinical details and important qualitative factors 
including illness experiences and caregiving responsibilities 
were not available; thus, our findings may not encompass the 
full spectrum of factors involved in within-group differences.
In conclusion, our study provides important insights 
with regards to the complex relationship between the coun-
try of origin and breast cancer stage at presentation among 
AA&NHPI subgroups. The identified disparities emphasize 
the urgent need for targeted and culturally tailored inter-
ventions to ensure equitable access to timely breast cancer 
screening, detection, and treatment. Finally, we draw atten-
tion to the need for sustained research on the diverse cultural, 
environmental, biological, and socioeconomic forces driving 
within-group disparities. In combination with the delivery 
of comprehensive, holistic interventions, this approach may 
alleviate the high burden of breast cancer among marginal-
ized AA&NHPI communities.
