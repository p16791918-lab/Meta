TITLE: Inter- and intra-ethnic differences for female breast carcinoma incidence in the continental United States and in the state of Hawaii

Epidemiology
Inter- and intra-ethnic diﬀerences for female breast carcinoma incidence
in the continental United States and in the state of Hawaii
Megan Fong1,2, Donald E. Henson3, Susan S. Devesa4, and William F. Anderson4
1School of Public Health and Health Sciences, George Washington University, Washington, DC, USA; 2Papa Ola
Lokahi, Honolulu, Hawaii, USA; 3The Oﬃce of Cancer Prevention and Control, The George Washington University
Cancer Institute, Washington, DC, USA; 4Descriptive Studies Section, DHHS/NIH/NCI/DCEG/BB, Rockville, MD,
USA
Key words: age-speciﬁc incidence rates, age frequency distribution, breast carcinoma, histopathology, hormone
receptor expression, racial diversity
Summary
Background. Ethnic diversity is well-documented for female breast carcinoma incidence in the continental US but is
not so well-established in the state of Hawaii.
Methods. Using the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program, we analyzed n=323,607
in situ and invasive female breast cancer cases for major ethnic groups in the continental US and in Hawaii,
diagnosed during the years 1992–2002.
Results. In the continental US, age-speciﬁc incidence rate patterns and prognostic factor proﬁles were good-risk
for Asian or Paciﬁc Islanders (API), intermediate for Whites, and poor-risk for Blacks. For example, early age-
at-onset, high nuclear grade, aggressive histopathologic subtypes, and hormone receptor negative expression was
associated with Black race in the continental US. In Hawaii, age-speciﬁc rate and prognostic proﬁles were more
favorable for API than for White women, albeit not so striking as in the continental US.
Conclusion. We observed inter- and intra-ethnic diﬀerences for female breast carcinoma in the continental US
and in the state of Hawaii. While inter-racial disparities were expected, intra-racial diﬀerences were somewhat
unexpected and possibly due to variations in racial subgroup mixing and/or cultural assimilation. For example, API
women with breast carcinoma in the continental US included 96.03% Asians and 2.4% Paciﬁc Islanders. In
contrast, API women with breast carcinoma in Hawaii included 76.52% Asians and 23.46% Paciﬁc Islanders.
Moreover, APIs were more likely to be ﬁrst-generation migrants in the continental US (92%) than in Hawaii
(34%). Future studies should attempt to disaggregate racial data to separately characterize epidemiological
patterns for individual ethnic groups.
Introduction
In the continental US and in the state of Hawaii, breast
carcinoma is the most common tumor diagnosed in
women and is the second-leading cause of cancer death
in females [1]. Previous national studies have shown
racial diﬀerences in breast carcinoma incidence and
prognosis, including the tendency for certain non-White
minority women to be diagnosed with more advanced
stages of breast carcinoma and to experience lower rates
of survival after diagnosis [2,3].
While trends in ethnic breast carcinoma disparity are
well-established for women in the continental US, fewer
studies have examined population-based data speciﬁc to
the state of Hawaii. Hawaii also has fewer Hispanic
Whites as well as higher concentrations of several Asian
and Paciﬁc Islander subgroups; and therefore, it may be
useful to compare populations in Hawaii with those
observed in the continental US.
Speciﬁcally,
this
study
described
ethnic-related
patterns in breast carcinoma incidence and survival for
the following groups; White, Black, and API women
in the continental US as well as White and API
women in Hawaii. For each ethnic group, breast car-
cinoma incidence was described according to standard
demographics, tumor characteristics, and age distri-
bution at diagnosis (rates and frequency). Actuarial
survival
and
mortality
hazard
function
also
were
compared between the groups as a metric of outcome
data, providing insight into biologic phenomenon,
eﬃcacy of cancer treatment, and post-treatment sur-
veillance. Additionally, we examined racial subgroup
composition and place of birth for each major ethnic
group.

Materials and Methods
We utilized the National Cancer Institute’s (NCI’s)
SEER Cancer Incidence Public-Use 13 Registry Data-
base (November 2004 submission) to analyze breast
carcinoma cases, diagnosed during the years 1992 to
2002 [4]. One record each for 323,607 female in situ and
invasive breast cases was associated with population
data for White, Black, American Indian/Alaska Native
(AI/AN), and Asian or Paciﬁc Islanders (API). We
stratiﬁed the major ethnic groups from the combined
registries in the continental US and the one registry in
the state of Hawaii. Ethnic groups in the continental US
were White (n=265,733), Black (n=27,412), and API
(n=16,518). Ethnic groups in Hawaii were White
(n=2995) and API (n=7390). There were not enough
Black (n=56) or AI/AN (n=28) women in Hawaii for
analysis, Table 2.
Data on standard demographics and tumor charac-
teristics related to the American Joint Committee for
Cancer (AJCC) staging system [5] (i.e., tumor size,
axillary lymph node status) as well as characteristics re-
lated to biologic features (i.e., grade, histopathology,
hormone receptor expression) were dichotomized into
low-risk and high-risk groups. For example, tumor size
was categorized as £ 2.0 cm (low-risk) versus >2.0 cm
(high-risk); lymph node status was negative (low-risk)
versus positive (high-risk); tumor grade was low (grades
1–2) versus high (grades 3–4); estrogen receptor (ER) and
progesterone receptor (PR) expression were categorized
separately as positive (low-risk) or negative (high-risk).
Histopathologic subtypes were identiﬁed using codes
from the International Classiﬁcation of Diseases for
Oncology-3rd edition (ICD-O-3) [6–8]; i.e., duct carci-
noma not otherwise speciﬁed (Duct NOS, ICD-O-3 code
8500), tubular carcinoma (ICD-O-3 codes 8211), lobular
carcinoma (ICD-O-3 codes 8520), medullary carcinoma
(ICD-O-3 codes 8510), inﬂammatory carcinoma (ICD-
O-3 code 8530), papillary carcinoma (ICD-O-3 codes
8050, 8260, 8503), mucinous carcinoma (ICD-O-3 codes
8480), and Paget’s disease (ICD-O-3 codes 8540–8543).
Other, missing, or unknown data were classiﬁed as
‘other or unknown’.
Incidence
Age-adjusted incidence rates (2000 US standard popu-
lation) were calculated using SEER *Stat 6.1.4 and
expressed per 100,000 woman-years. Relative risks for
tumor characteristics were expressed as incidence rate
ratios (RR) with 95% conﬁdence intervals, where the
incidence rate for a given racial characteristic was com-
pared to a referent characteristic among White women in
the continental US with an assigned RR of 1.0. Using
SPLUS 6.2, age-speciﬁc incidence rate curves by 5-year
age increments were plotted on a log–log scale [9,10]. The
density function in SPLUS 6.2, which utilizes a smooth-
ing method of the corresponding age-at-diagnosis fre-
quency histogram, was used to construct age frequency
density plots by 1-year age increments [11,12]. Brieﬂy, the
vertical axis for each density plot represented the pro-
portion of cases diagnosed with breast carcinoma at the
corresponding age-at-diagnosis on the horizontal axis.
Survival and mortality
The Kaplan–Meier product-limit method was used to
estimate breast cancer-speciﬁc survival, stratiﬁed by
major ethnic groups [13]. Survival curves for each ethnic
group were compared with the log rank test [14]. Percent
annual breast cancer-speciﬁc hazard (or mortality)
function was calculated with SEER Stat 6.1.4 using
expected survival to compute the crude probability of
death [15]. The hazard function is deﬁned as a condi-
tional rate of breast cancer-speciﬁc death at a given time
interval following breast cancer diagnosis, provided that
the subject was alive at the beginning of the time interval.
Results
Incidence rates and rate ratios (RR)
In the continental US, median ages at diagnosis ranged
from a high of 62 years for White women to a low of
54 years for API women (Table 1). Median tumor size
ranged from 2.0 cm for Black women to 1.5 cm for
White women. In Hawaii, median ages were 59 and
61 years for Whites and APIs, respectively. Median
tumor sizes were 1.5 cm for both groups.
Overall age-adjusted incidence rates ranged from a
high of 178.1 per 100,000 woman-years for White
women in Hawaii to a low of 104.1 per 100,000 woman-
years for API women in the continental US. Incidence
rates were higher in Hawaii than in the continental US
among both White and API racial groups. For example,
the incidence rate for Whites in Hawaii was 7.5% higher
than for Whites in the continental US (178.1 compared
to 165.6 per 100,000 woman-years). More strikingly, the
incidence rate for APIs in Hawaii was 41% higher than
for APIs in the continental US (147.2 compared to 104.1
per 100,000 woman-years).
Duct carcinoma not otherwise speciﬁed was the most
common histopathologic subtype among all ethnic
groups, ranging from a high of 76% for APIs in Hawaii
to a low of 64% for Whites in the continental US.
Compared to the White referent group in the conti-
nental US, duct NOS and tubular carcinomas were most
common among Whites in Hawaii (RR=1.2 and 1.9),
respectively. On the other hand, lobular and comedo-
carcinomas were most common among Whites in the
continental US. Blacks had the highest rates for med-
ullary (RR=2.0) and inﬂammatory (RR=1.4) breast
carcinomas. Papillary tumors were most common for
Blacks and APIs in Hawaii, i.e., RR=1.5 for both
groups. Notably, mucinous tumors were most common
among APIs in Hawaii (RR=1.2) and least common
among continental US APIs (RR=0.8).

Discussion
This analysis of SEER data for n=323,607 in situ and
invasive breast carcinoma cases identiﬁed inter- and
intra-ethnic disparities (i.e., between and within racial
groups) in the continental US and in the state of Hawaii,
including diﬀerent age distributions at diagnosis (rates
and frequency), tumor proﬁles, histopathologic sub-
types, cancer-speciﬁc survival and mortality hazard
function. Incidence tumor characteristics and prognostic
patterns were most favorable for APIs in the continental
US and worse for Blacks in the continental US. In
general, Whites in the continental US had intermediate
risk and prognostic proﬁles.
In the continental US, age-speciﬁc incidence rates for
White and Black women followed a pattern that has
been well-established [12,16]. Rates for Whites increased
rapidly until age 50 years then continued to rise at a
slower rate. Rates were higher for Blacks than for
Whites until ages 40–44 years, after which there was
ethnic crossover with rates higher for Whites than for
Blacks [17]. Rates for APIs in the continental US in-
creased rapidly until age 50 years then ﬂattened, similar
to patterns in low risk countries [18].
Divergent age-speciﬁc incidence rate patterns for
diﬀerent racial groups in the continental US reﬂected
diﬀerent breast cancer populations, as demonstrated
with density plots. For example, Whites in the conti-
nental US had bimodal breast cancer populations with a
predominant late onset mode; whereas Blacks and APIs
in the continental US had predominantly early-onset
modes of approximately age 50 years. In Hawaii, density
plots for Whites demonstrated a predominant early-on-
set component, i.e., similar to Blacks in the continental

The inter-ethnic diﬀerences between racial groups in
this study were expected and may be the product of
non-biologic as well as biologic factors. For example,
inter-racial disparity for tumor size, nodal status and
AJCC stage at diagnosis are often attributed to non-
biologic factors such as access to and/or utilization of
medical care [19,20]. However, if denied or delayed
access to health care were the sole determinant for
worse or later stage at diagnosis for Blacks compared
to all other racial groups, Black women should develop
breast tumors later (not earlier) in life. In other words,
delayed diagnosis should be associated with older (not
earlier) ages at diagnosis [21,22]. So, biologic determi-
nants may also play a role for inter-racial disparity
[3,23–26].
In contrast to the expected inter-ethnic diﬀerences
between racial groups, intra-ethnic diﬀerences within
major racial groupings were somewhat unexpected, but
probably
reﬂected
fundamental
diﬀerences
within
SEER’s major racial groupings. For example, White
women in the continental US included 8.2% Caucasians
of Hispanic origin (n=21,894 of 265,733) compared to
less than 1% Hispanic origin (n=18 of 2995) in Hawaii
(Table 2). Given that Hispanics have lower breast can-
cer risk than non-Hispanic Whites [27], Hispanics in the
continental US may have reduced overall rates among
Whites in the continental US compared to Whites in
Hawaii. API women with breast carcinoma in the con-
tinental US included 96.03% Asians (27% Chinese,
16% Japanese, and 27% Filipino) and 2.4% Paciﬁc
Islanders. In contrast, API women with breast carci-
noma in Hawaii included 76.5% Asians (9% Chinese,
49% Japanese, and 14% Filipino) and 23.46% Paciﬁc
Islanders. In accordance with their low-risk incidence
rate pattern [28], API women in the continental US also
appeared to be largely ﬁrst-generation migrants with less
than 8% (1,313 of 16,518) recorded as born in the
continental US. On the other hand and consistent with
their high-risk ‘westernized’ incidence rate pattern, API
women in Hawaii were mostly subsequent-generation
immigrants with 66% (4867 of 7390) listed as born in the
state of Hawaii. Other potentially important variables
such as socioeconomic status or educational level were
not recorded in the SEER database.
In sum, we observed inter- and intra-ethnic diﬀerences
for female breast carcinoma for White and API races in
the continental US and the state of Hawaii. While the
inter-racial variations were consistent with previous
studies, the intra-racial disparities may reﬂect geographic
variations for heterogeneous non-biologic and biologic
factors among racial subgroups in SEER, which were
combined into larger groups and then analyzed together.
Future studies should attempt to disaggregate data to
separately characterize incidence and prognostic patterns
for racial groupings. Accomplishing this goal will require
the NCI’s continued commitment for large-scale popu-
lation-based
data
sets
with
ethnic
diversity.
Over
sampling for some racial subgroups may be needed to
ensure adequate representation within SEER. This eﬀort
is warranted given the implications for prevention, early
detection, and treatment of breast carcinoma for the
special populations in the United States.
