BACKGROUND. Among the many diverse Asian ethnic groups living in the US,
Cambodian immigrants comprise a small fraction (1.8%) of the total Asian popu-
lation. Because of their small numbers, Cambodian vital statistics are often com-
bined into Southeast Asian (SA) cancer data consisting of Vietnamese, Thais,
Laotians, and Hmong.
METHODS. The 2000 Census counts were used for 2 Cambodian populations,
Cambodians alone and Cambodians alone and in combination with any other
racial/ethnic group for California and for Seattle (Puget Sound area), Washington.
Then the cancer incidence rates were calculated using cancer cases from the
California and Puget Sound cancer registries between 1998–2002. The 1998–2002
annual age-adjusted incidence rates, upper bound rates (based on the Cambo-
dian alone population), lower bound rates (based on the Cambodians alone or in
combination population) are reported and compared with the rates in the
non-Hispanic White (NHW) population in these regions.
RESULTS. The top 5 cancers in Cambodian males are lung and bronchus, liver,
prostate, colorectal, and stomach cancers. The sites where the rates are higher in
male Cambodians than NHW males are (in ascending rank) nasopharynx, liver,
stomach, myeloma, and lung and bronchus. The top 5 cancers for female
Cambodians are breast, lung, colon and rectum, cervix, and thyroid. The sites
where female rates are greater than NHW female rates are (in ascending rank)
nasopharynx, liver, stomach, cervix uteri, oral cavity, and thyroid.
CONCLUSIONS. The challenges to address the health issues of Cambodians are
complicated by historical events that caused their emigration to the US. Many of
the immigrants are survivors of the holocaust in Cambodia. Health programs for
Cambodians must deal with the consequences of these issues as well as cultural
issues of language and religion in helping Cambodians to reduce their cancer
disparities. Cancer 2007;110:1370–5.  2007 American Cancer Society.

n 1975, Pol Pot and the Khmer Rouge communist regime came to
power in Cambodia. Their reign lasted less than 5 years, but it is
estimated that 1.5 to 3 million of the 7 million population were
killed.1 The 1984 movie The Killing Fields depicted these events.
From 1983 to 1985, a large number of Cambodian refugees emi-
grated to the US.2 As 1 of the more recent Asian immigrant groups,
Cambodian refugees make up only 1.8% of the total Asian popula-
tions.3 The majority resettled in California, Washington, Texas,
Pennsylvania, and Massachusetts, where they can either be reunited
with their families and friends and/or where US refugee programs
were available to assist them.2–4

MATERIALS AND METHODS
The number and type of cancer cases for many Asian
ethnicities, such as Cambodians, are routinely col-
lected by SEER cancer registries since 1988. In our
study we used cancer cases for Cambodians diagnosed
from 1998–2002 for the state of California and the
SEER Puget Sound cancer registry for the state of
Washington. A total of 581 cases of cancers were diag-
nosed in California and Puget Sound, Washington
between 1998 and 2002, including 308 male cases and
273 female cases. However, the age-specific popula-
tions needed to calculate age-adjusted cancer rates are
generally only available from decennial Census data.
We used the 2000 Census data to determine the age-
specific populations for Cambodians. Then we multi-
plied the 2000 Census age-specific population counts
by 5 to cover the 1998–2002 years of cancer cases.
The cancer cases from California and Puget
Sound cancer registries represent 94.5% of all the
cancer cases for Cambodians in the 13 SEER regis-
tries. Data on cancer sites with 3 or more cases were
reported. Thus, the data from these 2 registries repre-
sent the best estimate of their rates. All rates are age-
adjusted to the US 2000 standard population.
The cancer incidence rates for NHW and Asian
and Pacific Islanders for the California and Puget
Sound cancer registries were also determined for the
period 1998–2002. The ratio of the Cambodian age-
adjusted rate to the age-adjusted rate for NHWs for
the same sex was determined for each cancer site
using the upper bound estimate of the rate. The rank
order of the ratios where the Cambodians had higher
rates than NHWs was also calculated.

DISCUSSION
Our study indicates the important cancers for Cam-
bodians in the US. We now discuss these cancers in
terms of their global distribution, etiology, and some
cancer prevention and control activities associated
with the top cancers for Cambodians.
Nasopharynx Cancer
Nasopharynx cancer is a relatively rare malignancy in
most countries; however, peoples from southeast Asia,
China, northeast India, North Africa, and Inuits (Eski-
mos) of Canada have higher risks.11–13 Males are more
likely to have the disease than females with an M:F ra-
tio of 2.3:1 in the general population.11 Our incidence
rates had an M:F ratio of 3.2:1 in favor of males. Com-
mon causes of this malignancy include environment,
such as dust and smoke, and lifestyle, with consump-
tion of salted and preserved foods.11 Epstein-Barr virus
(EBV) is well-documented in nasopharyngeal carci-
noma. Its viral influence and impact on the predispo-
sition to cancer have yet to be elucidated.12 In our
study, nasopharyngeal cancer was the twelfth leading
cancer for Cambodian men (8.6/100,000, 14 times
greater than the rate for male NHW rate) and the
fifteenth cause for Cambodian women (2.7/100,000,
14 times greater than the female NHW rate).

Thyroid
At the global level, thyroid cancer is more prevalent
among females in developing countries than among
females in the developed countries, 60 cases per 1000
versus 44 cases per 1000, respectively.11 Family his-
tory of thyroid cancer, endocrine abnormality, goiter,
and iodine or seafood intake are possible factors in
the malignancy.15,16 Haselkorn et al.16 proposed that
the consumption of cruciferous vegetables, antioxi-
dant vitamins, and phytoestrogens may provide pro-
tective effects against thyroid cancer. In our study,
Cambodian thyroid cancer was the fifth leading can-
cer in Cambodian women (14.4/100,000, 1.3 times
higher than the rate for female NHWs), whereas it
was the lowest incidence for Cambodian men.

Myeloma
A malignancy of plasma cells in the bone marrow
has a higher incidence rate in African-Americans
than the White and Asian population, affecting more
males than females. Some possible factors contribut-
ing to myeloma are chemicals and occupational
exposures,
autoimmune

or

and hepatitis.11,17 In our study melanoma was the
thirteenth leading cause of cancer for Cambodian
men (7.8/100,000, 1.2 times greater than male NHW
rate) and not reported for Cambodian women.
Lung and Bronchus
Lung cancer is 1 of the leading causes of cancer
death among men and women in the US. More than
50% of the lung cancer cases are caused by cigarette
smoking.18 Recently, incidence rates seem to have
decreased in men and increased in young women
due to an increase in tobacco use observed in that
young group.10 Among young Cambodian females
the prevalence of tobacco use is also rising, whereas
it is falling among Cambodian males.10 In this study,
lung and bronchus was the top cause of cancer in
Cambodian men (87.1/100,000 but 1.1 times the rate

Cultural Issues in Addressing Cancer Prevention and
Control Activities for Cambodians
Cambodians have unique cultural issues that must
be dealt with in developing cancer education pro-
grams for this population. Many immigrants from
Cambodia were affected by the genocide led by Pol
Pot, the leader of the Khmer Rouge (Cambodian
Communist Party) from 1975 to 1979. Millions of
Cambodians were tortured and executed by the Pol
Pot regime. The Pol Pot holocaust left a devastating
country of orphans, poverty, famine, and corruption.
As survivors from the ravaging war countries, the
Cambodian immigrants have been labeled with psy-
chological problems such as depression and post-
traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).22 These medical
terms are quickly used by medical practices, and the
lack of cultural sensitivity for immigrants who expe-
rience political repression is often missed.2 The Cam-
bodian immigrants do not usually talk about their
war experiences during family or social functions,
unless one raises the question. In 1 instance, a Cam-
bodian lady who lost her husband and children to
Pol Pot’s execution blocks the traumatic experience
from her memories so that she can cope with her
daily life activity. Thus, it is important to realize
these issues in working with Cambodians.
This problem is not the only cultural factor that
needs to be considered in dealing with the health of
Cambodians. Cambodian history dates back to the
Angkor era, where King and Buddhism dictated that
Cambodian civilization had a fixed social status.
Born into royal families or the upper class is a bles-
sing in which power for ruling the kingdom was
approved by God. It is unthinkable for a peasant to
rise above the status into which he/she was born.
This is her/his fate, and they should live prosper-
ously to accumulate good merit for the next life.23
This culture of fate, acceptance, and endurance
of life makes Cambodians stand apart from other
societies. It plays a major role in the downfall of
Cambodians’ politics and their kingdoms throughout
their history. Total obedience is the ultimate disci-
pline; silence is the golden rule that not only is
taught at an early age but has also been a survival
method to escape the punishment inflicted by the
Khmer Rouge.2 This ‘silence’ is often misunderstood
and misinterpreted by many foreigners as a charac-
teristic of being indifferent, passive, noncompliant,
and even not intelligent.23
To design and implement healthcare awareness
for health disparities in this population is a chal-
lenge. It is even more challenging when one must
deal with the Cambodian language, culture, and his-
torical events to address these complex issues. The
Cambodian immigrants have come a long way, sur-
viving the traumatic experiences under the Pol Pot
regime to a new culture that most of them are not
accustomed to. They are the ‘survivors’ of political
repression. It is this fundamental survival skill that
Western medical teams need to understand and sym-
pathize with, and to provide a healthcare system that
is geared to the needs of this special group.
