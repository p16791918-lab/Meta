BACKGROUND. The Hmong are an isolated, agrarian people who settled in the
mountainous regions of what today are Vietnam, Cambodia, and Laos. After the
Vietnam War, many Hmong were relocated to the U.S. Minnesota has the second
largest population (after California) of Hmong individuals. The objective of this
study was to examine cancer incidence in this population, because it may indicate
areas for targeted surveillance and intervention.
METHODS. The Minnesota Cancer Surveillance System database was screened for
Hmong surnames, and proportional incidence ratios (PIRs) were calculated for the
period 1988–1999.
RESULTS. Compared with all Minnesotans, the Hmong population had increased
PIRs for nasopharyngeal cancer (PIR, 39.39; 95% conﬁdence interval [95% CI],
21.01–66.86), gastric cancer (PIR, 8.70; 95% CI, 5.39–13.25), hepatic cancer (PIR,
8.08; 95% CI, 3.88–14.71), and cervical cancer (PIR, 3.72; 95% CI, 2.04–6.20) and
had decreased PIRs for prostate cancer, breast cancer, Hodgkin disease, and
melanoma.
CONCLUSIONS. The current observations have implications for cancer control in-
terventions. In particular, an increased incidence of cervical cancer might be
addressed in part by targeting culturally sensitive screening programs in the
Hmong population. Cancer 2003;97:3076–9. © 2003 American Cancer Society.

KEYWORDS: Hmong, cancer, incidence, Minnesota.
T
he Hmong, an isolated agrarian people from southern China,
migrated to the mountainous regions of what today are Vietnam,
Cambodia, and Laos.1 At the conclusion of the Vietnam War, many
Hmong were relocated from refugee camps in Thailand to the U.S.
and settled in New Jersey, Texas, Wisconsin, California, and Minne-
sota. Since the 1980s, the Hmong population in Minnesota has in-
creased signiﬁcantly. Minnesota has the second highest population
(after California) of Hmong individuals in the U.S. Although there is
some debate regarding the actual number of Hmong individuals who
reside in Minnesota (estimates range from 40,000 to 80,000), recent
data from the 2000 Census indicate that there are approximately
42,000, more than half of whom live in Saint Paul.2
Because of cultural barriers, high rates of poverty, and language
isolation, health status and practices are of interest in this unique
migrant population.3 Reports concerning blood pressure, appendici-
tis, reproductive outcomes, and breast cancer screening practices in
the Hmong have been published.4–8
A single study from California examined cancer incidence in the
Hmong population in California from 1987 to 1994.9 In that report,
compared with all races combined, the Hmong had statistically sig-
niﬁcant higher proportional incidence ratios (PIRs) of gastric, hepatic,

MATERIALS AND METHODS
Using the Minnesota Cancer Surveillance System
(MCSS), we evaluated cancer incidence in the Hmong
population in Minnesota during the 12-year period
from 1988 to 1999. The MCSS has been in operation
since 1988 and serves as the population-based cancer
registry for the state. U.S. 2000 Census data show that
approximately 5 million individuals reside in Minne-
sota, including 88% non-Hispanic whites, 4% African
Americans, 3% Asians, 3% Hispanics, and 2% Native
Americans/other. An external audit of the MCSS per-
formed in June, 1996 by the North American Associa-
tion of Central Cancer Registries estimated that the
MCSS hospital-based case ﬁnding was 99.6% com-
plete for microscopically conﬁrmed cancers and
99.1% complete for all cancers. Screening of the MCSS
database resulted in a list of cancers diagnosed be-
tween 1988 and 1999 among Minnesota residents,
which contained the variables of cancer type (invasive
cancers only), gender, age at diagnosis, and an indi-
cator for Hmong surname (Hmong surnames were
kindly provided by Dr. Paul Mills, who conducted a
similar analysis in California9). The ﬁnal Hmong sur-
name list was more speciﬁc than sensitive; surnames
were excluded that may have included individuals
from other Asian populations.
Because census data on the Hmong population in
Minnesota are considered unreliable, it was impracti-
cal to calculate cancer incidence rates. Therefore, age-
adjusted (20-year groupings: age younger than 20
years, ages 20–40 years, ages 40–60 years, ages 60–80
years, and 80  years) and gender-adjusted PIRs along
with 95% conﬁdence intervals (95% CI) were calculat-
ed.9–12 Brieﬂy, an observed-to-expected ratio was cal-
culated. The observed number was equal the actual
number of Hmong individuals for a particular disease
site. The expected number was calculated by multi-
plying the age speciﬁc (20-year groupings) and gender
speciﬁc numbers of cancers for all sites in the Hmong
population by a quotient that was derived as follows:
the (age/gender speciﬁc) number of the site speciﬁc
cancer in the entire Minnesota population divided by
the corresponding (age/gender) speciﬁc number of all
cancers in the Minnesota population. Conﬁdence in-
tervals were calculated using the methods described
by Ury and Wiggins.12
RESULTS
During 1988–1999, 222,406 incident malignancies
were ascertained by the MCSS. A total of 186 malig-
nancies ( 0.1%) occurred in the Hmong population,
as determined through surnames used previously.9
Eighty-four cancers occurred in males, and 102 can-
cers occurred in females. The age distribution was as
follows: age younger than 20 years, 6%; ages 20–40
years, 23%; ages 40–60 years, 30%; ages 60–80 years,
33%; and age 80  years (8%).

DISCUSSION
Although most of the data are similar to those re-
ported in the Hmong population in California,9 we did
not see an increased proportion of NHL. We also ob-
served a statistically signiﬁcant decreased proportion
of Hodgkin disease: The study in California did not
report on this malignancy.
The proportional occurrence of several of these
malignancies in the Hmong, including nasopharyn-
geal cancer,13 gastric cancer,14 liver cancer,15, breast
cancer,16–18 and prostate carcinoma,16–18 is in agree-
ment with ﬁndings in other Asian populations. It is
noteworthy that gastric, cervical, nasopharyngeal, and
hepatic malignancies all are associated with infectious
exposures (Helicobacter pylori, human papilloma vi-
rus, Epstein–Barr virus, and hepatitis B virus, respec-
tively) that are endemic in certain parts of Asia. Thus,
it is not surprising that the proportion of malignancies
would be higher in Hmong Minnesotans compared
with all Minnesotans.
There are some limitations to the approach used.
PIRs were calculated rather than direct incidence
rates. A limitation of using PIRs is that proportionate
excesses for some sites will be accompanied by pro-
portionate deﬁcits in other sites. It was not possible to

The results of this study may have implications for
the early detection of certain cancers in this popula-
tion. For example, an increased incidence of cervical
cancer might be addressed, at least in part, by target-
ing culturally sensitive screening programs in the
Hmong population. Furthermore, due to the markedly
increased incidence of nasopharyngeal cancer, it will
be important for physicians to consider possible ma-
lignancy when evaluating Hmong patients for signs
and symptoms involving the ear, nose, or throat.19 The
Hmong residing in Minnesota have maintained many
aspects of their culture and primarily are agrarian.
Therefore, as lifestyles of the Hmong change due to
Western inﬂuences, their cancer incidence rates (es-
pecially among descendants) also may change to more
closely reﬂect the cancer incidence rates of the native
U.S. population (for example, both colorectal cancer
and breast cancer incidence rates in the U.S.-born
Japanese population now approach the rates in the
non-Hispanic white population).20,21
