TITLE: Breast Cancer Incidence in Asian American, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander Populations

Invited Commentary | Equity, Diversity, and Inclusion
Breast Cancer Incidence in Asian American, Native Hawaiian,
and Pacific Islander Populations
Gregory S. Calip, PharmD, MPH, PhD; Kent F. Hoskins, MD; Jenny S. Guadamuz, PhD, MSPH
Gomez et al1 report a consequential and overdue finding: Breast cancer incidence is rising across
nearly all examined Asian American, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander populations in the US.
They found especially concerning increases among women younger than 50 years; in distant-stage
disease; and for more aggressive subtypes, including triple-negative, hormone receptor–negative
and ERBB2 (formerly HER2/neu)–positive, and hormone receptor–positive and ERBB2-negative
tumors. Using updated National Cancer Institute Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results data
from 14 states and disaggregating 9 Asian American, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander ethnic
populations, the authors reveal substantial heterogeneity in both incidence levels and trends that
would remain largely obscured if Asian American, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander populations
were examined in aggregate. Their study is, therefore, more than an update on breast cancer
incidence; it is a compelling demonstration that data equity—the principle that all individuals should
benefit from data science—is foundational to cancer surveillance, etiologic research, and an effective
public health response.2-4
For too long, Asian American, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander populations have been
aggregated in ways that flatten important differences in cancer burden.2-4 In reality, this population
is exceptionally diverse, encompassing US-born individuals and immigrants alike who trace their
heritage to dozens of nations, including Indigenous Pacific Islander peoples. Each subgroup carries
distinct migration histories, sociocultural contexts, and socioeconomic profiles that shape their
unique health care access, language needs, and risk factors.2-4 In Gomez et al,1 this heterogeneity is
not subtle. Incidence rates varied more than 3-fold across groups, with particularly high burden
among Native Hawaiian women and rapid recent increases among multiple Asian American
populations, including Asian Indian and Pakistani, Chinese, Korean, and Vietnamese women.
Particularly concerning were the rising trends among women younger than 50 years and the
increasing incidence of distant-stage disease, patterns that underscore the urgency of these findings
for both public health and clinical care.1,5
The importance of disaggregated data extends well beyond a more precise description. Aggrega-
tion can mask risk in communities that may otherwise be presumed to have low cancer burden, under-
cut the visibility of emerging trends, and delay the design of effective interventions.2-4 Incidence pat-
terns, stage at diagnosis, and subtype distributions are especially relevant in breast cancer and can in-
form both etiologic hypotheses and prevention strategies.1,6 For clinicians, the findings from Gomez
et al1 argue against a one-size-fits-all understanding of breast cancer risk in Asian American, Native Ha-
waiian, and Pacific Islander populations. For policymakers and researchers, they reinforce that the cat-
egories used in data systems can either illuminate inequities or conceal them.2-4 More broadly, com-
monly used aggregated descriptions of breast cancer risk in Asian American, Native Hawaiian, and Pa-
cific Islander populations, including in medical education and reference materials, may inadvertently
perpetuate the misconception that these populations are uniformly at lower risk, when in fact,
subgroup-specific incidence patterns are far more heterogeneous.3
At the same time, these trends should not be interpreted simplistically as evidence of an innate
biologic difference across racial or ethnic groups. Race and ethnicity are social and historical
constructs, and caution is warranted in how racial and ethnic differences in cancer incidence are
interpreted.3 Such patterns likely reflect the complex interplay of migration, acculturation, structural

structural conditions likely differ across Asian American, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander
populations and may include variation in insurance coverage, language access, affordability of care,
immigration-related barriers, and other social determinants that influence timely screening,
diagnostic follow-up, and treatment access. Gomez et al1 appropriately note that the rapid incidence
increases observed in some Asian American, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander populations may
mirror patterns seen in parts of Asia and may be associated with changing exposures across birth and
immigration cohorts. The increases in advanced-stage disease also suggest that these patterns are
not explained solely by greater screening.1 Instead, they may point to a more complex reality in which
changing risk exposures and inequities in awareness, screening access, timely detection, and
follow-up are occurring simultaneously.1,7
The findings are especially important in the context of broader concern about early-onset
cancers in the US.5 That nearly all examined Asian American populations showed increasing early-
onset breast cancer trends should prompt greater attention from clinicians, researchers, and cancer
control programs.1 Rising incidence in younger women highlights the need for targeted interventions
to improve breast symptom awareness, strengthen family history assessment, reduce diagnostic
delays, and inform future screening policy discussions, even if the current data are not designed to
answer those questions directly. Likewise, increasing distant-stage incidence among several Asian
American, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander populations raises concern that some communities
may endure barriers to early diagnosis that require culturally and linguistically tailored responses,1
alongside policies that address deep-rooted barriers to care. Previous research showing inequities in
breast and cervical cancer screening among Asian American women has further supported the need
to avoid treating these populations as homogeneous in outreach and prevention efforts.7
The Gomez et al1 study also highlights how far data equity efforts still have to go. The updated
Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results specialized database makes this analysis possible, and
that advance should be recognized. But important limitations remain, including increasing numbers
of cases categorized as not otherwise specified, uncertainty in denominator estimation, sparse
subgroup-specific screening and risk factor data, and the growing complexity of classifying
multiracial populations.1 These challenges are not merely technical. They shape what questions can
be asked, which populations can be seen, and how resources are allocated. More than 2 decades
after federal attention turned to improving data collection and reducing disparities for Asian
American, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander populations, investment has still lagged. Prior work
showed that only a very small proportion of National Institutes of Health clinical research funding
explicitly focused on Asian American, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander populations, helping
explain why important evidence gaps remain.2
The path forward requires more than increased surveillance; it demands the integration of
surveillance, research, and clinical action. To uncover the drivers of these trends, epidemiologic
studies must capture migration histories, such as nativity and arrival cohorts, alongside premigration
and postmigration social and environmental exposures.5 Furthermore, linking these factors to cancer
subtypes and tumor genomic data will be essential to understanding how shifting life course
exposures intersect with and influence tumor biology.1,6 Public health practitioners should prioritize
culturally and linguistically tailored outreach and screening efforts designed with specific Asian
American, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander communities rather than generic approaches for a
broad, aggregated population.3,4 Clinicians and health systems, as well as medical and scientific
educators, should recognize that Asian American, Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Islander populations
are not uniformly low risk and that subgroup-specific patterns may matter for awareness, outreach,
and care delivery. Teaching and reference materials should likewise avoid overly broad, aggregated
statements that obscure clinically meaningful heterogeneity across Asian American, Native Hawaiian,
and Pacific Islander populations. Furthermore, health information systems should continue to
improve disaggregation and harmonization, particularly through more accurate, standardized, and
interoperable capture of detailed race and ethnicity data across electronic health records and cancer
