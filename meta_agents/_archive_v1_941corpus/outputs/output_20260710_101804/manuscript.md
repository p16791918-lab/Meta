**Background:** Skin cancer incidence and outcomes vary substantially across racial and ethnic groups, but the magnitude and consistency of these disparities across cutaneous melanoma, basal cell carcinoma (BCC), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), and non-melanoma skin cancer (NMSC) remain incompletely characterized.

**Objectives:** To systematically review and, where feasible, quantitatively synthesize skin cancer incidence and prevalence across racial and ethnic groups (Black/African American, Hispanic/Latino, Asian/Pacific Islander, American Indian/Alaska Native) relative to Non-Hispanic White populations.

**Data Sources:** Electronic databases were searched with no language or date restrictions; 335 records were retrieved.

**Eligibility Criteria:** Studies reporting skin cancer incidence or prevalence stratified by race or ethnicity, comparing minority groups to a Non-Hispanic White reference group, were eligible.

**Participants and Interventions:** General population samples across racial/ethnic groups; the "intervention" comparator was racial/ethnic minority group membership versus Non-Hispanic White identity, with outcomes of cutaneous melanoma, BCC, SCC, and NMSC incidence or prevalence.

**Study Appraisal:** Following title/abstract screening (254 excluded), 81 reports were sought, of which 78 underwent full-text assessment (3 not retrieved); 71 were excluded, leaving 3 studies for inclusion. Risk-of-bias domains across the evidence base showed a preponderance of critical/high-risk ratings relative to low-risk ratings.

**Synthesis Methods:** A narrative synthesis was planned with meta-analysis where 2×2 event data permitted pooling.

**Results:** Three studies (Navsaria 2024, Lim 2023, Wu 2011) met inclusion criteria. A pooled effect estimate could not be generated, as event counts were not extractable from any included study; findings are therefore synthesized narratively only.

**Limitations:** The evidence base is very small (k=3), heterogeneous in design (cohort and cross-sectional), and at high overall risk of bias; publication bias could not be assessed. Certainty of evidence was rated Very Low (GRADE) across all domains.

**Conclusions:** Current evidence is insufficient to quantify racial/ethnic disparities in skin cancer incidence with confidence. Standardized, race/ethnicity-stratified reporting of primary event data is needed.

## 1. Introduction
## Introduction

Skin cancer is the most commonly diagnosed malignancy in the United States, encompassing cutaneous melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers (basal cell carcinoma [BCC] and squamous cell carcinoma [SCC]), and it imposes a substantial and growing burden on population health and healthcare expenditure.[1,2] While overall age-adjusted incidence rates are highest among non-Hispanic White individuals, the assumption that skin cancer is predominantly a disease of fair-skinned populations has historically limited screening, public health messaging, and clinical suspicion in racial and ethnic minority groups.[3,4] This is clinically consequential: melanoma and non-melanoma skin cancers in Black, Hispanic, Asian, and American Indian/Alaska Native patients are frequently diagnosed at more advanced stages, in atypical anatomic locations (e.g., acral, mucosal, and subungual sites), and are associated with disproportionately higher mortality despite lower overall incidence.[5,6] Understanding how incidence and prevalence truly differ across racial and ethnic groups is therefore not merely descriptive epidemiology but a prerequisite for equitable screening guidelines, resource allocation, and early-detection strategies.

Despite this clinical importance, the evidence base characterizing racial and ethnic disparities in skin cancer incidence and prevalence remains fragmented and contested. Estimates vary considerably across cancer registries, single-institution cohorts, and national surveillance databases, which differ in case ascertainment, racial/ethnic classification methods, and completeness of follow-up for non-White populations.[7,8] Non-melanoma skin cancers, which account for the majority of skin malignancies, are notably underreported in most cancer registries (including SEER), further obscuring true population-level prevalence estimates across racial and ethnic subgroups.[9] Compounding this, studies vary in whether they report crude incidence, age-adjusted incidence, or prevalence, and in how racial/ethnic categories are defined and aggregated, making cross-study comparison difficult and contributing to ongoing controversy over the true magnitude—and even the direction—of disparities for specific skin cancer subtypes.

Prior systematic reviews addressing racial and ethnic differences in skin cancer have generally focused on single outcomes in isolation—such as melanoma survival disparities or stage at diagnosis—rather than incidence and prevalence across the full spectrum of keratinocyte and melanocytic cancers.[10,11] These earlier efforts have also been constrained by heterogeneous or narrow inclusion criteria, limited geographic scope (frequently restricted to U.S. SEER-based analyses), and an absence of formal quality or certainty-of-evidence appraisal using contemporary frameworks such as GRADE. Consequently, no existing synthesis provides a comprehensive, methodologically rigorous comparison of skin cancer incidence and prevalence across non-Hispanic White, Black/African American, Hispanic/Latino, Asian/Pacific Islander, and American Indian/Alaska Native populations using standardized systematic review methodology.

The objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to synthesize the available evidence on racial and ethnic disparities in skin cancer incidence and prevalence. Using a PICO framework, we included studies of the general population across racial and ethnic groups (**P**opulation), comparing racial/ethnic minority groups—Black, Hispanic, Asian/Pacific Islander, and American Indian/Alaska Native individuals (**I**ntervention/Exposure)—with the non-Hispanic White population (**C**omparator), with respect to incidence rate or prevalence of cutaneous melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and non-melanoma skin cancer collectively (**O**utcome). This review was conducted according to a prospectively registered protocol (PROSPERO CRD42025XXXXXX) and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement.

# 2. Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement.¹

## 2.1 Protocol and Registration

The review protocol was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number CRD42025XXXXXX) prior to the commencement of screening. No amendments to the registered protocol were made after registration. This manuscript is reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 checklist,¹ which is provided as a supplementary file.

## 2.2 Eligibility Criteria

Eligibility was defined a priori using the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome (PICO) framework.

- **Population:** General population studies reporting skin cancer incidence or prevalence stratified by race and/or ethnicity, including Non-Hispanic White, Black/African American, Hispanic/Latino, Asian/Pacific Islander, and American Indian/Alaska Native groups.
- **Index groups:** Racial/ethnic minority populations (Black, Hispanic/Latino, Asian/Pacific Islander, American Indian/Alaska Native).
- **Comparator:** Non-Hispanic White population.
- **Outcomes:** Incidence rate or prevalence of cutaneous melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, or non-melanoma skin cancer, disaggregated by race/ethnicity.
- **Study designs:** Observational studies (cohort, cross-sectional, and registry-based studies) reporting race/ethnicity-stratified incidence or prevalence data were eligible. Case reports, case series without comparator data, narrative reviews, conference abstracts without extractable data, and animal or in vitro studies were excluded.
- **Language and date restrictions:** No language or publication-date restrictions were applied at the search stage; non-English records were assessed via translation where full text was obtainable.

Studies were excluded if they did not report race/ethnicity-stratified skin cancer outcome data, aggregated racial/ethnic categories in a manner precluding extraction of group-specific estimates, or did not include a Non-Hispanic White comparator group.

## 2.3 Information Sources

A comprehensive search was conducted across electronic bibliographic databases, supplemented by manual screening of reference lists of included studies and relevant prior systematic reviews (backward citation searching) and by forward citation searching of included studies. Database searches were designed to capture literature up to the search execution date. Gray literature sources and trial/study registries were consulted where applicable to identify unpublished or in-progress data. No contact with study authors for unpublished data was undertaken in this iteration of the review.

## 2.4 Search Strategy

Search strategies were developed by combining controlled vocabulary terms (e.g., MeSH headings) and free-text keywords across four concept blocks: (1) skin cancer terms (e.g., "melanoma," "basal cell carcinoma," "squamous cell carcinoma," "non-melanoma skin cancer," "skin neoplasms"), (2) race/ethnicity terms (e.g., "race," "ethnicity," "racial disparities," "Black," "African American," "Hispanic," "Latino," "Asian," "American Indian," "Alaska Native"), (3) epidemiologic outcome terms (e.g., "incidence," "prevalence," "epidemiology"), and (4) study-design filters where applicable, combined using Boolean operators (AND/OR). The full electronic search strategy, including database-specific syntax and an example query as executed, is provided in Appendix A (Supplementary Search Strategy). The search was executed by one reviewer and independently verified for reproducibility by a second reviewer prior to de-duplication.

## 2.5 Study Selection Process

Records identified through all information sources were imported into reference management software and de-duplicated. Title and abstract screening (phase 1) and full-text eligibility assessment (phase 2) were each performed independently by two reviewers against the pre-specified eligibility criteria; disagreements were resolved by discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer. Reasons for full-text exclusion were recorded. Of 335 records identified, 254 were excluded at title/abstract screening, yielding 81 reports sought for retrieval; 3 reports could not be retrieved, leaving 78 reports assessed at full-text level. Of these, 71 were excluded, and 3 studies met all eligibility criteria and were included in the qualitative and quantitative synthesis. The complete study flow, including exclusion reasons, is presented in the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (Figure 1).

## 2.6 Data Collection Process

Data were extracted independently and in duplicate by two reviewers using a piloted, standardized data extraction form. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or third-reviewer adjudication. When required data were incompletely reported, study authors were not contacted for this analysis; such studies were retained for qualitative synthesis where possible, with the missing data documented and its impact addressed in the Discussion and Limitations.

## 2.7 Data Items

For each included study, the following data were extracted: study identification (first author, publication year, country/setting), study design, data source (e.g., cancer registry), study period, sample size and population characteristics, racial/ethnic group definitions and classification method, outcome definitions (melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, non-melanoma skin cancer), and outcome data disaggregated by racial/ethnic group, including event counts and group denominators (ai/n1 for the index group and ci/n2 for the comparator group) where reported, or pre-calculated incidence/prevalence rates and measures of association (e.g., odds ratio, rate ratio) with corresponding variance estimates or 95% confidence intervals. Funding source and conflict-of-interest statements were also recorded.

## 2.8 Risk of Bias in Individual Studies

Risk of bias in individual studies was assessed independently by two reviewers using a domain-based risk-of-bias tool appropriate to observational study designs, with disagreements resolved by consensus or third-reviewer adjudication. Each study was rated at the domain level and assigned an overall risk-of-bias judgment (low, moderate, high, or critical/serious risk of bias). Across the domains assessed for the included studies, judgments were distributed as follows: low risk in 5 domain assessments, moderate risk in 14, high risk in 12, and critical risk in 31, indicating substantial methodological concern across the included evidence base. Risk-of-bias judgments were incorporated into the assessment of certainty of evidence (Section 2.12) and are summarized graphically (risk-of-bias summary and traffic-light plots) in the Results.

## 2.9 Summary Measures

The primary effect measure for comparisons of racial/ethnic minority groups versus the Non-Hispanic White reference group was the odds ratio (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI), calculated from 2×2 event data (ai/n1 versus ci/n2) where available. Where studies reported incidence or prevalence rates without extractable 2×2 counts, rate ratios or standardized incidence ratios with 95% CIs were extracted as reported. All effect estimates were oriented such that OR/rate ratio <1.0 indicated lower incidence/prevalence in the minority group relative to the Non-Hispanic White comparator.

## 2.10 Synthesis Methods

A random-effects meta-analysis (DerSimonian-Laird or restricted maximum likelihood [REML] estimation, as data permitted) was planned to pool effect estimates across studies reporting comparable outcomes and racial/ethnic comparisons, with results displayed as forest plots. Between-study heterogeneity was to be quantified using the I² statistic and tau² (between-study variance), with I² thresholds of approximately 25%, 50%, and 75% interpreted as low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively, consistent with Cochrane guidance. Subgroup analyses were planned a priori by skin cancer subtype (melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, non-melanoma skin cancer), racial/ethnic group, and study design (cohort versus cross-sectional/registry-based) to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, and sensitivity analyses excluding studies at critical risk of bias were planned. Given the small number of eligible studies anticipated at the outcome level, it was specified a priori that heterogeneity statistics and subgroup comparisons would be interpreted with substantial caution regardless of numeric result, owing to their limited statistical power at low k. Quantitative pooling was contingent on the availability of extractable event-level or effect-size data with variance estimates for at least two studies per outcome comparison; where such data could not be extracted, findings were synthesized narratively (Synthesis Without Meta-analysis [SWiM] approach²) rather than pooled.

## 2.11 Publication Bias Assessment

Assessment of small-study effects and publication bias was planned using visual inspection of funnel plots, together with Egger's regression test and the trim-and-fill method, for any outcome comparison with a sufficient number of contributing studies (conventionally ≥10) to provide adequate statistical power. It was specified a priori that, for outcome comparisons with fewer than 10 studies, formal publication bias testing would not be performed or would be interpreted with extreme caution and explicitly flagged as underpowered, consistent with Cochrane Handbook guidance on the unreliability of funnel-plot-based methods at low study counts.

## 2.12 Certainty of Evidence (GRADE)

The certainty of the body of evidence for each outcome comparison was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach,³ across the domains of risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias, with certainty rated as high, moderate, low, or very low. Certainty ratings began at "high" for observational studies assessed under a structured framework and were downgraded according to pre-specified GRADE criteria within each domain. Disagreements in domain-level or overall certainty ratings were resolved by discussion or third-reviewer adjudication. Summary of Findings tables were constructed to present pooled or narratively synthesized effect estimates alongside the corresponding certainty rating and an explanatory rationale for any downgrading decision.

## Discussion

### Summary of main findings

This systematic review identified three eligible studies examining racial and ethnic disparities in skin cancer incidence and prevalence, drawn from an initial pool of 335 records screened under PRISMA 2020 methodology. Consistent with the prior literature this review sought to synthesize, the included studies (Navsaria 2024, Lim 2023, Wu 2011) collectively suggest that skin cancer incidence patterns differ substantially by race and ethnicity, with Non-Hispanic White populations bearing a disproportionate burden of cutaneous melanoma while non-melanoma skin cancers and disparities in disease stage at diagnosis affect Black, Hispanic, Asian/Pacific Islander, and American Indian/Alaska Native populations differently. However, a critical limitation must be stated plainly: quantitative pooling of these findings was not possible because study-level event counts (ai, n1, ci, n2) were not available for any of the three included studies. Consequently, no summary effect estimate, forest plot, or formal heterogeneity statistic (I², τ²) can be reported. The findings presented here therefore represent a narrative synthesis of study-level findings rather than a meta-analytic pooled estimate, and should be interpreted accordingly.

### Comparison with previous literature and guidelines

The direction of the disparities identified in this review is broadly consistent with prior descriptive epidemiologic reports and with guidance from organizations such as the American Academy of Dermatology and the American Cancer Society, which have long noted that melanoma incidence is highest among non-Hispanic White individuals while racial and ethnic minority patients more often present with advanced-stage disease and experience worse melanoma-specific survival. This review did not identify a sufficient body of comparable quantitative literature to permit formal comparison of pooled effect sizes with earlier reviews, in part because, to our knowledge, no prior meta-analysis has attempted to pool incidence rate ratios or prevalence odds ratios across these specific racial/ethnic strata using comparable case definitions. This gap itself is a notable finding: despite decades of descriptive surveillance data (e.g., SEER-based reports), the evidence base amenable to formal quantitative synthesis remains sparse, underscoring a persistent methodological fragmentation in how race and ethnicity are operationalized and reported across skin cancer epidemiology studies.

### Biological and clinical plausibility

A biologically plausible explanation for the observed disparities in cutaneous melanoma and keratinocyte carcinoma incidence relates to differential cutaneous melanin content and its established photoprotective effect against ultraviolet radiation-induced DNA damage, which is consistent with lower melanoma incidence in populations with darker constitutive pigmentation. However, biological plausibility alone does not account for the disparities in stage at diagnosis, delayed care-seeking, and disease-specific mortality that have been reported in some minority populations; these are more plausibly explained by non-biological mechanisms, including reduced clinician index of suspicion for skin cancer in patients with darker skin, underrepresentation of diverse skin tones in dermatologic education and diagnostic atlases, and differential access to dermatologic care. The included studies in this review do not permit disentangling biological from healthcare-access mechanisms, and this distinction should be a priority for future primary research.

### Limitations

Several limitations affect the interpretation of this review. First, regarding search limitations, the search strategy retrieved 335 records, of which only 3 met full eligibility criteria after screening (81 reports sought for retrieval, 3 not retrieved, 78 assessed in full text), suggesting either a narrow set of studies directly addressing this PICO question or search terms that may not have fully captured the relevant literature; grey literature and non-English-language studies may have been missed. Second, risk of bias across the assessed body of evidence was substantial: of the domains rated, only 5 were judged low risk, compared with 14 moderate, 12 high, and 31 critical risk of bias ratings, indicating that methodological limitations in the included and excluded studies materially constrain confidence in the underlying evidence base. Third, heterogeneity could not be formally quantified because pooling was not performed; however, clinical and methodological heterogeneity is expected across studies that differ in cancer subtype definitions (melanoma vs. non-melanoma vs. all skin cancers), study design (cohort vs. cross-sectional), race/ethnicity classification systems, and case ascertainment methods, and this should be presumed substantial rather than assumed absent. Fourth, publication bias could not be assessed using funnel plot asymmetry or Egger's test, as such methods require a minimum of approximately 10 studies to yield reliable results, and with only k=3 studies, publication bias must be considered suspected but unquantifiable. Fifth, applicability is limited by the small number of included studies and their specific populations and settings, which may not generalize to all racial/ethnic minority populations, healthcare systems, or geographic regions; findings should not be extrapolated beyond the contexts directly studied. Accordingly, the overall certainty of evidence was rated Very Low using the GRADE framework, reflecting serious concerns across risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, and imprecision, compounded by the inability to generate a pooled estimate at all.

### Implications for clinical practice and future research

Given the Very Low certainty of the current evidence base, clinicians should exercise caution in drawing firm quantitative conclusions about the magnitude of racial and ethnic disparities in skin cancer incidence from this review alone, though the qualitative direction of disparities is consistent with a broader, non-pooled literature and supports continued clinical vigilance for skin cancer across all skin tones, not only in populations traditionally considered high-risk. For research, this review highlights an urgent need for primary studies that report extractable, stratified event-level data (case counts and population denominators by race/ethnicity) using standardized race/ethnicity classifications, to enable future meta-analytic pooling. Prospective, multi-registry studies — potentially leveraging SEER, NPCR, or international cancer registry consortia — are needed to generate the k≥10 studies required for adequately powered heterogeneity and publication bias assessment. Registration of this protocol (PROSPERO CRD42025XXXXXX) and transparent reporting of this review's inability to pool data are offered as a call to the field to standardize outcome reporting in future disparities research.
